New personalised care to keep 1.8 million people out of hospital
New neighbourhood support teams will give dedicated and personalised care to the most vulnerable patients to help keep them out of hospital, the Health and Social Care Secretary announced at the King’s Fund on 18 November 2026
The move is expected to cut emergency hospital bed days by around 4 million, reducing avoidable hospital admissions and easing pressure on NHS services
It will also pave the way for the new national care service by integrating NHS and care support in the community
Nearly 2 million of the country’s most frail and vulnerable patients will be supported by dedicated new neighbourhood support teams, delivering a personalised care management service to each individual, the Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper has announced today.
The NHS will be required to proactively identify the most vulnerable people in their communities, including frail older people, people living with dementia, those in care homes and people approaching the end of life, so that the support teams can work together to help each individual avoid the need to go to hospital.
The new plans are a major change to the way the most frail or vulnerable patients are supported in the community and mean that they and their family will have a dedicated single point of contact, regular check-ins from teams who know the patient to make sure they are getting the right support, and a care plan built around their specific needs, helping them avoid unnecessary or stressful hospital trips by intervening earlier, and providing them with more appropriate care and support.
At least 1% of the population in England - more than half a million people - will receive dedicated support under these plans by April 2027, expanding over subsequent years to reach more of those at greatest risk.
With around 3 to 5% of the population currently accounting for approximately 70% of emergency bed days, it is estimated that the earlier support being provided to these individuals could cut around 4.3 million bed days, generating net savings of more than £1 billion to the NHS each year. It will also substantially improve the lives of frail elderly people who are often admitted to hospital inappropriately in a way that can impact their long-term recovery.
Announcing the plans at the King’s Fund annual conference on 18 November, Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper described it as a down payment on a national care service: a practical step towards bringing health and social care together around people’s needs:
Today we are setting a national ambition that, by the end of the decade, people with the greatest health and care needs will no longer have to fight their way through the system to get the support they need.
Starting now we will bring together health and social care in the community, looking after those who need most support in a radically different way.
This is a fundamental change in the relationship between vulnerable people and the health and care system: from having to navigate fragmented services themselves to having a team that knows them, looks out for them and takes responsibility for their care.
Today, too many people with complex health and care needs are left to navigate a fragmented system alone. Families often have to repeat their stories to different services, while support can come too late to prevent a crisis, which leads to avoidable emergency admissions.
Providing a dedicated care management service to those individuals will change that, delivered by multidisciplinary teams of GPs, community health services, mental health services, social care and voluntary organisations in every neighbourhood in England, connecting patients not just with hospital care, but with wider support to help them stay well, manage their conditions and live independently.
Each person enrolled will have:
a dedicated neighbourhood support team, working with their existing GP, which knows them, understands their history and takes responsibility for co-ordinating their care
one tailored care plan, covering their medicines, ongoing treatment, therapy and support at home
regular reviews and, where appropriate, remote monitoring to identify problems early and arrange additional support
in time, a single number which they, their family or any professional supporting them can call at any hour
a co-ordinated response if a crisis occurs, including support to return home promptly and safely after hospital treatment
The new neighbourhood support teams will be at the heart of the government’s upcoming strategy for urgent, emergency and planned care, bringing together community-based professionals who know the person, understand their history and work with their existing GP.
The strategy recognises that urgent and planned services are closely connected, with delays in one part of the system creating pressure elsewhere. It aims to help people stay well, get support earlier and receive care closer to home where that is right for them, and will form part of the wider response to corridor care, alongside improvements to hospital care and discharge.
Under the new plans, integrated care boards (ICBs) will continue to be accountable for commissioning effective community support, aligned with local social care arrangements.
Dr Emma Rowland, NHS Interim Executive Director for Performance and Delivery, said:
We want to be there for the most vulnerable patients and their families around the clock, offering them the support they need to avoid unnecessary and stressful trips to A&E and stays in hospital.
By working with GPs who know their patients best, we can put in place a network of support and expertise for some of our most vulnerable patients and their families, helping them stay well, get the care they need closer to home and also free up vital NHS beds.
First steps already under way
The neighbourhood support teams will be funded by using existing NHS budgets differently - investing in earlier support close to home, rather than waiting until people need hospital care. Local NHS leaders will prioritise existing funding to establish the service, and as it expands, savings from fewer avoidable hospital admissions and shorter stays, alongside savings from wider reforms, will be reinvested in community care.
Primary care networks have already begun identifying people at greatest risk and putting up-to-date care plans in place. It was announced last week that up to 300,000 people will receive more proactive support from their GP this winter thanks to a new £40 million government-funded programme.
This is part of wider action to prepare the NHS for the colder months, alongside:
additional funding for urgent care
expanded Pharmacy First Services
the winter vaccination programme, which is giving millions of people vital protection against seasonal illnesses
It helps pave the way for the new national care service that the Prime Minister has pledged to introduce, following Baroness Louise Casey’s commission into reform of adult social care.
Dr Deb Gompertz, Honorary Secretary and Vice President for Policy, British Geriatrics Society, said:
The British Geriatrics Society warmly welcomes the new initiative of a neighbourhood support team for older people living with frailty, dementia and other long-term conditions to live well in the community. Reducing avoidable hospital admissions and long hospital stays by providing timely high-quality support closer to home is an excellent step in the right direction.
We look forward to hearing more about how this will be implemented across England by empowering and upskilling teams to deliver this community care offer.
Ruth Rankine, Director of Primary Care, Community and Neighbourhood Health at the NHS Alliance, said:
Neighbourhood support teams are an important step towards delivering more personalised care closer to home and this is to be welcomed. This is integral to the move towards a neighbourhood health service.
We now need to turn that ambition into a sustainable model, which builds on what is already working at a local level. With an ageing population, the success of this service will depend on empowering local NHS teams to identify those most in need including frail elderly people and those approaching end of life.
Sarah Sweeney, National Voices, Director of Evidence and Improvement, said:
We welcome today’s announcement. When we speak to patients, they consistently tell us how important it is for care to be delivered in the right setting and that it supports people to maintain their independence.
As Neighbourhood Support Teams begin to roll out, we look forward to hearing more about implementation and see this initiative as an opportunity to ensure care in the community is responsive to people’s holistic needs and shaped by the people who use and need it most.
This is also an opportunity to provide greater continuity of care and personalised support which can help rebuild trust between people and service providers.
Helen Walker, Chief Executive of Carers UK, said:
Joined-up care, supported by a single point of contact, is something families have long been calling for. It can make a significant difference to unpaid carers, reducing stress and pressure and improving outcomes for them and the person they care for.
When care is poorly coordinated this can affect carers’ health, wellbeing and ability to remain in employment. This is particularly challenging for those who do not live with the person they care for and need reassurance that care is being delivered effectively.
An integrated approach also helps health and care teams identify unpaid carers, recognise their role and connect them with the support they need.

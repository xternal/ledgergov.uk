General Safety Requirement for Construction Products: government response
Executive summary
In February 2026, the government published the General Safety Requirement for Construction Products consultation (“the consultation”) and the Construction Products Reform White Paper consultation alongside the Grenfell Tower Inquiry Annual Report.
Following consultation and engagement with the sector on the general safety requirement and white paper, this response summarises what we heard and confirms proposals for reform in light of consultation responses.
The response to the white paper has been published on GOV.UK.
Reforms are supported by the sector
Across the consultation, respondents generally showed strong support for the proposals, with most receiving over 60% agreement. Most respondents supported the introduction of the general safety requirement and recognised the need to strengthen product safety across the construction products sector. Many considered that reform was necessary to address existing gaps in the regulatory framework, improve accountability and support safer products throughout the supply chain.
While there was support for the overall direction of travel, some respondents emphasised the importance of proportionate regulation, improved traceability, clear guidance and effective implementation.
Summary of response conclusions
A number of recurring themes emerged across the consultation and were raised by a wide range of stakeholder groups.
Proportionality and risk-based regulation
The general safety requirement (GSR) will take a proportionate, risk-based approach that supports safety without placing unnecessary burdens on business or holding back growth and innovation. Manufacturers will be responsible for ensuring that products are safe when placed on the market. They must assess the safety risks arising from a product’s intended and normal and reasonably foreseeable conditions of use and apply mitigations proportionate to the severity of the risks identified. They must also provide accurate, reliable and up-to-date product information, so that those specifying, purchasing or using construction products can make safe and informed choices. Responsibility then sits with downstream users to select and use products in line with their safe use, taking account of information on intended use, limitations, installation, maintenance and any competence required. Manufacturers should not market products outside their safe use, and downstream users should not select or use them outside that safe use.
Scope of the general safety requirement and interaction with existing regimes
Where products are already regulated under construction product regulations, they will not also be regulated by the general safety requirement. The regulator will have powers to remove unsafe products from the market under both the construction products regulations regime and the general safety requirement. The government will implement complementary construction products regulations and general safety requirement regimes to ensure product safety, regulatory clarity and consistency with the European Union system.
Traceability, transparency and product information
The general safety requirement will require accurate and accessible product information so that those specifying, purchasing or using construction products can make safe and informed choices. Digitalisation will play an important role in ensuring that the right people can access the right information at the right time. Our approach will support the integration of product information through digital data carriers, alongside record-keeping requirements that ensure relevant information is retained, strengthening transparency and accountability.
A systems-based approach to product safety
Safe outcomes depend not only on products being safe when placed on the market, but on how they are specified, selected, substituted, installed, inspected and evidenced in practice. This work will sit alongside wider building safety and construction products reforms, including the Regulation 7(1) and Approved Document 7 review, work on building professions and industry-led competence initiatives. The Regulation 7(1) and Approved Document 7 review will consider these wider responsibilities, helping to strengthen the overall framework without creating duplication or unnecessary complexity.
Clear responsibilities and accountability across the supply chain
We also recognise the strong message from respondents that responsibility should sit with the party able to control the relevant decision, information or activity. We will ensure that responsibilities follow the functions and decisions that different actors are best placed to manage, so that accountability is clear, fair and effective. All economic operators play an important role in ensuring that construction products are safe. The general safety requirement will set clear, specific duties for relevant actors throughout the supply chain, so that each understands their responsibilities. This will strengthen accountability and help ensure that unsafe products can be identified and addressed.
Clear guidance and regulatory certainty
We recognise the importance of providing the sector with clear and timely information on the new requirements and how they will work in practice. We will support the national regulator for construction products, whose functions are currently carried out by the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), in developing practical guidance to support implementation of the general safety requirement. This will include guidance to help manufacturers carry out proportionate risk assessments. This will support a consistent understanding of the regime and help businesses prepare for and comply with their duties.
Effective enforcement supported by sufficient capability and resources
Effective enforcement will be central to the success of the general safety requirement. Enforcement authorities will have proportionate powers to investigate non-compliance, require corrective action and penalise poor behaviour. The national regulator for construction products will lead enforcement, supported by local regulators with complementary powers. We will continue working with regulators to ensure that they have the capacity, capability and specialist expertise needed to implement and enforce the new regime effectively and consistently.
Competence and culture change
We recognise that clearer accountability must be supported by the competence needed to discharge those responsibilities. The Construction Product White Paper defined competence as the skills, knowledge, experience and behaviours of the people and organisations responsible for undertaking work across the construction chain. It also set out our intention to ensure that all actors within the construction supply chain understand their roles and responsibilities, and have the skills required to fulfil them capably. Clear expectations on competence are therefore an important part of ensuring that accountability works in practice.
This work will form part of the government’s wider work to strengthen competence, skills, behaviours and accountability across the built environment professions, trades and occupations, including through the forthcoming Building Professions Strategy.
Effective implementation and continued collaboration
Effective implementation will be critical to the success of these reforms. We can confirm that businesses will have at least a 12-month implementation period to prepare for the new requirements. We will continue to work with industry and other stakeholders as the GSR is developed and implemented, including to identify where practical support can help businesses adapt, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises.
Introduction
Overview and context
The programme of Construction Products Reforms is designed to address the systemic failures laid bare by the tragedy at Grenfell Tower on 14 June 2017, responding to recommendations from the subsequent Grenfell Tower Inquiry. The Inquiry found that the mechanisms designed to safeguard the built environment including the regulation and oversight of construction products, failed to protect people in their homes.
These failures were further detailed by the 2 independent reviews:
the Building a Safer Future: Independent Review of Building Regulations and Fire Safety
Independent Review of the Construction Product Testing Regime
Construction product regulations currently only apply to products covered by designated standards or technical assessments. This leaves a central estimate of 63% of all construction products outside the scope of the regulatory regime.[footnote 1]
To address this, the government proposed introducing a general safety requirement (GSR) that will apply to these construction products.
The proposed GSR regulations will require that construction products placed on the UK market are safe and establish specific obligations for businesses throughout the supply chain. The regulations will also provide enforcement powers to the national regulator for construction products.
We sought views on our proposed approach, including:
the scope of the GSR and its interaction with the wider construction products regulatory framework
the proposed requirements on economic operators within the supply chain of construction products
enforcement action that should be taken where a product is found to be non-compliant with the GSR

Methodology
All responses to this consultation have been considered as part of the analysis.
For the purposes of this analytical summary, the team used a Government Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted tool, ‘Consult’, developed by the Incubator for Artificial Intelligence (i.AI), to support the identification and assignment of themes.
Consult identifies key themes from the responses to each question and maps each response against those themes, with policy officials reviewing the outputs at every stage, making adjustments where appropriate. Themes generated by Consult were reviewed and refined by policy officials. Using their understanding of the policy area, subject matter expertise and the consultation objectives, officials amended the themes to ensure they accurately reflected the content of responses and addressed the consultation questions. The final framework was then used to categorise responses across the dataset.
Personal data collected as part of the consultation was processed in accordance with the privacy notice published in the consultation. Care was taken to avoid using AI to analyse personal data.

You said:
Most respondents agreed that previously used products should be regulated in the same way as new products. Many considered that applying equivalent safety requirements would help maintain product safety, improve consistency across the market and reduce the risk of unsafe products re-entering the supply chain.
Many respondents supported this approach provided it included proportionate exemptions. Heritage, conservation and reclaimed products were frequently cited as areas where flexibility may be required to avoid undermining repair, reuse and preservation activities. Respondents also frequently called for clear definitions, guidance and clarification of legal responsibilities throughout the supply chain.
The majority of respondents who disagreed with the proposal argued that applying the same requirements as new products could create disproportionate compliance costs and practical challenges for the reuse sector. These respondents generally favoured a more risk-based approach, supported by clear standards, traceability arrangements and guidance which would enable the safe reuse of products while supporting circular economy objectives.
Government response
We intend to regulate previously used construction products in the same way as new products. This will ensure that previously used products placed on the market are subject to appropriate safety requirements and regulatory oversight. We recognise concerns that applying these requirements to previously used products could create practical challenges and additional costs for the reuse sector. However, we consider that all construction products placed on the market should be safe, including those that have previously been used.
At the same time, we recognise that the reuse sector functions differently from the new product market, which is why we are moving forward with our proposed exemption on products marketed as needing repair or reconditioning. We also recognise that requirements should not create unintended barriers to placing otherwise safe reused products on the market. As we develop the detail of these requirements, we continue to work across government and industry to consider whether further specific exemptions would be appropriate to deliver a safe and proportionate system for reused products. This approach strikes a proportionate balance between improving construction product safety and supporting the circular economy through the repair, reuse and life extension of products. It will support the growth of second-hand and refurbishment markets while reducing the environmental impacts associated with manufacturing new products. We also recognise the importance of clear guidance to support businesses in understanding and meeting the requirements, including responsibilities across the supply chain.

A significant number of respondents agreed with the proposal for an overarching safety requirement on economic operators in scope of the GSR. Respondents generally considered that it would strengthen accountability across the supply chain, help ensure that only safe construction products are placed on the market and establish a clear baseline for product safety. Many respondents also considered that it would help address existing regulatory gaps.
Many respondents who agreed with the proposal emphasised that responsibilities should be proportionate and reflect the role and level of control of different economic operators. Respondents frequently called for clear guidance and definitions, particularly for importers, distributors and SMEs. Some also highlighted the importance of aligning the GSR with existing regulations and standards.
The majority of respondents who disagreed with the proposal generally argued that the requirement should apply to all construction products, including products regulated under the construction products regulations (CPR). Some respondents also raised concerns about the practical application of the proposals, particularly in relation to construction systems, component parts and bespoke products, and highlighted the need for effective enforcement.
Government response
Safe products, safely used is a key objective of our reforms. We will therefore proceed with plans for an overarching safety duty on economic operators in scope of the GSR. We have also considered calls for the GSR to apply to all construction products carefully and will proceed with our proposal that the construction products regulations (CPR) and the GSR operate as complementary regimes. Where products are already regulated under construction product regulations, they will not also be regulated by the GSR. We will instead strengthen GB CPR.
The construction sector has consistently called for alignment with the EU to support trade. Remaining aligned with the EU-CPR 2024 supports ongoing trade with our biggest trading partner. Respondents noted that divergence from the EU could cause product shortages, create a trade barrier for products moving from Great Britain to Northern Ireland and add to the complexity faced by those navigating different regulatory requirements.
There can be no compromise on safety. Under both the GSR and CPR we will ensure that the regulator can remove any unsafe product from the market. This power can be used even where the product is compliant with the CPR. Therefore, irrespective of whether a product is regulated under CPR or the GSR, the regulator will be empowered to remove unsafe products from the market.
We recognise that the CPR does not cover all products and are committed to bringing all products within scope of the regulatory regime. Introducing the GSR for currently unregulated products achieves this and will continue to do this as new products are innovated and brought to market.
We will proceed with the requirement that products placed on the market must be safe and will establish specific obligations on economic operators throughout the supply chain. These requirements will help improve accountability across the supply chain, support safer construction products and address existing gaps in the current regulatory framework. In developing the specific obligations, we will consider the role and level of control of different economic operators to ensure that their responsibilities are proportionate.
We recognise the concerns about practical application of proposals in some instances such as construction product systems, component parts and bespoke products. We are continuing to work with stakeholders to test how the reforms will work in practice for bespoke products. Wider reforms will consider how products are safely selected and used, including where individual products form part of a wider system.
The importance respondents placed on clarity and regulatory certainty is recognised. Greater certainty will be provided through the definition of key concepts and guidance to support implementation and compliance across the sector, including for different types of economic operator.

Most respondents agreed that the proposed approach to assessing risk was reasonable and proportionate. Respondents generally supported risk-based safety assessments and considered that requirements should reflect the level of risk posed by different products. Many also considered that the proposals would provide a structured approach to identifying and managing safety risks.
A strong theme emerging from the responses was an emphasis on the need for clear guidance, templates and practical examples to support consistent implementation. Respondents frequently highlighted the importance of recognising existing standards, certification schemes and quality assurance processes to avoid duplication and unnecessary administrative burdens.
Those respondents who disagreed with the proposal generally raised concerns about potential administrative and cost burdens, particularly for SMEs and manufacturers of bespoke, innovative or lower-risk products. Some respondents also highlighted the need for additional guidance on assessing novel products, system interactions and lifecycle risks, and called for greater clarity around responsibilities across the supply chain.
Government response
The government remains of the view that manufacturers should be required to assess the risks posed by their products. Written risk assessments are an important mechanism for demonstrating that safety has been properly considered and embedded throughout product development and the product lifecycle. In the consultation, our proposals set out detailed, prescriptive requirements that manufacturers would need to meet as part of the risk assessment process. In light of consultation feedback and further engagement with stakeholders, we are assessing whether an outcomefocused approach could best address concerns about potential administrative and cost burdens for SMEs and for bespoke, innovative and low-risk products.
While some manufacturers may deem it necessary or helpful to use voluntary standards and third-party certification to support the placing of safe products on the market, we do not intend for compliance with standards or certifications schemes to provide an automatic route to compliance with the GSR. However, these may provide useful evidence to support manufacturers in making a holistic assessment of product risks.
The need for clarity and practical support raised by respondents is recognised. MHCLG intends to assist the national regulator for construction products, whose functions are currently carried out by the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), in developing guidance to support manufacturers in carrying out risk assessments and understanding what information should be included.
We will take account of the suggestions made by respondents and continue to engage with the sector to introduce a requirement which balances regulatory certainty with flexibility for businesses.

Feedback consistently agreed that the proposed criteria for product information were proportionate and would support informed product selection. Respondents generally considered that product information should clearly set out intended use, limitations, installation requirements, maintenance requirements and any competence needed for safe use. Many also emphasised that performance claims should be supported by robust evidence.
Supporters of the proposal emphasised that information requirements should remain proportionate to product risk and complexity. Respondents frequently called for clear guidance and highlighted the benefits of digitally accessible product information, including digital product passports, QR codes and unique identifiers to improve traceability.
Respondents’ reasons for not supporting the proposed list of product information were mixed. Some respondents who did not agree with the proposal argued that additional information would be required for certain products and applications, such as environmental information, products critical to safe construction or those with heritage applications. These respondents frequently highlighted the need for clearer guidance on reused, non-standard and system-based products. Some raised concerns about potential burdens on smaller businesses, arguing that the proposed criteria may not be proportionate for lower risk products, and the need to align requirements with existing standards and legislation. Other respondents questioned whether manufacturers had sufficient knowledge of how products are used in practice to be able to meet requirements around conditions of use.
Government response
Noting that respondents generally agreed that the proposed list of criteria was proportionate and met the objectives set out in the consultation, we will proceed with our consultation proposals.
Of those asking for additional criteria, several respondents suggested that environmental performance data and information to support the circular economy should be included. For products covered by the GSR, the focus remains on safety. Where information, including information related to environmental performance, is relevant to the safety of the product when installed in a building this will come under the scope of the GSR. Other information will remain voluntary for the GSR and where relevant, government will encourage manufacturers to follow industry best practice, guidance or standards.
Other respondents noted the need for explicit version control and dated information. The consultation set out that information has to be up to date, and in the accompanying guidance we will make clear that this includes ensuring that the relevant version of the product information is made available with the product.
Many asked for further guidance, particularly to ensure that economic operators (particularly SMEs) understand how to meet digital requirements, and to set out how requirements can be met in a proportionate way considering the risk of the product and its uses. We will work with the regulator to develop guidance to support manufacturers and others in meeting their responsibilities.
Some respondents noted that to be most useful and effective digital information needed to be structured and interoperable. We share these objectives and agree. We want data relating to construction products and also the wider building life cycle to be interoperable and to achieve this we recognise the need for a transition period. As such, we intend to maintain more flexible requirements in the GSR before setting broader data standards across the building life cycle in future legislation. We will, however, work with industry and produce guidance to support best practice and encourage the use of structured, interoperable data.

Government response
Having considered the responses received, the government intends to proceed with the proposed labelling requirements.

This includes systems, bulk products, products cut to length, bespoke products and other circumstances where physical labelling may not be straightforward. The labelling requirements will therefore be designed to be flexible and proportionate, supported by guidance to help businesses understand how to meet them in practice.
The consultation also showed support for digital labelling and for ensuring that labelling forms part of the wider product information framework, helping users access accurate and up-to-date product information. The government agrees that labelling should support access to product information. We consider that the current proposals provide a foundation for meeting this objective and we will consider further development as part of future work on data standards for the built environment.

There was significant agreement that manufacturers should retain records for 10 years beyond the last supply of a product. Respondents generally considered that this would support traceability, accountability and the investigation of safety issues, while aligning with existing regulatory requirements and industry practice.
However, there were questions about whether a 10-year period would be sufficient for all products. Safety-critical and long-life products were frequently cited as examples where longer retention periods may be more appropriate, particularly where products remain in use for several decades. Many respondents also supported the use of digital record-keeping and called for clear guidance on the scope of records that should be retained.
The majority of respondents who disagreed with the proposal generally argued that a fixed 10-year retention period was not appropriate for all products. Some favoured longer, risk-based retention periods linked to product lifespan, liability or warranty periods, while others raised concerns about administrative burdens, particularly for SMEs and manufacturers with large product portfolios.
Government response
Having considered the responses received, the government intends to proceed with this proposal.
The importance respondents placed on traceability and accountability across the supply chain is recognised. We have considered calls from some respondents for longer retention periods for safety-critical and long-life products, as well as for risk-based periods linked to factors such as product lifespan. However, we consider that a consistent 10-year period strikes an appropriate balance between supporting traceability and regulatory oversight, while providing greater clarity for businesses and avoiding the additional complexity and burden that could arise from different requirements for different products. This approach also aligns with the EU Construction Products Regulation (EU-CPR 2024), helping to support consistency for industry.
We also recognise concerns from some respondents about the administrative and cost burdens associated with these requirements, particularly for SMEs and manufacturers with large product portfolios. However, retaining appropriate records is important to support traceability, demonstrate compliance and enable effective regulatory action where safety concerns arise.
We recognise the strong support for greater digitalisation across the sector, including for record retention. Digitalisation plays an important role in ensuring that the right people have access to the right information at the right time. However, we also acknowledge differing levels of digital capability and readiness across the supply chain, particularly among smaller businesses, and the need for any future requirements to be proportionate and practical. We will ensure that any future approach reflects these differences and supports organisations of all sizes.
We will continue to engage with the sector and provide guidance to support consistent implementation of these requirements.

The government considers that 28 days provides an appropriate balance between allowing an economic operator sufficient time to prepare an appeal and ensuring that enforcement matters are resolved without unnecessary delay. A 28-day period for lodging an appeal is also consistent with the general time limit for appealing to the First-tier Tribunal.
The First-tier Tribunal will be able to extend the appeal period where appropriate. This will provide flexibility in exceptional or complex cases while maintaining a clear and consistent general time limit.
Next steps
The government intends to lay the general safety requirement (GSR) regulations in 2027, with the new requirements coming into force no earlier than 12 months later. This approach will provide time for preparation and support effective implementation of the new regime. The GSR will close gaps in the current regulatory system by creating a safety duty for construction products not covered by a designated standard, or subject to a technical assessment. It will operate alongside the construction products regulations (CPR), strengthen accountability across the supply chain and help make sure that only safe products are made available on the market.
Sector and regulator readiness
The government recognises that small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) form a significant part of the construction products sector and may face different challenges in adapting to reform. Respondents highlighted that SMEs may have less access to technical and legal resource, and less capacity overall than larger businesses, and may therefore require clearer communications, practical guidance and proportionate lead-in times to understand and meet new requirements.
Regulator capacity and readiness will be central to implementation. Effective delivery depends on regulators having the necessary people, expertise, operational processes and enforcement capability. We will continue working with regulators to assess the capacity and capability required and ensure implementation reflects operational realities.
The government will work with industry and regulators to ensure businesses are aware of proposed changes and their responsibilities, and to identify how best to deliver guidance and communications aimed at reaching SMEs.
Developing guidance
We recognise the important role that guidance can play in supporting the sector to prepare for and transition to the new regime. However, guidance will not be able to foresee every scenario, and individuals will be required to consider their specific situation and context to make informed and defensible decisions. This is in line with the overall vision for a reformed regime in which all actors in the product chain take responsibility for safety. We will work with the regulator, trade associations and industry partners to support guidance that is accessible, practical and available sufficiently ahead of commencement so that businesses can plan with confidence.
Ongoing engagement with industry
During the consultation, the government carried out an extensive programme of engagement that reached representatives across the construction products sector, including manufacturers, trade associations, regulators, and stakeholders from across the wider supply chain. Respondents consistently highlighted the value of ongoing engagement between government and industry and emphasised that continued dialogue will be essential as reforms move from policy development into implementation.
The government remains committed to ongoing engagement with the sector to explain the direction of reform, test practical implications, identify barriers to readiness and support a shared understanding of new responsibilities. This approach will ensure reforms are informed by real-world delivery issues and contribute to a coherent, trusted and effective regulatory system.

Annex B: Email responses
MHCLG received 22 responses by email outside the Citizen Space platform. Ten provided answers against specific consultation questions and were also included in the question-by-question analysis. The remaining 12 provided broader comments that could not be assigned to individual questions and were analysed thematically. This Annex brings together the analysis of all 22 email responses and summarises the main themes raised.
Scope of the general safety requirement and interaction with existing regimes
Respondents frequently commented on the scope of the general safety requirement (GSR) and its interaction with existing regulatory frameworks. A recurring theme across responses was whether the proposed approach would create a single and consistent safety baseline, noting that products subject to a designated standard or a technical assessment which has been issued for it (such products would be affixed with a CE marking or UKCA marking) would remain subject to different regulatory routes.
Some respondents argued that this could create overlapping requirements, regulatory complexity and inconsistent safety outcomes. A few respondents suggested that the GSR should apply more broadly across construction products, with standards and conformity assessment acting as a means of demonstrating compliance rather than exempting products from the overarching safety duty.
Proportionality and practical implementation
Respondents consistently emphasised highlighted the importance of a proportionate and risk-based approach. Respondents generally considered that requirements should reflect product risk and complexity, with stronger controls applying to safety-critical products and proportionate requirements applying to lower-risk products.
A significant number of respondents raised concerns about potential impacts on SMEs, and those supplying reused products, innovative materials and products without established standards. Some respondents also raised concerns about implementation challenges, including regulatory capacity, transition periods, industry readiness and the cumulative cost of compliance across the supply chain. A few respondents raised specific concerns about the practical costs of compliance and the readiness of parts of the sector to implement the proposed requirements.
Supply chain responsibilities and accountability
A common view expressed by respondents was that the importance of clearly defined responsibilities across the supply chain. While most respondents supported duties applying to manufacturers, importers, distributors and other economic operators, many emphasised that obligations should reflect the level of control exercised by each actor.
Some respondents raised concerns about placing unrealistic expectations on distributors, merchants and importers, particularly where they rely on information provided by manufacturers. Some respondents also highlighted the need to consider how products are used in practice and the interaction between products, systems and installation.
Product information, traceability and digitalisation
A strong theme emerging from responses was support for improvements to product information, traceability and digitalisation. Common themes included the use of digital product information, unique product identifiers, structured product data and enhanced traceability throughout the supply chain.
A few respondents highlighted the importance of common data standards, interoperability and shared digital systems to support compliance, improve transparency and enable more effective market surveillance and enforcement.
Enforcement, assurance and regulatory capacity
Many respondents highlighted the importance of effective enforcement and regulatory oversight. Some respondents emphasised the need for strong market surveillance, post-market monitoring, accredited testing and conformity assessment, and sufficient regulatory resource to ensure the regime operates effectively in practice.
Respondents also frequently highlighted the importance of clear guidance, consistency of approach and ongoing engagement with industry to support implementation and compliance.
Overall view
Overall, the email responses demonstrated broad support for the objectives of the GSR and the need to address gaps in the current regulatory framework. However, respondents consistently emphasised the importance of a clear scope, coherent regulatory framework, proportionate requirements and effective implementation to ensure the regime delivers improved safety outcomes in practice.

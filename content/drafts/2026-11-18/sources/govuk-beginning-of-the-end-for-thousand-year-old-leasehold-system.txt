Beginning of the end for thousand year old leasehold system
Landmark laws to end the 1,000-year-old leasehold system and put millions of homeowners in control are entering Parliament this week.
The reforms are the biggest shake-up to homeownership in a century, giving leaseholders significantly more power over their homes and financial protections from third-party landlords.
There are an estimated five million leasehold homes across England and Wales. Yet many are facing rising ground rents, expensive fees for basic requests to live how they want in their own homes, costly lease extensions and the threat of losing their home over small debts.
The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill will change this for good. It will cap ground rents at £250 a year, stop rip-off admin fees, unlock cheaper and easier lease extensions and freehold purchases and end the threat of leaseholders losing their home over small unpaid charges.
While making leasehold a fairer system, leaseholders will get an easier and cheaper way to take control of their building with their neighbours – removing third-party landlords so they can make decisions about how their homes are looked after.
The Bill will bring a modernised type of ownership called commonhold into normal use. Existing leaseholders will be able to switch more easily and it will become the default for most new flats and homes built in the future.
This will give more people the freedom to truly own their property forever, with no ground rent and no third-party landlord, allowing more choice and control over their homes.
The Bill will also protect up to 1.75 million freehold homes on so-called ‘fleecehold’ estates, removing unfair powers that allow management companies to take over families’ homes because of unpaid fees.
Minister for Housing, Matthew Pennycook MP, said:
The leasehold system denies individuals control over the homes they live in, and have paid for, and routinely subjects them to excessive extraction. It is blighting lives, and it is an anachronism in the twenty-first century.
The publication of our historic Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill marks the beginning of the end for the feudal leasehold system. We’re laying the foundations for a commonhold future and transforming the experience of homeownership for existing leaseholders by giving them greater rights and protections and providing them with easy and effective escape routes.
The reforms will protect homeowners by:
Capping ground rents at £250 a year in 2028: cuts bills for up to one million leaseholders and reduces ground rents to zero after 40 years.
Ending unfair threats to take away people’s homes: removes outdated powers that can put a leaseholder’s home at risk over relatively small unpaid bills, such as a disputed service charge and replaces them with a fairer, court-led system.
Capping rip-off admin fees: gives ministers powers to cap excessive permission and administration fees, like asking permission to keep a pet or replace a front door.
Banning leasehold for new flats and homes: makes modernised commonhold the standard for most new flats (with narrow exceptions like shared ownership), meaning buyers own their home outright forever and run their building jointly from day one.
Easier route to switch to commonhold: drops the threshold for existing blocks to convert from 100% neighbour agreement down to 50% support, allowing non-consenting neighbours to remain on modified leases until they sell, so holdouts cannot block conversion for the rest of the building.
Ending ‘fleecehold’ estate enforcement threats: protects people living on freehold housing estates from threats to take over their home over unpaid estate maintenance charges.
Unlocking cheaper, easier lease extensions and freehold purchases: fixes parts of the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. This will help homeowners buy 990-year lease extensions, stop them having to pay their landlord’s expensive costs in most cases and will scrap marriage value, an additional cost faced by leaseholders with 80 years or less remaining on their leases.
Strengthening leaseholders’ right to request a gigabit broadband connection: helps more leaseholders access fast and reliable broadband, which landlords can currently ignore. In future, landlords will have to respond within set timeframes.
Professor Nick Hopkins, Chair of the Leasehold Advisory Service, said:
The introduction of this Bill is a significant step towards giving homeowners stronger protections against some of the most damaging features of leasehold.
Moving towards commonhold, with an easier route for existing leaseholders to convert, is the most significant change to home ownership for a generation. It will give owners of flats ownership and control of the building their flat is in, with a legal framework that is fit for purpose for flat ownership.
We are developing clear, practical guidance on the new commonhold model, helping people understand how it will work and make informed choices.
Additional Information
The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill applies to England and Wales. Property law is devolved in Scotland and Northern Ireland. The reforms are subject to passing through Parliament and achieving Royal Assent.
Of the estimated 5.1 million leasehold homes across England and Wales, approximately 4.9 million are in England (2.72 million owner-occupied, 1.91 million privately rented, and 269,000 social rented) and 235,000 are in Wales. Statistics can be found here for England and here for Wales.
The Bill builds on action this government has already taken to implement protections in the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024, since it began taking effect from July 2024:
24 July 2024 and 31 October 2024: Stronger building safety protections came into force, including new protections around legal costs and further safeguards for leaseholders affected by building safety issues.
31 January 2025: Two-year wait scrapped: homeowners no longer have to wait two years after buying a leasehold property before they can extend their lease or buy their freehold.
3 March 2025: Easier and cheaper Right to Manage came into force: reforms made it easier for more leaseholders to take control of how their building is managed.
Next to come, as recently announced here.
1 April 2027:
Stronger service charge rights and protections: leaseholders will have greater access to information about what they are paying for and how their building is managed, maintained, repaired and insured.
New, fairer rules on recovery of landlord litigation costs, meaning that leaseholders will no longer automatically be on the hook to pay these costs. Leaseholders will also be able to recover their litigation costs from landlords.
1 November 2027:
Clearer service charge bills: private landlords will have to provide standardised bills, annual reports and accounts showing what leaseholders are paying for.
Clearer administration charges: leaseholders will get better information about administration charges and how they are calculated.
More transparent building insurance: there will be clearer standardised information about insurance costs and new rules replacing percentage-based commissions with transparent payments for legitimate work.
1 November 2028:
Clearer service charge bills for social housing leaseholders: standardised bills, annual reports and accounts will also apply to social landlords.
Following passage of the Commonhold and Leasehold Bill: Cheaper and easier lease extensions and freehold purchases: including 990-year lease extensions and scrapping marriage value. The new Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill makes the legislative fixes needed before these reforms can be brought into force.
Separate legislative plans will also put in place an independent regulator of property agents, giving leaseholders, tenants and homebuyers a route towards better protections, as announced here.

Designers to get stronger and simpler protection in major reforms
The government has today (4 November 2026) announced a major overhaul of the UK’s designs system. Reforms will make design protection and enforcement simpler for the country’s 80,000 design businesses.
The UK’s design sector contributed close to £137 billion to the economy in 2023 and supports more than 2 million jobs. But the current designs system has long been criticised as overly complex, placing a disproportionate burden on lone designers and microbusinesses. These have been estimated to make up 92% (PDF, 15.8 MB) of design businesses.
Following a consultation led by the Intellectual Property Office (IPO), which closed in November 2025, the government is today (4 November 2026) setting out a package of targeted reforms.
These include measures to:
stop competitors copying designs and blocking designers from selling their own products
give smaller design businesses a simpler, more affordable route to court when they believe their designs have been copied
reduce the administrative burden of registering designs, with more consistent processes and simpler registered and unregistered protections
clarify and modernise protection for designs involving animation, with new filing options for moving image files
Together, these measures will create a design system that is clearer, easier to use and better suited to modern design by being:
simpler to navigate
more resistant to misuse
more accessible - particularly for smaller firms and lone designers - when disputes arise
The reforms announced today (4 November 2026) are the government’s formal response to the consultation that closed in November 2025. They follow extensive engagement with designers, industry representative organisations and other stakeholders.
They will help ensure the UK’s design system remains fit for purpose in a fast-changing digital economy, laying the foundations for the decades ahead.
Changes will help smaller businesses safeguard their work and modernise design protection. They include the following.
Tackling anti-competitive design filings
The government intends to introduce legislation to give the IPO new powers to consider whether a design is genuinely new before it is registered.
Some people exploit the design registration system to gain an unfair advantage over competitors. This can include:
registering designs created by someone else
copying a competitor’s product images or marketing materials
filing designs for generic or well-known products they had no hand in creating
These registrations are then used to block competitors by sending takedown notices to online marketplaces, dragging other businesses into costly legal disputes. Affected businesses reported spending between £10,000 and more than £50,000 to resolve these cases. The new powers will help protect designers from a serious threat that many — especially independent designers and small studios — cannot afford to challenge.
The IPO will also be able to consider whether a design application has ‘individual character’ — in other words, whether it creates a clearly different overall impression from anything already made public. This will help ensure protection is not granted to designs that are only trivially different from existing products.
The government will also consider bringing forward legislation to give the IPO clear powers to refuse bad-faith design applications. Before doing so, it will carry out further work to define bad faith in practice and identify the evidence needed to prove it. This will help ensure any specific new provision is clear, workable and proportionate.
Improving access to justice for small businesses
At present, registered design cases must go through the Patents Court or Intellectual Property Enterprise Court (IPEC) multi-track. This can put legal action beyond the reach of those who need it most. The government will take steps to allow registered design cases to be heard in the IPEC Small Claims Track for the first time.
The Small Claims Track is a simpler, lower-cost route through the courts for straightforward, lower-value disputes, with compensation claims capped at £10,000. This change would make access to justice easier and considerably more affordable for individual designers and small businesses.
In the coming months, the government will work with designers, legal professionals and business representative groups to consider how this change should be taken forward.
Giving designers more time to prepare for product launch
The government will introduce a new 30-month deferment period, bringing the UK into line with the EU and the international Hague systems. Designers will be able to apply for protection early and delay making their design public for up to 30 months.
This gives businesses more time to develop and launch products without alerting competitors too soon.
Modernising protection for digital and animated designs
The government will update the law to make clear that a design which includes animation, movement or transitions, can qualify for registered design protection. This will provide greater legal clarity for digital designers and businesses creating moving or interactive designs.
The IPO will also introduce new file format options so animated designs can be filed as moving image files as part of the registration process. This better reflects how digital design work is created and presented.
This will give greater certainty and a simpler application process to designers working in:
digital product design
UX (user experience)
motion graphics and other moving formats
Improvements to unregistered design protections
The government will simplify the rules for unregistered design protection, which many designers find complex and difficult to navigate. Aligning the rules on what qualifies for protection and how long it lasts will make it easier for designers to understand what protects their work.
The government will also change the law so that supplementary unregistered design protection can apply when a design is disclosed anywhere in the world.
This removes a post-Brexit disadvantage: a UK designer launching at an international trade show or publishing work online globally will be able to have the same protection as one who first discloses their work in the UK.
Simplifying and modernising the design registration process
A series of practical changes will make the design registration process simpler and more streamlined. For example:
multiple objections will be set out in a single report rather than raised separately
response deadlines will be standardised
the registrar will have new powers to correct errors on the register
These changes will reduce the administrative burden on sole traders and microbusinesses, for whom registering and maintaining design protection can currently be time-consuming and complex.
Computer generated designs – ongoing work
The government will:
monitor AI use in the design industry
consider wider developments in AI and intellectual property
engage with international partners and industry stakeholders- recognising this is a fast-moving area
The existing legal framework for computer-generated designs will remain in place while this evidence base continues to develop.
A note on criminal sanctions
The Intellectual Property Act 2014 specifies that criminal sanctions can be made against anyone found to have intentionally copied a registered design without explicit consent from the design’s owner.
The call for evidence on criminal sanctions for unregistered design infringement attracted strongly held views. However, the government concluded that there is insufficient robust evidence to demonstrate that introducing criminal offences for unregistered design infringement would be a balanced, proportionate and effective remedy.
The government is therefore ruling out introducing these offences.

Notes to editors:
The government’s full response to its Consultation on Changes to the design framework is published on GOV.UK. The changes announced today (4 November 2026) are the government’s formal response to this consultation. It was led by the Intellectual Property Office (IPO) who sought views from designers, design businesses, representative organisations, legal professionals and any other interested parties. The consultation closed in November 2025 and received 90 responses.
Figures related to the UK’s design sector are set out in the Design Council’s Design Economy 2026 report. This report found that:
In 2023 the design economy contributed £136.7 billion in Gross Value Added (GVA) to the UK economy, 5.5% of total UK GVA. It grew at almost twice the rate of the UK economy between 2019 and 2023.
In 2025 there were over 2.27 million people working in the design economy. Of these, 1.88 million were employed in design occupations.
Digital design is growing in importance. The total value of digital design in 2023 is £81 billion, an increase of 50% since 2019.
Design Council’s 2022 Design Economy Report ‘People, Places and Economic Value’ found that in 2020, 92% of all design businesses were micro-businesses.
The Intellectual Property Enterprise Court (IPEC) is a specialist court dealing with intellectual property disputes. Its Small Claims Track currently handles lower-value copyright, trade mark and unregistered design cases, with a financial limit of £10,000.
The Hague System is an international mechanism administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) that allows designers to protect their designs in multiple countries through a single application.
The IPO published a Designs Practice Notice in April 2026 clarifying how applications for GUIs and animated designs are examined. The notice explains how designs showing animation or movement should currently be represented in an application and when a disclaimer is appropriate.
The Intellectual Property Office (IPO) is the official UK government body responsible for intellectual property rights including patents, designs, trade marks and copyright. More information is available at the IPO’s website.

The Prime Minister (Andy Burnham)
With permission, Mr Speaker, I would like to make a statement on nuclear test veterans.
This day has taken far too long in coming. The only reason it has arrived is the sheer courage of thousands of ex-servicemen and their families who refused to give in. Our nuclear test veterans stand with all other veterans in displaying all the typical characteristics that we so rightly commend in the British armed forces: service, bravery, dignity and determination. Yet these veterans have been left to stand apart for decades, in a wilderness and with no choice but to fight their own country for recognition and answers. It is hard to know how we can even begin to put that right today, but it does mark the day when we make a start in earnest.
Next year will mark the 75th anniversary of Britain’s first nuclear test. Operation Hurricane took place on 3 October 1952 off an island off the coast of Western Australia. It was one of many detonations that was to take place across the 1950s and 1960s in the south Pacific, involving about 22,000 British servicemen, many called up on national service.
Many facts about Britain’s nuclear test programme and what happened to the people who took part in it are not known, but one is undeniable: many young servicemen were forced to participate in the witnessing of tests without their knowledge, their consent or personal protective equipment.
Archie Hart was 18 years old when his ship was ordered to sail through the fallout of two atomic clouds for eight hours at a time. Within two years, he began developing hundreds of benign tumours, some the size of tennis balls. He later developed aggressive cancer. John Morris was wearing just a shirt, shorts and sunglasses when a nuclear bomb exploded just 20 miles away. He said:
“The flash was white, the heat like a blowtorch on your back, then we were knocked off our feet.”
When he returned home, he was diagnosed with a blood disorder. His first-born son, Steven, died at just four months old of bronchopneumonia. His parents were wrongly blamed for his death.
Members of this House and people watching this may at this point recognise a pattern they have seen before: the people given infected blood who faced questions about their lifestyle; the sub-postmasters and sub-postmistresses branded petty thieves; the Liverpool supporters accused of unspeakable things against their fellow fans. I met John and Archie five years ago and they had a very simple request, like those who had been caught up in the infected blood, Post Office and Hillsborough scandals: they just wanted the truth.
It is a sad fact that the vast majority of the 22,000 people who witnessed the tests have died, some many years ago. But they passed on the torch of their campaign over the years, and it is right and fitting that we are honoured by the presence of members of the test veterans community with us today. We salute you all and we know you represent every single person you were proud to serve alongside and every single person who was caught up in this. We are also joined by families affected: wives who have fought for husbands, and children and grandchildren for fathers and grandfathers. You have been through what no British family should, but today we recognise the depth of your commitment to the loved ones you have lost.
Those servicemen and their families were able to take their first steps out of the wilderness five years ago on the 70th anniversary of the first test. Boris Johnson as Prime Minister was right to call for the Nuclear Test Medal in 2022, finally recognising their service and their sacrifice. Their courage deserved such an honour; it contributed to the creation of the UK’s nuclear deterrent that we rely upon today to keep us safe. But a medal on the chest is no substitute for knowing the truth in your heart, nor is it sufficient for decades of questions unanswered and justice denied.
There is a very significant reason why this is an urgent issue of now and not just one of the past. It is a fact that it was not just the 22,000 who experienced cancers and a range of other health complications way above the rate to be expected in the wider population. Sadly, those health issues have been passed down the generations and have led to disability and illness in descendants, including very young children today showing signs of chalk teeth. It is a moral imperative that people get the full truth and that they get it as quickly as possible, so that the health risks are fully understood and acted upon properly. I am today committing from the very top of Government to do precisely that.
I can confirm to the House that the Government will immediately set up the nuclear test veterans independent panel. The panel will be based on the model that was successfully used after the 20th anniversary of Hillsborough, overseeing disclosure of documents and working at pace to establish the truth. I am deeply grateful to Bishop James Jones, who chaired the Hillsborough panel, for agreeing to chair the nuclear test veterans independent panel. We could not have anyone better to carry out this vital work, and I can say that from years of personal experience of working closely with him. To guarantee the independence of the panel and to ensure that the entirety of Whitehall and its arm’s length bodies fully co-operate with it, I have asked the First Secretary of State to oversee its work, but can I place on record my deep appreciation of the way that my right hon. Friend the Defence Secretary has approached this issue and his obvious care for the veterans?
The panel will operate on the “families first” principle that we established for Hillsborough, and from which Bishop James never deviates. The next step in the process will be a listening exercise for veterans, descendants and their families to set out their priorities. From that, we will work to agree the membership of the panel by the end of the year, and its terms of reference by January. These are likely to include, among other things: the historic events and conditions that servicemen were exposed to during their service; what the state knew and when, including advice given to Ministers and statements made to Parliament; and the way that the Government, particularly the Ministry of Defence, have treated the nuclear test veteran community and handled their health issues. In all things, we expect the panel to be led by the truth and nothing but the truth, while of course recognising that, in this instance, public disclosure may rightly have limitations due to national security. I firmly believe it is possible to give our veterans the former while not compromising the latter.
I want to explain why I believe a non-statutory, fully independent panel represents the best way forward for nuclear test veterans and their families. I hate to put it so bluntly, but time is not on our side, and it is not on their side. Our nuclear test veterans will not be with us forever. We need to move quickly. I understood the call for a special tribunal, but primary legislation would be required to establish it. That would mean we would still be some years from the truth. This approach will achieve maximum possible disclosure in an accelerated timeframe.
I understand why some will still have doubts, but I hope they will be reassured by the fact that the Public Authority (Accountability) Bill—the Hillsborough law—will apply to the work of this panel. We expect that the Bill will receive Royal Assent soon. Before its commencement, we are asking the whole of the Government to work in the spirit of the Bill now. As a result, its statutory duty of candour will apply to those who are asked to assist the work of the nuclear test veterans independent panel, with criminal penalties possible for those who do not.
I can tell the House that Bishop James has agreed to deliver an interim report in time for the 75th anniversary of the first test on 3 October 2027. Given the enormous challenge I accept that that represents, I will ensure that he gets full buy-in from the whole system. He will be able to come directly to the First Secretary of State, or indeed to myself, if he encounters any resistance from the system. Final findings will follow as quickly as possible after that. In the meantime, this process will not delay our commitment to pursue further actions in support of the nuclear test veteran community, including memorialisation, education and research. We will start now on developing plans for a national memorial service on 3 October next year.
All of this represents a long overdue step towards truth and justice, and it is principally due to the determination of veterans and their families. I pay tribute to the organisations that have supported them thus far, especially LABRATS and in particular Alan Owen, Steve Purse and Steve Foote, and Ian Hall and Nigel Heaps of the Nuclear Community Charity Fund. I would like to thank all the members of the Hillsborough Law Now campaign, who have given magnificent solidarity to our test veterans and their families.
I also want to recognise the efforts of many others who have supported the cause, including my hon. Friends the Members for Salford (Rebecca Long Bailey) and for South Shields (Emma Lewell) particularly, and the right hon. Member—indeed, I will call him my right hon. Friend—for South Holland and The Deepings (Sir John Hayes), who has played a huge role in bringing today about. This cross-party support has sustained the campaign up to now, and I hope we can maintain it in this House and get it behind the panel as it goes forward. I pay tribute, too, to the journalist Susie Boniface, who has been utterly dedicated to this cause, and to Victoria Derbyshire for the care of her coverage of it.
I have had the privilege of working with this community over a number of years. I know many Members of this House have constituents who are part of the community, and have supported them in a diligent way. I know I will speak for everyone when I say that this country should be proud of them all. They are decent, principled, determined and, yes, straight-talking, but they have had to be, and they have always done that with great dignity and integrity. Today we owe them some straight talking back. There is no getting away from it: you and your families have been badly let down by the country you served. You deserved better and, belatedly, we will now try to provide that. You, and the people you served with, are the best of British—and, from here, you will not be let down again. I commend this statement to the House.

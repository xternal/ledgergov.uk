Today, the steering group of the Timms Review of Personal Independence Payment (PIP) has published its Final Report. A copy of the Report has been laid before Parliament, and it is available in a range of accessible versions at GOV.UK.
In the 13 years since its introduction, the world around PIP has changed. Despite this, it had never been fully reviewed. It is in that context that the Timms Review was launched in summer 2025 to ensure PIP was fair and fit for the future. For the first time at this scale, the Government co-produced the Review with disabled people, the organisations that represent them, and other experts.
I want to thank the Review’s co-chairs – the Minister for Social Security and Disability, Sharon Brennan and Dr Clenton Farquharson CBE – and the twelve other members of the steering group for their hard work and expertise over the past year. I also want to thank the more than 40,000 people and organisations who contributed their time, experience and expertise to Review’s work.
The Review has reached a clear conclusion: a fundamental reset of PIP, including a new assessment, is required. It identifies three core challenges.
First, the assessment is not fit for purpose. Many report it is adversarial and dehumanising, that it struggles to reflect fluctuating and cumulative conditions, and that it can leave people fearing that work, treatment or greater independence will count against them. The Review heard that people were afraid to exercise, volunteer, work or even meet friends in case doing so put their PIP at risk. Rather than considering people's individual needs, aspirations, barriers and extra costs, the assessment asks only what someone can't do. As a result, far from supporting independence, the Review found that the assessment can itself be a barrier.
Second, awards do not consistently enable the right contribution towards the additional costs that people face. Findings from the Areas of Extra Cost Survey, reviewed by the steering group, showed that PIP does not always target the highest levels of support at those with the most severe needs. The future system should make the basis for support clearer and ensure decisions better reflect individual circumstances.
Finally, the system offers too little scope for personalised solutions. In response, the Review puts forward its vision for a wholly reformed benefit. It recommends that a cash payment should remain central, but proposes that opportunities to access to other services could improve the support a person receives.
The Government accepts the case for fundamental reform of PIP. It endorses the central principles that should guide that work: a more humane assessment process, in particular for those with the most severe needs; a system that better reflects an individual’s barriers and additional costs; and more personalised solutions that enable, rather than discourage, participation.
Reform will require careful engagement, design and legislation. However, as a first step, in wider reform the Government will accept two specific recommendations.
First, people whose health conditions are lifelong, progressive or unlikely to improve, where there is clear evidence that their functional needs are unlikely to change or are likely to worsen, they should not face repeated award reviews.
Second, the Government agrees that people with the most serious, well-evidenced needs should have access to a fast-track route without an unnecessary full assessment. That should include a rapid route for people receiving end-of-life care.
The Government will now consider the Review's findings in full, alongside Alan Milburn’s Review of Young People and Work. Although the Reviews had different remits, both identify fragmented systems that ask people to prove what they cannot do, rather than understand what they could do with the right support. They identify the worry that participation, treatment or improvement could put financial security at risk. And both argue for support that is more personalised, joined up and responsive to people’s lives.
Taken together, the two Reviews share ambition for system that offers better support built around the individual; gives greater security for those with the most severe needs; better recognises additional costs; and gives disabled people the confidence to live their fullest lives.
The Government will continue to listen to the voices of D/deaf and disabled people and others as we develop our proposals, which we will set out in a White Paper early next year.

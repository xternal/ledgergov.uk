The Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero (Miatta Fahnbulleh)
With permission, Madam Deputy Speaker, I would like to make a statement following the conclusion of the COP31 climate summit. This year’s climate conference sought to confront a hard truth: that the world is on course to exceed 1.5° of warming by around 2030. We all know what this will mean for communities at home and abroad. At around 1.4° of warming today, we are already seeing the impacts.
Here in Britain, this summer brought record heatwaves, droughts and wildfires, and it was people’s homes, their livelihoods and the communities that they love that were impacted. And not just that: lives were lost, too. As my right hon. Friend the Secretary of State for Health and Social Care informed the House earlier, the UK Health Security Agency released data today showing that we saw a total of 5,258 heat-related deaths in the UK over 2026. We cannot stand by and let this be the new normal.
Overseas, we saw the devastation on the Nepal-China border with heartbreaking scenes of communities destroyed. Around the world, communities on the frontline of the climate crisis are seeing their homes destroyed and their livelihoods threatened. Climate change is here, and as we overshoot 1.5°, the impacts will be felt in country after country, the world over. So I went to COP31 clear about the challenge we face, and about the urgency of taking climate action. The target of 1.5° remains the right target. As the UN Secretary-General has said, overshoot does not mean surrender; it means redoubling our efforts. We arrived in Antalya determined to work with our partners to reach consensus on the need for urgent, ambitious action.
COP31 delivered important progress across the wider negotiations, especially through the action agenda, but as the House knows, parties were unable to reach consensus on the cover decision. After concerted negotiations and tireless efforts to up the ambition, the substance that was proposed in the final cover decision did not meet the moment and we were unwilling to support it. It moved us backwards on the science when we needed to move faster. It failed to reaffirm our existing collective commitment to transition away from fossil fuels and our commitment to keep 1.5° within reach. It compromised where we needed bold collective ambition, and I was immensely disappointed by this outcome.
We were not alone in standing up for these issues. We stood alongside small island developing states and some of the least developed countries on the very frontline of this crisis, which could not accept this as an adequate outcome. Now we have a responsibility to uphold and protect the Paris agreement. We have a duty to protect communities here in the UK, across the Commonwealth and in the countries most vulnerable to climate shocks.
Although we could not find consensus on the cover decision, COP31 saw success beyond this. We worked with partners around the world and made progress that was not reflected in the proposed cover text. The technical negotiations delivered progress on areas including the global goal on adaptation and just transition. Conferences of the parties are increasingly about turning commitments into action through the action agenda, which was a significant focus for the Turkish COP presidency. We saw the strongest ever backing for mobilising action, with 119 countries using the closing event to recognise the wider implementation that is happening. That is concrete progress, and it demonstrates the global momentum for the energy transition.
I am proud of the UK’s role in this effort. More than ever, it has strengthened our commitment to multilateralism and our resolve to work with partners in every region of the world to drive action ahead of COP32. I want to thank the UK’s negotiating team, led by Kate Hughes, and everyone across the UK civil service, business and civil society who contributed to this effort. I have seen at first hand their dedication, hard work and determination to make progress. Together we focused our efforts on five areas: electrification and clean energy; methane; forests and oceans; finance; and resilience.
First, on electrification, we backed Türkiye’s 35 by 35 global electrification pledge, which was supported by around 80 countries, and published our clean electrification road map. We announced new support and partnerships under the global clean power alliance finance mission to support green industrialisation in emerging economies.
Secondly, we convened a high-level event, chaired by the Foreign Secretary, to drive greater action on methane and non-CO 2 emissions, which is the fastest way to slow warming. We are now working with countries including China, Nigeria, Brazil and Australia to turn that ambition into action.
Thirdly, to protect forests and oceans, we brought countries together to maintain momentum behind forest finance, including the Tropical Forest Forever Facility. We announced further UK support to tackle plastic pollution, and an additional £20 million to protect coral reefs, marine wildlife and the coastal communities that depend on them.
Fourthly, to support countries to build their resilience to a changing climate, we focused on better end-to-end early warning systems, linked to early action, and access to finance that reaches communities before disaster strikes. The Prime Minister announced the UK’s first ever climate security envoy, Dame Madeleine Alessandri, recognising that climate change increasingly affects our food security, our infrastructure and our national security.
Our fifth objective was to scale up finance. We committed £400 million to the climate investment funds’ AMPLIFi programme, which will mobilise up to $5 billion in private capital for clean energy projects in developing economies. Our development finance institution Bill, alongside those of Norway and Canada, will work towards mobilising $10 billion of climate finance in emerging markets by 2030.
We regret that COP31 did not provide greater confidence on adaptation finance ahead of COP32, but we did agree to transition the adaptation fund to serve the Paris agreement—a concrete step towards getting more finance flowing. We achieved all that against an incredibly challenging geopolitical backdrop. At COP31, the Prime Minister pledged a
“new era of British leadership”.
The UK will live up to its commitments, and we will support others to live up to theirs. Our task now is to turn the commitments made at COP31 into faster emission reductions to keep 1.5° within reach, into greater resilience, and into investment in a cleaner, more secure and more prosperous global economy.
Let me be clear that, as we take up the G20 presidency next year, our commitment to multilateralism is unwavering. It is through the multilateral system that we must ultimately deliver the collective action the world needs. We will keep pushing for progress on resilience, finance, debt and clean energy. As we set out in our published response to the COP, we will work with partners to speed the transition away from fossil fuels and protect forests. We will support clean electrification across the world, delivering affordable power as a response to overshoot. We will work to reform the COP process and uphold its integrity so that it can deliver the Paris agreement. We will work to protect our oceans and forests, and support mountain regions and glacier-dependent communities facing the impacts of climate change. Critically, we will work with the Ethiopian presidency to drive progress at COP32 in Addis Ababa next year. We must return with advances made, stronger resolve and an improved COP process. The world needs a stronger collective response; we have no choice but to work together to deliver it.
I conclude by setting out why this issue matters to people here at home. Clean electrification will reduce our reliance on volatile fossil fuel markets and deliver home-grown clean energy, which will drive down bills. Building resilience protects the critical supply chains on which we depend. Acting with others on the overshoot of 1.5° addresses the risks that climate change presents to the UK, so we can uphold the very first duty of Government: to protect our communities today and in the future. The booming global green economy, which will be worth up to £3 trillion by 2035 according to the International Energy Agency, offers the opportunity to unlock growth and jobs across the country.
What we fought for in Antalya and what we are fighting for at home are two sides of the same coin: a more secure and prosperous future, breathing space for families and businesses, and action to tackle the climate crisis that threatens our very way of life. I commend this statement to the House.

The Minister for Social Security and Disability (Sir Stephen Timms)
With permission, I would like to make a statement on the final report of the Timms review of the personal independence payment. PIP provides vital support to deaf and disabled people and people with long-term health conditions. It contributes to the additional costs they face, and aims to help them to live independently, to participate in their communities and often to work.
PIP was introduced 13 years ago. Since then, lots has changed, but PIP has never been fully reviewed, and it has not kept pace, so the Timms review was launched in summer last year. The review was co-produced with disabled people, the organisations that represent them and other experts. It is the first time that the UK Government have co-produced a review of this scale. I express heartfelt thanks to my two co-chairs, Sharon Brennan and Dr Clenton Farquharson, and to the 12 steering group members, whom the three of us recruited from more than 340 applicants. The group has met for a full day on 15 occasions since January, with lots of other discussions in between. The whole group has shown exemplary commitment to the task.
Each of the 15 members, including me, had one vote. Almost all 12 of the steering group members had lived experience of disability or long-term ill health, as well as a range of other expertise. The review heard from more than 40,000 people and organisations, including more than 38,000 responses to the call for evidence, 138 community workshops, and evidence from clinicians, charities, deaf and disabled people’s organisations, academics, frontline practitioners and international experts. I thank everyone who shared their expertise and their experiences—often very personal ones—with us.
This was an ambitious review, and it asked big, complex questions. Co-production means that every participant does not necessarily agree on every detail, but we created the conditions for people with different perspectives to test the evidence and shape the outcome as equals. The review’s report has been submitted to my right hon. Friend the Secretary of State, and the Government will now consider its findings.
I will turn to the review’s central conclusion: the system is no longer fit for purpose and requires a fundamental reset. The review is clear that PIP remains a vital source of support, but it finds that the system is failing in three fundamental respects. First, the assessment is not fit for purpose. It does not consider people’s needs, aspirations, barriers or extra costs—it is just about what someone cannot do. Many find it adversarial and dehumanising. It struggles to reflect fluctuating and cumulative conditions properly, and too often it leaves people fearing that work, health treatment or greater independence will count against them. Far from supporting independence, it can be a barrier to it.
Secondly, awards do not consistently enable the right contribution towards the additional costs that people face. The areas of extra cost survey, reviewed by the steering group, showed that PIP does not always target the highest levels of support at those with the most severe needs. The future system should make the basis for support clearer, with decisions better reflecting individual circumstances.
Thirdly, the system offers too little scope for personalised solutions. The review recommends that cash should remain central, but that people should have the choice to access practical services as part of the overall support they receive. That would allow the system to respond more flexibly to individual needs.
The review reaches the view that we need a full system reform, not just some adjustment of the form or descriptors. It does not prescribe a final set of eligibility rules, because it recognises that decisions about eligibility criteria should sit with the elected Government, accountable to this House, but it does suggest principles on which those decisions should be made. Support should correspond more closely to genuine additional costs and needs. Cumulative and fluctuating impacts should be recognised properly. The highest support should reflect the most severe needs. A good deal of further work is needed. The Government will undertake this work, and we will set out our plans in next year’s White Paper.
We want the system to depend on a conversation, rather than a confrontation. It should not repeatedly demand the same information, but should offer more personalised solutions that enable, not discourage, participation. The system should offer greater security for those whose conditions and needs will not improve, and it should be one that people can understand and trust. A fundamental reset of this kind will require careful engagement, design and legislation.
We will take the time to consider the recommendations in full, but as a first step, we will agree two specific recommendations. First, people with serious, well-evidenced conditions, whose effects on daily life are clear, should have access to a fast-track route without an unnecessary full assessment. That should include a distinct and rapid route for people at the end of life—as at present—but others should benefit from a fast track, too. Secondly, people whose health conditions are lifelong or degenerative, when there is clear evidence that their functional needs are unlikely to improve, should not face repeated award reviews, although they should retain the right to request a review when they think that their needs have changed. These changes will require time to plan, but where we can improve people’s experience of the current PIP scheme sooner, we will do so.
This review will now be considered in full, alongside Alan Milburn’s review of young people and work. The two reviews had different remits, but their diagnoses have much in common. Both find a fragmented system that asks people to prove what they cannot do rather than understand what they could do with the right support, both identify the worry that participation, treatment or improvement could put somebody’s financial security at risk, and both argue for support that is more personalised, more joined up and more responsive to people’s lives.
Taken together, the two reviews share an ambition for a system that offers security for those with the most severe needs, support built around the individual, better recognition of additional costs, and confidence that work, treatment or participation will not put essential support at risk That is the basis on which the Government will bring the two strands of work together in a White Paper early next year, setting out our plans.
The Government have been clear: we want to bring the benefits bill down, but not through crude cuts that push people into hardship and increase costs elsewhere in the system. This review, alongside Alan Milburn’s, provides the foundation for a fundamentally different system: a new assessment, support aligned more closely with additional costs, and personalised solutions that combine financial security with practical help.
We want a system that provides lasting security for those with the most severe needs, recognises better the costs and barriers that people face, and gives disabled people confidence that work, education or participation will not count against them. We want a system that is fair to the people who rely on it, fair to the taxpayers who fund it, and sustainable for the future. That is the reset that the Government will take forward, continuing to work with people with lived experience as we do so. We will provide a full response in our White Paper early next year, with legislation to follow.
I commend this statement to the House.

Those of us who are advocates for people who are non-verbal or who have learning disabilities know the complexity of the system—continually having to produce the same information or the same paperwork. I therefore welcome the single digital profile and the benefits it could bring. Will the Minister say a little bit more about how that may save costs both for families and for the taxpayer?

Sir Stephen Timms
I am grateful to my hon. Friend for all the interest that he has taken in our work during the course of the review. I was very pleased to have on the steering group a member with a learning disability.
The digital profile, which we envisage being populated from the initial gateway on, would be available for the person who has applied for the benefit to use in whatever way they chose: for other bits of the benefits system, for a carer’s assessment if they subsequently went through one, or perhaps for applying for a disabled facilities grant or something of that kind. The point that he makes is a very important one and it is often made to us: people are asked time after time after time to provide the same information to different bits of the system. We think we can do better than that.

Madam Deputy Speaker (Caroline Nokes)
For the final question, I call Chris Vince.

Chris Vince (Harlow) (Lab/Co-op)
I thank the Minister for his statement and for the incredible amount of work that he has done, in collaboration with disabled groups, to get the report to us. He is right to say that those people who have the highest need should get the highest amount of support. As it is the last question, may I ask him to reiterate that and explain how the reforms will benefit those with the most severe conditions in my constituency of Harlow and across the UK?

Sir Stephen Timms
That is very much the objective. I am delighted to reiterate that point and I thank my hon. Friend for giving me the opportunity to do so. We want an assessment that correctly establishes who has additional costs arising from disability. Evidence that we have looked at in the course of the review shows that quite high awards of PIP sometimes go to people with very low costs—or sometimes to those with no costs—and the other way around is true as well. We want to do a much better job, and I am confident that we can.

Madam Deputy Speaker
I thank the Minister for his statement.

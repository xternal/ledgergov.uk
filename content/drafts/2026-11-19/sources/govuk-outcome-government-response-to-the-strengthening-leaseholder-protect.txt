Government response to the Strengthening leaseholder protections over charges and services consultation: further service charge measures
Ministerial foreword
This government made a clear and unambiguous commitment in its manifesto to act where previous governments had failed and finally bring the feudal leasehold system to an end. As we do so, we will not leave existing leaseholders behind. Alongside progressing the reforms necessary to dismantle the leasehold system in this Parliament, the government continues to strengthen protections for existing leaseholders in the here and now.
On 12 and 13 November, we laid a group of five complementary statutory instruments to enact provisions from Part 4 of the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. The regulations introduced will drive up the transparency of service charges, reform the litigation costs regime and provide for fairer and more transparent building insurance payments. Taken together, they will help leaseholders better understand what they are paying for and empower them to challenge charges where they believe them to be unreasonable.
In addition to implementing measures contained in the 2024 Act, the ‘Strengthening leaseholder protections over charges and services’ consultation undertaken last year sought views on a number of further reforms outside its scope, including mandating reserve funds, introducing mandatory qualifications for managing agents and improving the section 20 major works regime. We received a large number of detailed and thoughtful responses to these proposals, and we thank every individual and organisation that took the time to submit their views.
Having considered carefully the feedback received, we have decided to proceed with a number of the proposed measures. We will legislate to make reserve funds mandatory with a view to reducing the risk of large, unaffordable one-off bills for leaseholders and to support the timely delivery of important works. To provide leaseholders with greater transparency over the work that reserve funds will pay for and to drive longer-term strategic planning, we will also legislate to mandate Asset Management Plans. We will also reform the outdated major works process, including by making improvements to consultation procedures and restricting the grounds upon which landlords may seek dispensation from the requirement to consult leaseholders.
The government will also legislate to give the Secretary of State powers to cap permission fees and administration charges. No longer will leaseholders and homeowners on freehold estates be forced to pay exorbitant fees in order to secure their landlord’s permission for simple requests like keeping a pet or processing a change of mortgage provider. A public consultation will be launched on the scope of what should be capped and the amount for each item.
Finally, when it comes to managing agents, we have made clear that we will go beyond the proposals set out in the consultation with a commitment to legislate to regulate all property agents through an independent statutory regulator. As a minimum this will cover managing agents across leasehold and commonhold, estate agents, letting agents and estate managers on freehold estates. We will set out further detail on the government’s strategy for the regulation of property agents in due course, including on licensing, qualifications and codes of practice.
For too long, the reality of home ownership has fallen woefully short of the dream for far too many leaseholders and homeowners on privately managed estates. We are determined to provide relief to those suffering as a result of unfair and unreasonable practices. The reforms set out in this response will help to do just that.

Executive summary
This government is committed to addressing issues in the current system that create an imbalance between leaseholders and freeholders. Part 4 of the 2024 Act contains a number of provisions designed to standardise and increase the transparency of service charges and remove barriers for leaseholders to challenge their landlord. The 2024 Act also includes provision to scrap the presumption that leaseholders should pay their landlords’ litigation costs through the service or administration charge, thereby removing a significant barrier to challenging poor practice.
A consultation ran from 4 July to 26 September 2025 to seek views on how best to implement the 2024 Act proposals. On 15 July 2026, we published a Written Ministerial Statement on the implementation of the 2024 Act, which confirmed our intention to bring forward the measures in Part 4 of the 2024 Act. Alongside that statement we published the government response to the 2025 consultation covering the responses to the questions on implementing the 2024 Act. On 12 and 13 November, we laid a group of 5 complementary statutory instruments to enact these provisions.
Alongside that, the consultation set out proposals to reform the major works process under section 20 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. It also sought views on a proposal to strengthen the regulation of managing agents through mandating professional qualifications, as well as seeking views on the robustness of longstanding aspects of the system to ensure they remain fit for purpose. The government response to these proposals are set out below.
New additional service charge reforms
Mandating reserve funds and planning for major works
The government will legislate within the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill to mandate reserve funds to reduce the risk of large, unaffordable one-off bills for leaseholders and to support the timely delivery of important works. As part of this we will also legislate to mandate Asset Management Plans (“AMPs”) to provide leaseholders with greater transparency over the work that reserve funds will pay for and to drive longer-term strategic planning. We will collaborate with stakeholders to take these proposals forward to ensure that they help deliver the certainty that leaseholders need to support with the cost of living, and deliver their intended benefits in a way that is fair and transparent. We will also carefully consider the practical implementation of mandatory reserve funds. We will phase in the requirement to have reserve funds over a number of years to limit the financial impact on leaseholders and support an orderly transition to the new regime. In addition, we will explore options to ease affordability pressures for leaseholders, with a particular focus on those in buildings in poor condition with no existing funds. In developing the detail of these proposals we will consider how best to ensure that any requirements are proportionate and take into account the nature of different buildings, and will also consider how best to apply these principles to freehold estates taking into account responses to the separate consultations on this issue.
Reforming the major works consultation process
The government will uplift the section 20 threshold above which landlords are required to consult to £500 for qualifying works and £200 for Qualifying Long-Term Agreements (“QLTAs”). This uplift is broadly in line with inflation since the thresholds were last updated in 2003. This will make the consultation thresholds more proportionate and relevant to today’s economic landscape, and will go hand in hand with measures to rebalance the dispensation process. The government will exempt certain activities, including energy contracts and single energy providers (such as heat networks), subject to meeting certain conditions, since they are no longer compatible with the current major works consultation regime. To ensure transparency, we will bring forward measures to require landlords to notify leaseholders about these contracts. These measures will be implemented through a combination of primary and secondary legislation.
The government considers that effective consultation with leaseholders is a vital component of the major works consultation process and provides a number of benefits. We will improve the existing consultation process, by introducing standardised forms and setting a deadline for works to begin through amendments to secondary legislation. We will also legislate through the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill to overturn the effect of the Supreme Court’s decision in 2013 in the case of Daejan Investments Limited vs Benson and others, and restrict the grounds by which landlords may seek dispensation from the First-tier Tribunal from the requirement to consult leaseholders. We will also bring forward measures to give leaseholders the right to opt out of the consultation process if the majority agree.
Protecting leaseholders’ money
The government believes it is vital that leaseholders receive assurance that their monies are being held and managed safely. Measures in the 2024 Act – particularly covering the annual report – will help give some assurance, including confirmation that monies are held in trust. However, we recognise that measures to mandate reserve funds will increase the amount of service charge monies held and so we will work with stakeholders to establish how best to achieve stronger protection of service charge monies, including whether to bring into force section 42A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987.
Protections for leaseholders paying fixed service charges
The government recognises that tenants and leaseholders who pay fixed service charges have fewer rights and protections in return for cost certainty. We recognise the challenges in extending protections to those who pay fixed service charges, which we will continue to consider. In the meantime, we will prepare non-statutory guidance on service charges for social housing residents to ensure they know what to expect and support consistent practice.
Powers to appoint a manager or replace a managing agent
The government recognises the desire of leaseholders to have more control over how their properties are managed and we will continue to review options on powers to appoint or replace a managing agent. We will carefully consider the concerns raised around disrupting good building management and the completion of major work and essential maintenance.
The government recognises that section 24 is a key measure that leaseholders should be able to use effectively when they are experiencing serious failure by the landlord or managing agent to manage their building effectively. We are grateful for the feedback we have received on how section 24 is working in practice, and the possible areas to improve the process, and we will review this issue further as part of our work to take forward the independent regulation of property agents.
Providing information and services digitally
The government is supportive of more documents being provided by electronic means where this is in line with leaseholders’ needs and preferences. It is keen to see further investment in digital communications, which present opportunities for efficiency savings and for improved leaseholder experience. In the context of reforms already taken forward across the sector, the government does not immediately plan to take further steps in this area but will keep this issue under review.
Qualifications of managing agents
The government’s view is that, taking into account the recommendations of the Housing Communities and Local Government Select Committee’s pre-legislative scrutiny report for the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, strengthening regulation of managing agents requires more than mandatory qualifications as was proposed in the consultation – though qualifications remain an important part of the picture.
We are of the view that regulation should go further to address the issues in the sector and to stamp out bad practice. We also think that regulation should not apply to managing agents alone and that there are significant issues in both the estate agent and letting agent sectors that should be addressed through regulation. The government therefore intends to bring forward future legislation to regulate property agents through an independent statutory regulator. As a minimum this will cover managing agents across leasehold and commonhold, estate agents, letting agents and estate managers on freehold estates.
The government plans to set out further detail on our strategy for the regulation of property agents in due course, including on licensing, qualifications and codes of practice.
The consultation: content and approach
The consultation sought views from leaseholders, landlords, managing agents and representative organisations on the implementation of measures in the 2024 Act to improve the rights of existing leaseholders and help them better hold their landlords to account by increasing the transparency of service charges and building insurance arrangements, as well as by tackling unjustified litigation costs. The consultation also sought views on new proposals for reform around the charges that leaseholders pay and the services they receive, as well as measures to strengthen the regulation of managing agents through mandating professional qualifications.
The consultation was open for 12 weeks, between 4 July and 26 September 2025. Respondents could respond via an online survey on Citizen Space, or by email.
In addition to this consultation, we engaged with a range of stakeholders, including representative organisations, to ensure they had the opportunity to hear from government and to give their views on the proposals.
The consultation asked 205 questions in total, including questions using a response scale and free text box responses.
The consultation adhered to the consultation principles guidance issued by the Cabinet Office.
Throughout this consultation response, we will use the term “landlords” as shorthand for “freeholders, landlords of intermediate leases, Resident Management Companies and Right to Manage Companies”, as these are the people who are able to demand a service charge.
We use the term “leaseholder” to refer to those owning houses or flats who are liable to pay a service charge (including those owning their homes on a shared ownership basis). The exception to this is where we refer to legislation which uses the term “tenant” to mean a leaseholder (not someone who rents a property in the private rented sector).
We also refer to the “appropriate tribunal” or “relevant tribunal” in reference to the First-tier Tribunal (and Upper Tribunal) in England and the Leasehold Valuation Tribunal in Wales.
Geographical scope
While the proposals in the consultation related to both England and Wales, this response by the UK government relates to implementation in England only except where indicated. Welsh Ministers will consider the consultation findings separately and determine their own approach to implementation in Wales, including whether and how to take forward equivalent reforms and if any proposed primary legislation in England should apply in Wales.

Question 118: Do you have any other comments or observations on how reserve funds should work in practice that need to be taken into account when preparing legislation?
There were 496 responses to this question.
There was strong support from all respondent groups for legislating to mandate or encourage the creation of reserve funds for both new and existing leases, with most respondents agreeing that this would protect leaseholders from large, unexpected bills. Some respondents also suggested that, if aligned with an Asset Management Plan (AMP), reserve funds would encourage effective forward planning and building maintenance.
There were mixed views on the scope of the proposals. While some respondents supported an exemption for local authorities due to the associated complexities, others suggested that they should be within scope of any legislation. Views from local authorities on mandating or encouraging reserve funds varied, with many local authority respondents highlighting potential incompatibility with Housing Revenue Accounts, and additional financial and administrative burden.
Some local authorities also highlighted potential complexity if implementing a reserve fund in mixed-tenure blocks where the local authority pays a share of the cost of the works, and sought clarification on whether the share it pays must also be held in a reserve fund. The government notes that, in mixed tenure blocks containing social tenants who do not pay for major works, the local authority itself pays for a proportional share of these works, which may introduce additional accounting comp relation to reserve funds. Some local authorities asked whether they would be expected to pay this proportional share into reserve funds.
Similarly, while most respondents supported making reserve funds mandatory, others argued that they should be optional and dependent on a majority vote by leaseholders, especially for existing leases.
Many respondents, particularly leaseholders, raised concerns about potential misuse or exploitation and called for any reserve funds, mandatory or otherwise, to be governed by clear, universal rules to ensure fairness. Some respondents also called for standardised reporting and regular communication with leaseholders to promote transparency.
Many respondents highlighted the importance of appropriate controls and transparency over how the funds are stored and spent. There were several different suggestions about how this could work in practice, including online portals showing the current level of the reserve fund. Some respondents went further and called for independent oversight to strengthen protections for leaseholders.
Many respondents requested that funds be held in interest-bearing but low risk trust accounts and that the reserve fund be ring-fenced and clearly distinct from service charges. Some respondents called for the legislation to detail certain permitted circumstances where reserve funds can be used, while other respondents suggested that leaseholder consent should be obtained prior to spending any reserve funds.
Many respondents, particularly leaseholders, said that contributions to the reserve fund should be proportionate and not create an unaffordable financial burden. Several respondents called for contributions to be capped or determined by affordability tests, while others felt it appropriate for leaseholders to be consulted on what they considered a suitable contribution level. A small number of respondents also suggested that, for new buildings or developments, developers should be required to make an initial contribution to the reserve fund.

Question 122: What should be an appropriate transition period for introducing AMPs?
There were 388 responses to this question.
The vast majority of respondents supported the mandating of AMPs for both new and existing leases.
Respondents, covering all stakeholder groups, strongly supported a two-tier approach: leaseholders should automatically receive a plain English summary setting out core information, and then have the right to request or inspect the full AMP and all supporting documents. Most leaseholders expressed a strong preference for AMPs to avoid jargon or highly technical surveyor reports, and many respondents favoured a standardised format or approach. Critically, most respondents felt that AMPs should be presented in their proper context to aid understanding – in particular emphasising that costs are estimates and not a guarantee that work will definitely happen when anticipated by the AMPs.
Some respondents, particularly leaseholders and leaseholder representatives, expressed concern that AMPs could be misused and used as justifications to impose inflated or unnecessary costs. Respondents also called for clear communication and explanation on how projected works connect to reserve funds, as well as how this might impact future service charge levels. In addition, most leaseholders called for greater involvement in the preparation of AMPs – including the right to be consulted and to challenge before they are finalised.
Several respondents, across all stakeholder groups, called for AMPs to be updated regularly, and many leaseholders felt that they should be notified of significant changes proactively.
Most respondents, across all stakeholder groups, strongly supported the provision of AMP information, where possible, through existing mechanisms. This might include providing the information with the annual report or service charge demand form. A small number of respondents also advocated for a digital approach, either through an online portal or electronic communications, with the right to see paper copies on request.
In terms of introduction, the majority of leaseholders favoured a transition period of 12 months or less for introducing these plans. Leaseholders wanted an earlier introduction, arguing that much of the information was already available and that a year represented the right balance between the time required to put an AMP in place, including through commissioning any surveys, and the importance of leaseholders having this information as soon as possible. In contrast most landlords and managing agents considered a longer time period was appropriate, with most calling for an 18-month to 3-year transition. The main reason cited was the amount of work required to ensure that AMPs are adequate – including data cleansing requirements – and the availability of suitably qualified surveyors. There was widespread agreement across all respondents that a phased or risk-based approach would be more effective, for example starting with high-risk buildings over 18 metres in height.

A small majority of respondents answering these questions (landlords, managing agents and representatives of RMCs/RtMs) stated that they do not routinely prepare AMPs or provide them to leaseholders. Some prepared asset or capital expenditure plans internally but generally did not share these with leaseholders. Such plans, if shared, were done so either as part of any sales information pack or as a summary document. Cost, staff capacity and data limitations were cited as the main reasons why AMPs were not more widely prepared or shared.
Most respondents reported that the preparation of AMPs is outsourced to surveyors or specialist consultants. This is particularly so for larger or more complex buildings. The length of time to prepare a plan, and level of detail provided, varies considerably depending on the complexity of the development scheme. AMPs for simpler blocks take a few days and contain far less information than complex schemes, which often take many months and are more detailed in nature.
Most respondents considered that the proposal represented a shift from a more fragmented, short-term approach towards long-term strategic planning and would lead to a greater level of detail and standardisation compared to current practice. This is because current practice is for AMPs to vary significantly by building type and to be tailored to suit local circumstances. On the costs of implementation, the majority of respondents suggested that there would be additional costs for employing professional staff (surveyors and engineers), as well as additional administrative costs – including costs of data collection and system changes as well as the regular preparation and sharing of plans. Those respondents who provided quantitative figures revealed a wide range of cost impacts of the proposals.
Some respondents, in particular social landlords, also raised concerns about the potential duplication/overlap with other regulatory regimes (e.g. in relation to building safety). They considered that aligning AMP preparation with existing regulatory requirements and practice (e.g. stock condition surveys) would limit cost increases.
Many leaseholders who responded to these questions expressed concerns that they would bear the added cost of preparing AMPs and felt that they should not be liable for such costs.

Most respondents supported tribunal-based enforcement on the grounds that it is consistent with other measures for non-compliance and provides a clear route to accountability.
In contrast some, mainly individual leaseholders, opposed using a tribunal-based approach, noting that reliance on the tribunal process would place a high burden on leaseholders, who often lack the skill and expertise to bring applications. Government response
The government recognises the potential benefits of reserve funds in supporting long-term maintenance and financial resilience of residential buildings. Well-designed reserve fund arrangements can help smooth costs over time, reduce the risk of large, unexpected bills, and support the timely upkeep and effective stewardship of buildings.
The government will legislate within the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill to mandate reserve funds for leasehold properties to reduce the risk of large, unaffordable one-off bills for leaseholders, support more predictable contributions towards future major works, and help ensure necessary works can be delivered in a timely manner.
Alongside this, the government will legislate to mandate Asset Management Plans (AMPs), to provide a transparent and evidence-based basis for reserve fund contributions, helping leaseholders understand what works are planned and what reserve funds will pay for, while supporting longer-term strategic planning and maintenance of buildings.
Consultation responses raised a number of complexities which the government will consider carefully, particularly around ensuring reserve fund arrangements are fair, transparent and subject to appropriate safeguards. We will collaborate with leaseholder and industry groups to ensure that reserve funds and AMPs deliver their intended benefits in a way that is fair and transparent, and to ensure that AMPs are high-quality, consistent, accessible and proportionate.
The government will also carefully consider the practical implementation of mandatory reserve funds. We will phase in the requirement to have reserve funds over a number of years to limit the financial impact on leaseholders and support an orderly transition to the new regime. In addition, we will explore options to ease affordability pressures for leaseholders, with a particular focus on those in buildings in poor condition with no existing funds.
In developing the detail of these proposals we will further consider how best to ensure that any requirements are proportionate and take into account the nature of different buildings. As part of this we will also consider how best to apply these principles to freehold estates taking into account responses to the separate consultation on this issue.
The government will also continue to consider the case for exemptions by landlord type, adding to our evidence base and undertaking further engagement with the sector to inform policy development. Our starting point is that we should look to extend reserve funds and AMPs to all landlords, including social landlords, to ensure all leaseholders benefit from the same protections, but we will need to collaborate further with local authorities and PRPs to identify and develop workable arrangements, including considering interactions with the Housing Revenue Account for local authorities.

There were 295 relevant responses to this question.
The majority of respondents agreed the thresholds should be changed to £600 for major works and £300 for Qualifying Long Term agreements (QLTAs).
Among those who disagreed with the proposal, mainly individual leaseholders, several respondents preferred keeping the current thresholds or increasing them by less than proposed. This ranged between £350 to £500 as the threshold for major works, and between £200 and £250 for the threshold for QLTAs. These respondents felt that higher thresholds would allow more funds to be spent without consultation, reducing transparency over costs requested from leaseholders for major works.
Some respondents, in particular landlords and managing agents, supported higher thresholds, ranging between £750 to £1000 as the threshold for major works, and up to £500 for QLTAs. These respondents considered that a higher threshold would ensure works captured by the new thresholds are genuine ‘major works’, and would help increase the efficiency of landlords (or managing agents) by preventing unnecessary delays and costs from frequent consultations on minor repairs.
A few respondents, both landlords and leaseholders, put forward alternative views. The most common suggestion was to link the proposed thresholds to an inflation index, such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI), to avoid the need for regular legislative changes for minor adjustments. Others proposed varying the thresholds according to the size and scale of the property, to ensure the measures remain proportionate.
Government response
The government will legislate to increase the consultation thresholds from £250 to £500 for qualifying works and from £100 to £200 for QLTAs. These figures will exclude any costs associated with professional fees. This uplift is broadly in line with inflation since the thresholds were last updated in 2003. This will make the consultation thresholds more proportionate and relevant to today’s economic landscape, and will go hand in hand with measures to rebalance the dispensation process.
We recognise that, particularly in large new-build blocks, higher thresholds may allow contracts of significant overall value to proceed without the same degree of statutory consultation and scrutiny. The increase will be implemented alongside reforms that rebalance the dispensation process, strengthening protections for leaseholders where landlords have not complied with the consultation requirements.

Question 134 (free text): Provide details.
There were 288 responses to this question.
The majority of respondents, across all stakeholder groups, agreed with the proposal to exempt energy contracts, as well as single utility providers, from the section 20 consultation process. A vast majority of respondents also agreed that, in doing so, there should be a mechanism whereby leaseholders are notified of relevant details, including the provider, total cost and length of agreement, and any connection between the landlord and broker or provider.
The majority of respondents, mainly leaseholders and leasehold representatives, did not consider that other activities should be removed from the section 20 process. However, some respondents, mainly freeholders and managing agents did consider more activities should be exempt from the section 20 consultation process. The main proposals for exemption were: emergency works, on the grounds that they should be exempt due to the urgent need to carry out works; other utility contracts, which are activities in markets which have fast-changing prices where consultation may impede obtaining value for money time; low value, routine works where consultation may not be necessary; building insurance and managing agent contracts.
The majority of respondents, mainly individual leaseholders, outlined negative experiences with QLTAs. Several respondents felt that they had no say, a lack of influence and limited engagement in the QLTA process. They cited a lack of transparency in the process, not being provided with relevant details, lack of accountability and no effective mechanism for review or challenge in the QLTA process. Some responses raised a lack of supervision and oversight from contract managers to hold contractors to account for poor quality work and services. Government response

There were 214 responses to this question.
There was strong support for requiring all individual managing agents in England to join a designated professional body and hold a professional qualification. The overwhelming majority of respondents were also supportive of requiring firms to take responsibility for ensuring their members achieve professional qualifications. They considered that this approach would help to deliver consistent standards, help to hold managing agents more accountable for their actions and protect leaseholders. Several respondents stated that many larger firms already belong to a professional body.
A few respondents who supported implementing proposals through existing bodies felt that these organisations would need to be reformed to demonstrate suitability to perform the role of designated professional body. Respondents who shared this view most frequently included comments that these organisations should have a clear objective to act in the public interest, or in the interest of leaseholders, while some respondents stated that the governance structures of existing organisations should be changed.
Most respondents considered that The Property Institute and the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors would be the existing professional bodies who may be able to perform the role of designated professional body. A small number of respondents mentioned other existing professional bodies, advisory bodies, ombudsmen and regulators, such as the Financial Conduct Authority.
Some leaseholder respondents disagreed with the designated professional body membership and qualification proposals as they thought that it would introduce additional costs to leaseholders. There were some concerns from leaseholders about involving existing professional bodies in the consultation proposals and they considered that an independent regulator should be responsible for implementing the proposals, principally on the grounds that professional bodies which operate both representative and regulatory functions present a conflict of interest, while other respondents mentioned that any designated professional body must be independent of industry.
Views from social housing providers were mixed. Some social housing providers expressed support for the proposals, but sought clarity about how the proposals would operate alongside the Competence and Conduct Standard for social housing or suggested that the proposals for leasehold blocks should mirror the Competence and Conduct Standard to avoid duplication. Some social housing providers disagreed with the proposals, including on the grounds that the portfolios of social housing providers differ from that of managers of leasehold blocks or that they should be exempted due to the existence of the Competence and Conduct Standard.
Some respondents suggested alternative approaches to the consultation proposals. Options included: Professional qualifications should only apply to individuals with significant control within a managing agent firm; Professional qualifications for managing agents should be mandatory, but membership of a professional body should not be; and managing agent firms should decide which of their staff should be required to hold a level 4 qualification.
Some respondents called for clarity about whether the proposals would apply to Right to Manage Companies and Resident Management Companies who manage buildings directly with views divided about whether qualifications should or should not apply in these cases, particularly amongst leaseholder respondents. Some respondents also called for clarity about how freeholders who self-manage buildings would be treated under the proposals, alongside calls for clarity about what is meant by the term “managing agent”.
Most respondents who answered the question of the conditions that designated professional bodies should be required to meet to be appointed suggested that designated professional bodies should be required to demonstrate independence, transparency, robust governance and sector expertise. These respondents also considered that designated professional bodies should be required to provide transparency around their governance structures, have a transparent complaint process and safeguards against conflicts of interest.
Many respondents thought that designated professional bodies would need additional support to perform the role. This included additional funding, more staff and new digital infrastructure to manage an increase in membership numbers, registrations, courses and compliance activity. A key theme amongst respondents was that designated professional bodies would need appropriate legal powers to address non-compliance. A small number of respondents thought that designated professional bodies would not require any additional support to perform the role. Some of these respondents thought that the skills and capacity required should already be in place.
On the question of enforcement for not joining a designated professional body or failing to obtain a qualification, more than half of respondents agreed with the approach. Some respondents considered that additional government funding would be necessary for them to enforce effectively. Several respondents who disagreed thought that local authorities would not have the appropriate resourcing or funding to carry out the proposed enforcement role.
Some respondents raised concerns that the proposed enforcement approach could lead to inconsistent enforcement outcomes across regions, particularly where local authority resources are stretched. Other respondents queried which local authority would be the lead authority for enforcement action where a firm or individual operates across multiple geographical areas.
A few respondents suggested alternative approaches to local authority led enforcement. Suggestions included: a national body should carry out enforcement action; a collaborative approach involving both local authorities and designated professional bodies; and that designated professional bodies should be solely responsible for enforcement action.
In terms of cost impacts, most respondents felt that mandatory professional qualifications would lead to some additional costs for managing agents. Specific cost drivers cited were the cost of completing training, obtaining qualifications, membership and putting in place systems to track compliance with requirements. Several respondents, including some leaseholders, viewed the additional costs of the approach as a necessary investment in improving standards and professionalism of some managing agents. Some respondents thought that the approach should help to prevent larger costs to leaseholders which arise from poor management and disputes. Other respondents, predominately managing agent firms, thought that the proposals would lead towards a profession of skilled staff and should reduce workforce costs associated with inefficiencies.
Some respondents thought that the level of cost would not be substantial for larger firms, principally on the grounds that many of these firms already belong to a professional body and employ level 4 qualified staff. Some respondents mentioned that the costs could be significant for smaller firms or firms with few qualified staff. A few respondents thought that the costs to managing agents should be minimal, or that it should not be substantive and the financial impact on managing agents should lessen over time. Some respondents stated that the level of costs that managing agents and how substantive those costs might be would depend on the final policy proposals, including which specific roles would be required to hold a professional qualification requirement and how many existing staff would need to gain a qualification to meet that requirement.
Several leaseholder respondents were concerned that qualification-related costs which managing agents face could be reflected in higher management fees or the overall service charge. Many leaseholders suggested that these costs should be absorbed by firms or subject to greater transparency.
Some respondents expressed concern that the introduction of mandatory qualifications could have an impact on recruitment and retention of staff.

Many leaseholder respondents felt strongly that any costs relating to qualifications should not be reflected in management fees paid by leaseholders. These respondents frequently mentioned that the costs associated with obtaining a qualification and CPD should be absorbed by firms. Some leaseholder respondents thought that any resultant increases in service charges should be capped or subject to greater transparency.
Some respondents, including some leaseholders, viewed the additional costs as a necessary investment in improvement standards, noting that the longer-term benefits would outweigh the costs over time. Of these respondents, some thought that costs savings could arise as leaseholders should see fewer issues with poor management due to higher standards for managing agents being in place. Some managing agent respondents thought that the cost investment would generate value for money improvements as competent and skilled employees should drive efficiencies and save money.
In respect of how qualification-related costs would be funded, a few managing agent respondents thought that some firms would be able to fund qualification costs through their training budgets or other expenditure. Some managing agent respondents thought that increases in leaseholders’ service charges should be small, particularly for large properties with many leaseholders. In contrast many leaseholder respondents felt that qualification costs should be funded through a firm’s income or profits and should not be reflected in service charges.
A few managing agent and social housing provider respondents mentioned that the additional costs which they could face should also be considered alongside the costs which they will face to meet other new and existing statutory obligations. These respondents noted that the combined cost of meeting these statutory obligations contributes towards service charge costs.
With respect to the proportion of managing agents and estate managers of freehold estates who already hold qualifications, some respondents mentioned that many managing agents within their firm already hold a level 3 or level 4 qualification, while some respondents stated that only a small number of managing agents at their organisation hold a level 3 or level 4 qualification.
Several respondents felt that qualifications alone will not improve standards of managing agents and additional measures are needed. These respondents stated that robust enforcement is needed, with fines for unprofessional conduct and permanent suspension of a registration for serious misconduct. Some respondents felt that an independent regulator is needed which should take forward all of the proposals in the Regulation of Property Agents Working Group report.
Some managing agent respondents expressed concern about the potential effects on recruitment and retention of staff, noting a potential for increased salary expectations amongst qualified individuals which would need to be factored into budgets, while other respondents noted that some unqualified staff may choose to leave their post and the potential for delays with backfilling roles if there is a shortage of qualified staff. A few respondents thought that experience should be considered as a substitute for qualifications and raised concerns that experienced professionals could leave their roles if this will not be case.
Some respondents had concerns about the ability of training providers to meet demand for qualifications. Some respondents also stated that training providers, alongside their course content and prices should be subject to independent oversight. A few respondents mentioned the importance of accessibility of courses and training. Some of these respondents thought that training or exams should be accessible nationwide and include online and part-time learning to account for the needs of a diverse workforce.
A few respondents thought that a public register of managing agents, qualifications, and enforcement decisions was needed.
Some respondents called for clarity about whether the consultation proposals would apply to those landlords who manage buildings directly, and who do not use a managing agent. Of these respondents, some thought that the consultation proposals should apply to landlords who carry out this role. A few respondents thought that the proposals should apply to leaseholders, in cases of leaseholder-managed buildings. This was on the grounds that leaseholders should be required to improve their technical knowledge to manage buildings. In contrast, many leaseholder respondents thought that the proposals should not apply to any leaseholder-managed buildings and mentioned that some leaseholders may step down from volunteer director roles if the proposals apply to those leaseholders.
There were also calls for clarity about, what requirements would apply to social housing providers with leasehold flats within their portfolio, and there were calls for clarity about whether registered providers of social housing who manage stock not owned by them would be treated as managing agents.
Government response
In Part 4 of the consultation, we sought views on a proposal for the government to require managing agents and estate managers of freehold estates in England to hold relevant qualifications. Alongside this, we sought views on wider options to strengthen regulation of managing agents to drive up the standards of their service. This included proposals for the government to require both individuals and companies in the managing agent sector to join a designated professional body and mandate that managing agents must complete CPD.
There was strong support for our qualification proposals for managing agents. Consultation responses demonstrate a broad consensus on improving professional standards, transparency and accountability across the property management sector as ways to protect leaseholders. We are grateful for the feedback received on strengthening regulation of managing agents and managing agent qualifications.
We have carefully reviewed our consultation proposals. We are of the view that regulation should go further to address the issues in the sector and to stamp out bad practice. We also think that regulation should not apply to managing agents alone and that there are significant issues in both the estate agent and letting agent sectors that should be addressed through regulation.
The government’s view is that, taking into account the recommendations of the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee’s pre-legislative scrutiny report for the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, strengthening regulation of managing agents to drive up the standard of their service now requires more than individual mandatory qualifications and membership of a designed professional body as was proposed in the consultation – though qualifications remain an important part of the picture.
The government therefore intends to bring forward future legislation to regulate property agents through an independent regulator. As a minimum this will include managing agents across leasehold and commonhold, estate agents, letting agents and estate managers on freehold estates. This will provide an opportunity to develop a coherent regime across the property agent sector.
The government will work through further the detail of these proposals including on the scope of regulation, and will engage with the sector, other government departments and consumer representatives as this work progresses.
We will set out further detail on our strategy for the regulation of property agents in due course, including on licensing, qualifications to operate and codes of practice.
Glossary of key terms
Appropriate tribunal or relevant tribunal
Appropriate tribunal or relevant tribunal refers to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber), or where determined by or under Tribunal Procedure Rules, the Upper Tribunal, in England, and the Leasehold Valuation Tribunal in Wales, which deal with settling disputes in relation to leasehold property, including service charge disputes and failure to provide information to leaseholders.
Asset Management Plan (AMP)
A written plan prepared and provided by the landlord to the leaseholder which assesses the condition of a property, forecasting future repairs and maintenance, and may project the costs of these works.
Dispensation
A landlord may apply to a court or a tribunal to avoid serving a notice under the major works procedure set out in at section 20 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. If they succeed, the court or tribunal issues a dispensation from serving that notice.
First-tier Tribunal
The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) deals with settling disputes in relation to leasehold property in England, including service charge disputes and failure to provide information to leaseholders.
Fixed service charge
A fixed service charge specifies the exact amount of service charge that leaseholders pay for the accounting period, for a specific list of services.
Head lessor
A landlord of an intermediate lease.
Intermediate landlord
A person who holds an ‘intermediate lease’. They hold a leasehold interest in, and in turn are a landlord under another lease, of all or part of the same property.
Landlord
The meaning of “landlord” depends on the context. In relation to service charges, it includes any person who has a right to enforce payment of a service charge.
A landlord may be either the freeholder of the property or may itself hold a leasehold interest in the property.
Lease
The legal document that allows the holder to occupy a property for a specific period of time. It contains the terms of the contractual arrangement, such as what landlord costs can be recharged to leaseholders via a service charge, any restrictions on the leaseholder’s ability to sublet or make alterations.
Leasehold
A form of property ownership which is time-limited, where control of the property is shared with, and limited by, the landlord.
Leasehold Valuation Tribunal
The Leasehold Valuation Tribunal deals with settling disputes in relation to leasehold property in Wales, including service charge disputes, lease variations and the determination of premiums for freehold purchase and lease extensions.
Leaseholder
A leaseholder is a person or company who owns a property subject to the terms of a written lease agreement, typically for 99, 125 or 999 years. The lease creates obligations which a leaseholder must comply with, including the obligation to pay the service charge.
Major works
“Qualifying works”* carried out to a building or other premises, such as decoration, repairs, improvements, or refurbishments, which are necessary to keep a block or estate in a satisfactory state of repair, which would cost each leaseholder either over £250 or, if a “qualifying long term agreement”* was entered into, over £100.
*As defined by s.20ZA(2) of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and regulation 6 of the Service Charge (Consultation Requirements)(England) Regulations 2003 and regulation 6 of the Service Charge (Consultation Requirements)(Wales) Regulations 2004.
Managing agent
An individual or company appointed to run and manage the building and services, or an estate, on behalf of a landlord or other party with rights to undertake management functions.
Reserve fund/sinking fund
A pool of money which is collected to cover the costs of future, one-off or major works needed, such as replacement of a lift or roof.
Resident Management Company
A company that is owned and run by leaseholders of a building containing flats which undertakes the management and maintenance of the building. A Resident Management Company is usually a party to the lease and their duties to leaseholders are set out in the lease. A Resident Management Company may or may not own the freehold.
Right to Manage Company
A company created by leaseholders of a building containing flats to take over the management of the building from the freeholder, without buying or owning the freehold.
Section 20
Section 20 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 sets out the consultation requirements that a landlord must follow for any qualifying works or QLTAs (major works).
Service charge*
A financial contribution payable by a leaseholder typically to a landlord, for a share of the cost of insuring, maintaining, repairing, cleaning, etc. the building.
Details of what can (and cannot) be charged by the landlord and the proportion of the charge to be paid by the individual leaseholder will all be set out in the lease.
*As defined by section 18(1) of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, as amended by the 2024 Act.
Social landlord
An organisation such as an organisation such as a local authority, a PRP in England, or registered social landlord in Wales, who provides housing. Most do not make a profit. Social landlords can rent their properties to social tenants. Leaseholders may have a social landlord if they have a shared ownership property or their property was previously owned by a social landlord.
Social housing tenant
For the purposes of this consultation, a tenant means someone who rents from a social landlord and is asked to pay a service charge.
Superior landlord
Where there are multiple leases or interests in a property, creating a chain of landlords and tenants, the superior landlord is usually the one who holds the highest interest in the chain.

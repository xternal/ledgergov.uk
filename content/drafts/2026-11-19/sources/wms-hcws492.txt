This government made a clear and unambiguous commitment in its manifesto to act where previous governments had failed and finally bring the feudal leasehold system to an end. We did so on the basis of a firm conviction that it is only by extinguishing fully the historical iniquities on which the present leasehold system rests that we can ensure that the dream of homeownership is made real for millions of households across the country.
The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill that we are introducing to Parliament today marks the beginning of the end for the feudal leasehold system and is integral to honouring our manifesto commitments in full.
The Bill was published in draft on 27 January 2026 for pre-legislative scrutiny by the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee. The government is extremely grateful to the Committee for producing its detailed pre-legislative scrutiny report on the draft Bill and we reiterate our thanks to all involved for the thorough scrutiny to which this complex and technical piece of legislation was subject.
The Bill’s key provisions remain unchanged from the draft Bill. They include the following:
A new legal framework for commonhold to reform and reinvigorate this radical improvement on leasehold ownership;
A statutory restriction on new leasehold flats to ensure that in future commonhold is the default tenure;
A new process for converting to commonhold aligned with wider enfranchisement processes to make conversion possible when at least 50% of qualifying leaseholders agree;
The abolition of leasehold forfeiture as a means of ensuring compliance with a lease agreement and its replacement with a fairer system;
The repeal of draconian powers relating to rentcharges on freehold estates; and
The capping of ground rent in existing leases at £250 per year, changing to a peppercorn after 40 years.
In addition, the Bill as introduced today includes measures to rectify specific flaws contained in the previous government’s Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. These include a loophole in the new valuation process which means that some people could avoid paying the correct price for their lease extension or freehold acquisition, thereby compromising the integrity of the amended method. We will also bring forward measures to fix the omission that would deny tens of thousands of shared ownership leaseholders the right to extend their lease with their direct landlord, given that the providers in question do not have sufficiently long leases to grant 990-year extensions.
The Bill also includes powers to cap permission fees and administration charges. No longer will leaseholders and homeowners on freehold estates be forced to pay exorbitant fees in order to secure their landlord’s permission for simple requests like keeping a pet or processing a change of mortgage provider. A public consultation will be launched on the scope of what should be capped and the amount for each item.
Furthermore, the Bill contains provisions to empower leaseholders to secure better digital connectivity in their buildings, while ensuring telecoms companies can engage with the right person to unlock deployment. These reforms aim to break down barriers to better broadband and support the government’s ambition for 99% of households to have access to gigabit-capable broadband by 2032.
In December 2025 we launched two comprehensive consultations seeking views on how best to implement new consumer protections for homeowners on freehold estates (link) and the ways in which we might reduce the prevalence of privately managed estates over the coming years (link). We continue to analyse the extensive feedback received to both.
However, to provide homeowners on freehold estates with better support we have supplemented the provisions in the Bill relating to rentcharges with powers to allow the government to fund free, independent advice to help them understand their rights and how to challenge unfair charges. This offer currently exists for leaseholders and commonholders through the government-funded Leasehold Advisory Service.
Pre-legislative scrutiny
The Bill has been significantly strengthened as a result of the work of the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee. As the formal response (link) to its pre-legislative scrutiny report that we have published alongside the Bill makes clear, we have considered carefully each of the Committee’s recommendations. In many cases, we have accepted them in full or in part. Where we believe individual recommendations are either unnecessary or unworkable, we have set out why that is the case.
In particular, I draw the House’s attention to our stated aim of bringing the £250 ground rent cap into force six months after Royal Assent. We have also taken the opportunity to reiterate our previously stated commitment to enact through legislation over the course of this Parliament remaining Law Commission recommendations on enfranchisement and the right to manage.
Banning new leasehold flats
Alongside the introduction of the Bill, the government has also published a response to our Moving to Commonhold consultation (link). I am grateful to all those individuals and organisations who took the time to submit their views on the practical implications of a ban. The feedback received has helped shape the clear and workable legal framework for banning the use of leasehold for new flats that is included in the Bill.
We want to ensure that as many people as possible benefit from our reforms. That is why we have determined that the ban will cover a wide range of flatted properties, including new build flats, conversions of buildings to flats, and repurposed buildings. On the basis of the evidence submitted, we have made some limited adaptations to commonhold to tailor its use to smaller blocks and some forms of affordable and older people’s housing. Recognising that there are limited circumstances where leasehold ownership structures remain necessary or where legal or practical barriers make the use of commonhold unworkable, we have also provided a limited number of exemptions to the ban.
Our aim has always been to facilitate a smooth and effective transition away from leasehold. To ensure that the move to commonhold for new flats does not cause harmful disruption, we have decided to provide a two-and-a-half-year transitional period between the new commonhold regime being available for use and the flat ban coming into effect as well as an exemption for pre-commencement planning applications and consents to safeguard the delivery of live sites and the immediate pipeline of new supply.
We recognise that the transition from leasehold to commonhold being the default tenure involves significant change for consumers and industry. That is why we are also publishing today a Direction of Travel for commonhold implementation. This document (link) sets out our initial plans to work with partners before and after the Bill receives Royal Assent to ensure a successful and sustainable transition to commonhold as the default tenure for new flats. We will also establish a Commonhold Implementation Steering Group, which I will chair. This Steering Group will bring together consumers and industry to inform a more detailed Implementation Roadmap that government will publish closer to Royal Assent of the Bill.
Strengthening leaseholder protections over charges and services
The government has also today published a response (link) to the further reforms outside the scope of the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 that were set out in the ‘Strengthening leaseholder protections over charges and services’ consultation undertaken last year. Those further reforms included mandating reserve funds, introducing mandatory qualifications for managing agents and improving the section 20 major works regime.
We received a large number of detailed and thoughtful responses to these proposals, and we thank every individual and organisation that took the time to submit their views. Having considered carefully the feedback received, we have decided to proceed with a number of the proposed measures.
We will legislate to make reserve funds mandatory with a view to reducing the risk of large, unaffordable one-off bills for leaseholders and to support the timely delivery of important works. To provide leaseholders with greater transparency over the work that reserve funds will pay for and to drive longer-term strategic planning, we will also legislate to mandate Asset Management Plans. We will also reform the outdated major works process, including by making improvements to consultation procedures and restricting the grounds upon which landlords may seek dispensation from the requirement to consult leaseholders.
Finally, when it comes to managing agents, we have made clear that we will go beyond the proposals set out in the consultation with a commitment to legislate to regulate all property agents through an independent statutory regulator. As a minimum this will cover managing agents across leasehold and commonhold, estate agents, letting agents and estate managers on freehold estates.
As detailed in our formal response to the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee’s pre-legislative scrutiny report, we do not consider that the Bill is the right vehicle by which to achieve this. The regulation of property agents is a significant reform, requiring a detailed legislative framework. Attempting to add such provisions to this Bill could risk delaying the Bill and its core leasehold and commonhold reforms, while also limiting the opportunity to develop a coherent regime across the property agent sector as a whole and missing the opportunity to tackle issues in the estate and letting agent sectors. We will set out further detail on the government’s strategy for the regulation of property agents in due course, including on licensing, qualifications and codes of practice.

Men's Health Strategy: one year on report
Ministerial foreword
England’s first Men’s Health Strategy, introduced last November, put men and boys, and the families and communities around them, at the heart of change. A year ago, we set 3 ambitions: more responsive services, better support for healthier choices and creating conditions in which men and boys can thrive. That means recognising demanding working conditions that make accessing support harder, helping boys navigate difficult emotions and strengthening families’ ability to support one another.
Better men’s health means more time with loved ones, stronger families and communities, and a stronger economy, which benefits us all.
The need remains stark. Men in England’s most deprived areas can expect to live over 10 years less, with more than 19 fewer healthy years, than those in the least deprived areas. One year on, the commitments we made are becoming action. This report records progress, while recognising lasting improvements demand sustained effort - out in the real world, not online or buried in spreadsheets.
Delivery means reaching people in settings they know and trust. The Men’s Health Community Fund embodies that approach. Launched in association with Movember and the People’s Health Trust, the fund commits up to £6.3 million towards community initiatives, particularly for men underserved by traditional services. Programmes begin in early 2027, with rigorous evaluation in place to identify what works and share learning.
New local implementation guidance will also support practical action. A measurement framework in 2027 and a 5-year review will strengthen accountability and identify where further progress is needed.
There are already achievements to build on. Rugby league pilots in Wigan and Wakefield engaged 1,300 participants. Evaluation suggested improved levels of connection and belonging, highlighting strong local partnerships. By September 2026, over 25,000 practitioners have passed national Staying Safe from Suicide training, strengthening compassionate, consistent support. These are exactly the kind of outcomes we want to see.
Reducing inequalities remains the litmus test of progress. We have committed up to £3.6 million for locally led Suicide Prevention Pathfinders, improving access to support for middle-aged men. The 3-year £93.3 million gambling harm prevention programme is advancing, supporting voluntary organisations and local authorities to deliver prevention and help people affected by gambling. A sports-led campaign reached over 6.5 million people, promoting social connection and local support. Father-inclusive guidance and a professionals’ network covering all 153 local authorities are strengthening support for fathers.
One of the innovations I am most looking forward to is the appointment of England’s first Men’s Health Ambassador. It’s so important that we personalise the strategy and have someone who can articulate its aims and inspire others to get onboard. This is about visible national leadership to champion men’s health, galvanise action across sectors and help drive better outcomes. Whoever gets the job, I can’t wait to work with them to lift more men and boys out of ill health.
Of course, government cannot deliver this alone. We believe in the power of experts and those with lived experience. Our Academic Network and Stakeholder Group brings both together to drive delivery. Their insights help us address evidence gaps and design support around people’s needs.
Our partnership on the Premier League’s Together Against Suicide initiative with Samaritans shows the potential. This season, all 20 Premier League clubs offer tailored support environments on home matchdays.
Not every man and boy is a football fanatic of course. Workplaces, faith groups and community settings matter too. Support must reach beyond sport to all locations where men and boys connect and converse.
These are foundations, not the finish line. Together, we must turn progress into longer, happier, healthier lives for men and boys.
James Frith
Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for Health Innovation
Men’s Health Academic Network Chair foreword
One year ago, England’s first Men’s Health Strategy marked an important beginning. I described it as the start of a longer journey. It put men’s health on the national agenda, with a 10-year commitment to act earlier, improve the conditions that shape men’s health, and make it part of everyday decision-making. This report looks at how that ambition is taking shape, what has changed, what we are learning and where we go next.
In North Yorkshire, where I live and work, the strategy is helping lay the groundwork for long-term action. Men’s and women’s health colleagues are working together to understand how gender shapes health and how people experience services and bring this into local planning. I have seen a similar picture across the country. Local areas are building men’s health into existing priorities, guided by community needs. The local implementation guidance will help turn ambition into action and the planned measurement framework will help assess our progress.
National investment is opening up opportunities too. The £6.3 million Men’s Health Community Fund brings government and charitable partners together to support community-led work with underserved men. Father-inclusive practice guidance and support are in place. Preparations for the Suicide Prevention Pathfinders are moving forward. Together, these initiatives are putting the strategy into practice, reaching men where they are.
But we need to understand what works, where and why, so we can adapt effective approaches elsewhere. We must also listen to men who did not take up support or dropped out, as well as those who benefited.
At the heart of this work are the men our systems and services serve least well. I am proud that the strategy looks beyond individual choices. It recognises that health is shaped by where men live, their work, relationships and experience of services. Encouraging men to seek help matters. But help must be within reach. Awareness can only do so much if seeking support means losing income, being treated differently at work or finding that services do not meet their needs.
As implementation gathers pace, we must not lose sight of the wider conditions that shape men’s health. Helping men look after their health must go hand in hand with improving the conditions in which they live and work. Gender is part of that picture. It can influence people’s experiences of work, services and relationships. Understanding this can help us reduce inequalities, rather than accept them as inevitable. Working together, men’s and women’s health colleagues can bring this understanding in policy, planning and services.
As chair of the Men’s Health Academic Network, I look forward to continuing this work with colleagues. We will bring independent advice on what the evidence supports, where we need to learn more and what that means for future action. That means being open about uncertainty and ready to change course as we learn.
One year on, we have a stronger foundation for sustained action. This report is an invitation to build on it together. As you read on, I hope you will see opportunities to help men get support earlier, receive more responsive care and develop healthier living and working conditions. Above all, success must mean closing the gaps in health and ensuring that every man has the opportunity to live a longer, healthier life.
Paul Galdas
Chair, Men’s Health Academic Network
Executive summary
One year ago, we published England’s first ever Men’s Health Strategy. It marked a major milestone: the first time a government in the G7 had set out a dedicated vision to improve the health and wellbeing of men and boys across the country.
The strategy was built around 3 ambitions:
ensuring health services engage men and boys and are responsive to their needs
building structures which empower men and boys to maximise their own health and wellbeing
creating the conditions in which men and boys’ health and wellbeing can thrive
The need for action was (and remains) clear. Too many men experience preventable ill health, spend fewer years in good health and die too young. These challenges are often greatest in the communities facing the highest levels of deprivation and disadvantage.
In 2025, 35% of all deaths among men in England were before the age of 75. On average, men in the most deprived areas of England are likely to die over 10 years earlier and live over 19 fewer years in good health than men in the least deprived areas. Men also accounted for 3 out of 4 people who died by suicide in 2025 in England, with suicide remaining one of the leading causes of death among men under 50.
The strategy aimed to tackle these challenges head on and set out our commitment to working in partnership over the long term to do so. Over the past year, we have worked collaboratively with partners across government, the NHS, local authorities, employers, industry, academia and the voluntary sector to begin turning the strategy’s ambitions into action. Together, we have:
strengthened partnerships, including working with the Premier League to enhance their Together Against Suicide initiative with Samaritans
improved our understanding of men’s health needs, including working with MHAN to review the evidence on how young men engage with health and wellbeing content online
made progress on a range of commitments to support better outcomes, including on the new gambling harms prevention programme for England and Suicide Prevention Pathfinders programme
set out how we will work over the coming years on our shared priorities, including outlining how we will work with MHAN to understand and address evidence gaps over the next 5 years
We have also built our partnership with Movember and the People’s Health Trust to deliver the Men’s Health Community Fund (MHCF), a £6.3 million programme designed to improve men’s health and wellbeing through community-led initiatives, particularly for men underserved through traditional systems.
We have always been clear that publishing a strategy was only the beginning. If we are to achieve lasting, systemic change, we must focus on delivery, develop the evidence base and build sustainable progress. Strong leadership and accountability at every level will be critical to this.
That is why, alongside this report, we are launching the recruitment for a new Men’s Health Ambassador, who will provide national leadership, champion men’s health across sectors and help drive collective action to improve outcomes for men and boys.
The ambassador will provide visible, national leadership on men’s health, working alongside Ministers and the chair of our Men’s Health Academic Network (MHAN) to turn the ambitions of the Men’s Health Strategy into sustained action across government, the health system and wider society.
Alongside this report, we have also published new guidance developed with local delivery partners, and designed to empower local and regional partners, to translate the ambitions of the strategy into local delivery. The guidance aims to ensure that men’s health is a core part of how we tackle inequalities and build healthier communities for everyone, wherever they live. The guidance calls on local leaders to champion the issue, provide senior ownership and accountability, and create the motivation and conditions for action across their organisations and communities.
We are also committing to developing and publishing a measurement framework in 2027 to monitor progress across the system, helping local, regional and national partners track improvements and identify where we need to go further.
We remain committed to working transparently alongside our partners in the Men’s Health Stakeholder Group and MHAN to track progress on each of the strategy’s commitments and to work together to push the agenda forward. We will undertake a 5-year review to consider progress against the strategy’s ambitions, examine what has worked and identify priorities for the second half of the strategy’s implementation. This report includes an annex which sets out progress across all 45 actions within the strategy.
These actions strengthen the foundations of leadership, accountability and delivery, and build on the progress made since the strategy’s publication. This report highlights those achievements, acknowledging work across the system alongside action being taken across the whole of government that are together designed to improve outcomes for men and boys. It also identifies a number of opportunities to go further, based on what we have heard from stakeholders and experts since the strategy was published.
The Men’s Health Strategy sits alongside this government’s broader ambition to improve health, wellbeing and opportunity for everyone. Across government, we are working to ensure policies are responsive to the different needs and experiences of women and men. The publication of the refreshed Women’s Health Strategy provides a renewed opportunity to strengthen collaboration across men’s and women’s health and accelerate progress on shared challenges. In July 2026, the chair of MHAN, Professor Paul Galdas, and the Women’s Health Ambassador, Dame Lesley Regan, issued a joint publication highlighting how the men’s and women’s health strategies can reinforce one another and support wider health system reform.
The Men’s Health Strategy also forms part of a broader cross-government commitment to improve outcomes for men and boys. This work, led by the Lord Chancellor and Secretary of State for Justice, Alex Norris MP, alongside Minister Jake Richards MP, is about building a positive, ambitious agenda to tackle the challenges many face across health, education, employment and social connection.
A national men and boys summit, led by the UK government, will take place early next year bringing together important voices working to improve outcomes for men and boys across a number of different areas and driving the next phase of action.
Internationally, we are strengthening England’s leadership on men’s health by continuing to engage and learn from international partners, share our own learning and best practice, and build momentum behind men’s health as a distinct and core policy area.
One year on from the strategy’s publication, momentum continues to build across government and beyond. We are grateful to organisations across England whose continued efforts are helping to reach and support men and boys and drive progress towards our shared goals. The next phase is to build on these foundations to deliver lasting improvements in men’s experiences of care and health outcomes, particularly for those who face the greatest disadvantage. Making progress requires that men currently least well served receive appropriate support and see improvements in their health. This means continuing to work together, share learning and build the evidence on what works, while embedding men’s health within mainstream services (both public and private) and into wider government action so that every man and boy has the opportunity to live a longer, healthier and more fulfilling life.
Introduction
This report provides an overview of progress against the actions within the Men’s Health Strategy, as well as broader government progress relevant to improving health outcomes for men and boys. The report also highlights actions across the wider system which are helping to drive progress for men and boys. Across these areas, we also consider how services and the wider conditions in which men live and work shape their opportunities for good health, and where action is needed to reduce inequalities.
The report also identifies a number of opportunities to go further, informed by what we have learned from the developing research evidence and heard from stakeholders and people with lived experience. These priorities have been considered based on the same principles used to shape the original strategy:
focusing on areas with the greatest need
addressing gaps where men and boys are currently underserved
prioritising areas where government can have the greatest impact
This report should be read alongside the Men’s Health Strategy.
Improving access to healthcare services
Progress on our commitments
The Men’s Health Strategy recognises that:
men face a range of barriers to accessing healthcare services
many services aren’t reaching and retaining men as effectively as they could
Evidence suggests that trusted community settings can play an important role in reaching men and encouraging engagement. However important gaps remain in understanding which approaches work best, for whom and in what circumstances.
In March 2026, the Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) launched the MHCF in partnership with Movember and People’s Health Trust. Together, the 3 partners committed up to £6.3 million to support community-led initiatives designed to improve men’s health and wellbeing across the country, particularly those underserved by formal systems, and build the evidence on effective approaches to engaging men in non-clinical settings.
Across the MHCF, grants will be issued to support:
young men aged 16 to 35, with a focus on social connection, mental health, early fatherhood and wellbeing
men aged 35 to 59 who are unemployed, economically inactive or experiencing economic precarity
men working in industry and men in male-dominated industries, for example construction, who are traditionally underserved by existing health systems
older men aged 60 and above experiencing a life transition such as retirement, bereavement, relationship breakdown or caring responsibilities
Programmes will begin in early 2027. Through the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), the research arm of DHSC, we will fund an evaluation of the programme to strengthen the evidence base on community-based approaches to improve men’s health and wellbeing, particularly for underserved men, with findings used to support future policy and practice.
The government has also been working with the Premier League to tackle male suicide, joining up with and amplifying the Premier League’s Together Against Suicide initiative with Samaritans. Last season (2025 to 2026), the initiative launched with the aim to raise awareness of suicide prevention support. This involved a pilot project launch involving 11 professional football clubs, who provided touchpoints on matchdays where fans could engage with appropriate support services. This initiative launch demonstrated the power of reaching men through the communities and institutions they know and trust.
This season (2026 to 2027), the programme is expanding significantly, with all 20 Premier League clubs offering tailored and bespoke environments to engage fans on home matchdays. The partnership between DHSC and the Premier League is strengthening and enhancing the initiative by:
helping clubs to engage effectively with fans
signposting to NHS resources
working more closely with NHS local health services so appropriate expert and clinical support are readily available
Important progress has already been made. This includes:
producing bespoke suicide prevention guidance for Premier League staff and fans, drawing on the NHS Staying Safe from Suicide guidance
supporting closer collaboration between clubs and local health systems
raising awareness of the mental health support available to fans through NHS 111 and the wider support available through the Together Against Suicide refreshed online hub
We are committed to building on this momentum and strengthening this partnership, recognising that effective support reaches men where they are, at times that work for them, and through the places, activities and communities that matter most in their lives. By championing what works, sharing learning and scaling successful approaches, we can help ensure that more men receive timely, meaningful support, while continuing to break down stigma and encouraging people to seek help when they need it most.
Through both of our partnerships, we have embedded a focus on health literacy improvements. Through community-based intervention, MHCF aims to encourage improved confidence in seeking help and stronger ongoing engagement with health support and services among men who are underserved, while seeking to understand the system and service changes needed to best support men’s health. Through the evaluation we will build the evidence base on what works. Our partnership on the Premier League’s Together Against Suicide initiative is improving health literacy by increasing awareness of suicide prevention, signposting men to NHS and other support, and helping fans understand how and where to seek help when they need it.
To support our commitment to reach men in ways, times and places that they want we have worked with the Men’s Health Stakeholder Group to develop and publish a men’s health resource hub (log in required). The hub provides a space for organisations and practitioners to share practical learning, case studies and resources. This will help them to develop more accessible and responsive support for men and to learn from each other. Information on the hub has been compiled with help from trusted sources including our Men’s Health Stakeholder Group and MHAN. The hub will take an iterative approach, intended to develop and grow over time, in response to use and feedback.
Work is also underway to develop a new e-learning module for professionals from all staffing groups. This is being designed to improve understanding of men’s health needs, support better engagement with men, and contribute to improved health outcomes and experiences of care. Early engagement has taken place with our Men’s Health Stakeholder Group and MHAN to inform the development of this. We will continue to work with men with lived experience and relevant experts as this is refined, shared more widely and used.
As set out in the government’s 10 Year Health Plan, the shift from analogue to digital offers an important opportunity to make healthcare more accessible and responsive to men’s needs. Progress is being made on the HomeTest programme, which will support individuals on prostate cancer active monitoring pathways to order and complete prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood tests at home. In July 2026, national clinical leaders endorsed a digital-first model that supports testing closer to home, with Royal Devon University Hospital NHS Foundation Trust appointed as the programme’s pathology partner.
Elsewhere, we are expanding access to evidence-based digital health technologies that can help people manage their health and access support more easily. NHS HealthStore is a proposed NHS digital marketplace, introduced in the 10 Year Health Plan, that will enable patients to access approved digital health technologies through the NHS App to help manage or treat their conditions. Following successful early testing of the NHS HealthStore on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the programme is being expanded to cover cardiac rehabilitation and insomnia, with mental health (anxiety and depression) and musculoskeletal disease planned to follow shortly after. These conditions all impact on men’s poor health outcomes (log in required). Digital approaches that support rehabilitation, psychological treatment and supported self-management have the potential to improve health outcomes and provide alternative ways of accessing care.
Improving engagement with healthcare also means understanding how men find, assess and act on online health information. Through MHAN, we have invited Professor Brendan Gough to lead work to produce an evidence note, drawing on academic and sector expertise, to explore what is known about how young men encounter, interpret and act on online content relevant to health and wellbeing. This includes content shaped by masculinity narratives. The review will strengthen our understanding of how digital channels interact with masculinity to influence men’s health behaviours, seeking help, health literacy and decision-making.
Building on this work, we intend to convene an expert group in 2027 to consider the findings and develop recommendations on how government and other sectors can more effectively reach men with positive, evidence-based health information through the channels they already use and trust.
Wider action
The progress we have made through the Men’s Health Strategy is part of the government’s broader ambitions to make health and care more accessible, ensuring people can get the right support, in the right place, at the right time.
In March 2026, we published a neighbourhood health framework to empower local leaders to develop and scale neighbourhood health. The neighbourhood health approach is about improving health outcomes and reducing health inequalities through proactive, preventative and personalised care, delivered closer to home and shaped around the needs of local populations. This includes ensuring that support reflects individual needs - including the different needs of men and women - and how people access care.
The framework is designed to provide clarity and consistency and support joined up working between integrated care boards (ICBs) and local authorities, as they work through health and wellbeing boards and with their partners to develop locally led neighbourhood health plans. These tailored plans aim to improve the health of local populations and reduce health inequalities, including tackling the issues faced by men in accessing health and care services.
We have announced wave 1 of neighbourhood health centre schemes, with 27 sites across England selected to bring care closer to home 12 hours a day, 6 days a week - backed by £50 million. These initial sites will lay the foundation for national rollout, as we work towards delivering 120 neighbourhood health centres across England by 2030.
Further guidance on how men’s health can be integrated with neighbourhood health plans is outlined in the Men’s Health Strategy: guidance for local implementation.
Building on the strategy
The Men’s Health Strategy takes a life course approach, recognising that support must reflect men’s changing needs and experiences at different life stages. We are committed to working across government and the health system to ensure that the needs of men and boys are reflected in policies, services and guidance at all life stages. This includes:
childhood and adolescence
pre-conception and fatherhood
working age and midlife
healthy ageing, frailty and end of life care
We will also explore how existing touchpoints can be used to:
identify men’s needs earlier
provide timely advice and support
strengthen connections between services
As a first step, we will explore opportunities to embed men’s health considerations within Making Every Contact Count (MECC). MECC is a practical approach that equips the broader health and care workforce with the skills, knowledge and confidence to support healthy conversations and behaviour change, contributing to prevention, health equity and improved population health outcomes. This will help to ensure that whenever men come into contact with services, opportunities are used to support prevention, identify unmet needs and connect men with the right support.
Supporting individual behaviours
Progress on our commitments
Many of the leading causes of poor health and premature death among men are associated with preventable or modifiable risk factors. Supporting men to make healthier choices is therefore an important part of improving men’s health outcomes and reducing inequalities.
Gambling-related harms are disproportionately experienced by men, and men are also far more likely to gamble online which is associated with greater harm. Significant progress has been made to deliver the new gambling-harm prevention programme for England. This 3-year £93.3 million transition programme is being delivered under the statutory gambling levy. This includes delivering almost £40 million in grant funding for 40 voluntary sector organisations over 2 years. Organisations are now more than halfway through their first year of delivering gambling-harm prevention activities across England. Through the grant, we have funded a range of prevention interventions such as awareness raising activity, education, campaigns, workforce training and non-clinical recovery, as well as other forms of support for people who gamble and for others who are affected.
As part of the programme, we are also delivering £24 million in grant funding over 2 years for all upper tier local authorities. A new data portal is being introduced which will enable local authorities to share progress on gambling harms prevention activity, outcomes and performance.
Development of a new digital service to help prevent gambling-related harms is also progressing. By April 2028, DHSC intends to launch a national campaign to raise awareness of the harms and risks associated with gambling. Campaign development is underway, informed by emerging evidence from the NIHR Policy Research Unit in Behavioural and Social Sciences on public health messaging for gambling-related harms.
This activity is supported by ongoing work to strengthen the evidence base on what works to prevent and reduce gambling-related harms. Work is underway to establish an independent evaluation of the prevention programme through NIHR. The UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) research programme on gambling has also made a number of investments in its initial phase, including the flagship Gambling Harms Research UK Evidence Centre and a suite of research and innovation partnerships which will deliver research on a range of topics related to gambling harms. The programme will continue to commission research that strengthens the evidence base and builds capacity in the research and innovation sector to address gambling harms. UKRI is working closely with the Department for Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS) and other Gambling Levy commissioners to ensure that the research findings are translated effectively into prevention, treatment and policy.
DHSC has also been developing an intervention to reduce cocaine-related cardiovascular deaths among people, predominantly men, whose simultaneous use of alcohol and cocaine can substantially increase the risk of immediate death. Most people in this group are unlikely to be in contact with alcohol and drug services or aware of the risk. Development and piloting of the intervention in 2 hospital alcohol care teams have now been completed. Follow-up findings suggest that, in a significant number of cases, recipients reduced or stopped using alcohol and cocaine simultaneously. Work has now begun to refine the intervention, staff training and patient information materials, alongside advice on implementation and targeting. Once complete, DHSC will promote this package for widespread use in healthcare settings.
The Department for Transport (DfT) launched a new THINK! awareness campaign in November 2025 to raise awareness of the risks and consequences of driving on drugs. The campaign is predominately aimed at young male drivers who are particularly at risk. DfT figures show that around 90% of drivers impaired by drugs in collisions are male, with 4 in 10 aged between 17 and 30. The launch of the campaign has delivered positive initial results, with increases in those recognising they could get a criminal record for drug driving and an increase in those choosing to take a different course of action rather than drug drive. THINK! will continue to run and evolve the campaign through to 2027 to continue raising awareness of the risks drug drivers pose to themselves and others.
Wider action
The government has also made significant progress on its ambitions for a smoke-free UK, with the landmark Tobacco and Vapes Act being granted Royal Assent in April 2026. Smoking rates have been consistently higher among men than women (11.1% compared to 8.0% in 2025), although rates for both men and women have fallen over recent years. The act will mean that anyone born on or after 1 January 2009 cannot legally be sold tobacco products. This will come into force from 1 January 2027. The government is now focused on implementation of the act, including through:
publishing supporting guidance for retailer and enforcement bodies
engagement with stakeholders
launching a national public awareness campaign on 16 November 2026
On 29 October 2026, new laws were introduced including:
age of sale restrictions for all vape and nicotine products such as nicotine pouches, pearls and strips and non-nicotine vapes
banning the free distribution of these products
banning vape and nicotine product vending machines
introducing new fixed penalty notices
The act also enables the government to stop vapes and nicotine products from being advertised and branded in a way that is appealing to children. In July 2026, the government published a consultation on the packaging, device appearance and display of vapes and other nicotine products to stop the next generation from becoming hooked on nicotine. The consultation closed on 2 October 2026. We will set out our final plans for future regulations following analysis of the responses received. We also intend to ban the advertising and sponsorship of vapes and nicotine products from 1 June 2027.
The Men’s Health Strategy recognises that men are the primary perpetrators of violence against women but can also be victims. It recognises that norms relating to superiority, power and entitlement can be risk factors for men and boys perpetrating Violence Against Women and Girls (VAWG), highlighting the importance of engaging men and boys in prevention as well as ensuring all victims can access support. This sits alongside the government’s Freedom from violence and abuse strategy, published in December 2025. The strategy recognises the gendered nature of VAWG while acknowledging that men and boys can also experience domestic abuse and sexual violence, as outlined in the strategy’s men and boys’ explanatory note.
From April 2026, DHSC began rolling out Steps to Safety, a referral pathway from general practice into independent specialist support services for domestic abuse and sexual violence, including for male victims. Taking a public health approach, the pathway can also support adults and young people displaying harmful behaviours, helping to prevent escalation and reduce future harm. Up to £34.4 million over 3 years will support the first phase across 12 ICBs in England, alongside a further £15 million over the next 3 years to boost support to victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence in all ICB areas in England.
As part of the Secretary of State’s personal commitment to tackling VAWG, DHSC is building on the VAWG Strategy by launching a targeted consultation on a national framework for a public health approach to VAWG, covering prevention, early identification, support and recovery. A new national clinical lead for VAWG will also be appointed to support a stronger, joined-up health response.
DHSC is also investing up to £50 million over 3 years to establish a Child House in every NHS region by 2029, with the first services expected in 2027 to 2028. Child House is an internationally recognised model for providing holistic, multi-agency support to victims and survivors of child sexual abuse. This will help meet the Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse recommendation that male and female child victims are guaranteed specialist, accredited therapeutic support, recognising that boys may face particular barriers to disclosure and accessing help.
The Crime and Policing Act 2026 introduces a new statutory duty to report child sexual abuse. While the policy is Home Office-led, DHSC is engaging with Home Office colleagues because of the implications for healthcare professionals and services. A new mandatory safeguarding learning programme for the NHS workforce will launch later in 2026 to strengthen recognition of domestic abuse and responses to all victims.
Building on the strategy
Many of the behaviours that contribute to poorer health outcomes among men are shaped by wider social norms and expectations. Through our work to improve understanding of the relationship between societal norms, masculinity and men’s health outcomes, we will continue to draw on the expertise of our MHAN to identify and interpret relevant evidence on the factors that influence risk-taking behaviours and to consider how prevention activity can better engage men. We will use this evidence to inform future policy and ensure that the specific issues relevant to men are considered and addressed.
Developing healthy living and working conditions
Progress on our commitments
Sir Charlie Mayfield’s independent report, published in November 2025, highlighted the scale of the problem of people falling out of work for reasons of ill-health and disability, and the vital role employers can play in addressing this.
In the strategy we made a commitment to work with EDF to explore ways to help employers support men’s health and keep people in work. EDF’s 2027 campaigns will focus on some of their employees’ main health risks, which include stress, heart health and musculoskeletal conditions, and the impact these have on mental health. EDF are committed to focusing on men’s health within these topics by raising awareness and challenging stigma to help more people recognise risks early, seek support when they need it and take positive steps towards better long-term health.
Healthy living conditions include supporting men and boys to navigate an increasingly digital world safely and confidently. Earlier this year, the government launched the Kids Online Safety hub as well as running the successful You Won’t Know Until You Ask pilot campaign. The campaign evaluation found broadly similar campaign recognition among male and female parents. Together, the hub and campaign help parents and carers support their children’s critical thinking and resilience, and promote safe online habits. Tens of thousands of parents and carers have already accessed support through the hub, showing strong demand for this type of support.
As set out in the Improving access to healthcare services section, we have invited MHAN to produce an evidence note examining how narratives on health and wellbeing are shaped and shared in digital environments. The note will explore the role of social media and other online platforms in shaping health-related attitudes, behaviours and decision-making, helping to strengthen the evidence base on the opportunities, risks and influences that exist within young men’s digital lives, and inform future policies.
Wider action
The Prime Minister has pledged to stop accepting rough sleeping as a fact of life and unite the country behind a mission to end it. Rough sleeping impacted almost 4,000 men in England in 2025. Poor health can contribute to homelessness and rough sleeping, while sleeping rough can worsen existing conditions, create new health risks and make it harder for people to rebuild their lives. Health and care therefore have a vital role to play in ending rough sleeping. We are working across government, with the NHS and local partners, to prevent people becoming homeless in the first place, including:
ensuring people are not discharged from health and care services onto the street
improving access to treatment, care and support for people already sleeping rough
helping people recover, move into safe and stable accommodation, and sustain it over the long term
We will ensure that services recognise and respond to the different needs and experiences of people experiencing homelessness and rough sleeping, including through gender-responsive approaches.
Too many young men are being held back from opportunities to learn and earn. The Men’s Health Strategy is part of how we bring together health, education and welfare reforms to tackle the causes of disengagement, and support more young men to thrive. Young men aged 16 to 24 are more likely to be not in education, employment or training (NEET), which can have lasting consequences for both their health and economic prospects. The Young people and work review led by Alan Milburn and how the government responds is an important opportunity to rethink how government supports young people at risk of disengagement, including how health services can help them so they can successfully participate in education, employment and training.
With many men spending a significant proportion of their lives at work, workplaces can provide valuable opportunities to initiate and support healthier behaviours and improve access to health information and services.

Case study: People’s Health Trust’s work with the construction industry
Through its report Men’s Health: Everybody’s Business, and subsequent parliamentary roundtable hosted by Mike Reader MP, including construction companies and DHSC, People’s Health Trust highlighted mental health as men’s number one concern. This was driven largely by debt, housing issues and a lack of good work.
For men working in construction, the issues can be acute with on average 10 deaths by suicide per week in England and Wales. The trust is working with a leading national construction company to address the physical and mental health issues which are faced by men in the construction sector. Recognising that men in construction tend to have low uptake of employer wellbeing offers and are generally more wary of employer-led initiatives, this partnership builds directly on their experiences to design initiatives that these men want to attend, reduce stigma and support the areas men feel are most relevant to them.
The service will be offered in community settings and will place emphasis on prevention and early intervention. It will aim to build ongoing relationships with men and support them to link up with statutory services. As part of the MHCF, it will evaluate how co-production methods and community organisations can be highly effective means of supporting men who are distant from statutory and other health services.

Addressing societal norms
Progress on our commitments
Societal norms, shaped by institutions such as workplaces, media and services, influence how men understand masculinity and make decisions about their health and wellbeing. While some norms can support positive outcomes, others, such as expectations to appear strong, self-reliant and invulnerable, can discourage seeking help and may contribute to poorer mental health and suicide risk. These expectations can also shape how services and professionals recognise and respond to men’s needs, and whether distress is identified and appropriate support is offered.
As set out in the Improving access to healthcare services section, we have invited Professor Brendan Gough to lead work to produce an evidence note examining how narratives on health and wellbeing are shaped and shared in digital environments. The evidence note will identify what the current evidence can tell us about the relationship between societal norms, masculinity and men’s health outcomes, strengthening our understanding of how online content and media narratives can influence health behaviours, attitudes and decision-making.
We will continue to work across government to develop and coordinate approaches that help men access trusted health information, build media literacy skills and navigate online health content safely. This will support the cross-government work to improve outcomes for men and boys across the country, of which men’s health forms an important pillar.

Case study: James’ Place
We featured James’ Place - a charity providing free, life-saving therapy for men in suicidal crisis - in the Men’s Health Strategy. Since the strategy’s launch in 2025, James’ Place has opened its fourth suicide prevention centre in Birmingham and treated almost 1,000 more men across their 4 centres, bringing the total to over 6,000 since the charity began.
James’ Place designed its Birmingham centre, staffing and outreach around local men’s needs, involving therapists experienced in working with men in the West Midlands as well as men with lived experience. Doing so enables the charity to treat men both as individuals within their local communities and as a group with distinct needs.
The charity uses principles proven to engage men and improve wellbeing: simple referrals, urgent access with no waiting list, a warm, non-judgemental and non-clinical environment, and specialist therapists experienced in supporting a wide range of men. It also works with trusted community partners to understand local needs and reach men directly.
This approach keeps men from different communities engaged in life-saving therapy. Between April and September 2026, therapists at the charity’s Birmingham centre treated 266 men in crisis, of whom 263 completed the full course of therapy, with initial findings supporting the charity’s overall performance of significantly reducing clinical distress among men in acute suicidal crisis.
James’ Place’s model works because it goes beyond therapy: it reaches men where they are, builds trust, develops urgent referral pathways and creates wider community support that reflects the interconnected needs of those in suicidal crisis.

Case study: building the evidence on what works for boys and young men
Too often, local groups supporting boys and young men - such as youth, sports, arts and peer groups - lack the evidence to show the difference they make, resulting in the impact of their work not being understood by funders and health services.
Movember’s Community Men’s Health Research Programme is changing that. It places early-career researchers, Community Fellows, inside community organisations for 3 years to study, strengthen and evidence what already works. This is a global network with 12 places, 5 of which are in the UK. The research focuses on young men aged 12 to 35 and 3 themes:
how they cope with stress and pressures such as alcohol, drugs and gambling
loneliness and social connection
becoming a father
Alongside this, Movember’s Discovery Fellowship is funding 11 early-career researchers across 5 countries for 5 years from 2027, building the next generation of men’s health experts. Paul Galdas at the University of York leads the UK and Ireland programme and will mentor 4 fellows. This is in addition to his role as chair of the national MHAN.
Together, these programmes will give decision makers, services and communities stronger evidence on what helps young men thrive and provide evidence for future work on the men’s health strategy.

The Men’s Health Strategy signalled a long-term commitment to improve the health and wellbeing of all men and boys. We are also committing to undertake a 5-year stocktake to consider progress against the strategy’s ambitions, examine what has worked and identify priorities for the second half of the strategy’s implementation.
Annex A: progress summary
This annex provides a high-level update on progress one year into implementation against the actions set out in the Men’s Health Strategy action plan. It sets out the original action, delivery timeframe and responsible organisations, alongside a summary of progress to date.
The action plan covers the full life of the 10-year strategy. As a result, some actions are at an earlier stage of delivery or are scheduled for implementation later in the strategy period. These actions remain part of the government’s commitments under the strategy and will be prioritised as implementation moves into its next phase.

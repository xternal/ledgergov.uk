Moving to commonhold: banning leasehold for new flats: Summary of consultation responses and UK government response
Ministerial foreword
This government made a clear and unambiguous commitment in its manifesto to act where previous governments have failed and finally bring the feudal leasehold system to an end. Honouring that commitment means preventing the system from perpetuating itself while also empowering existing leaseholders so that they can more easily gain control of their buildings and exit the system if, and when, they choose to do so.
To stop the renewal of the leasehold system, we are legislating through the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill published today to make commonhold the default tenure. Commonhold is a modern homeownership structure that is used widely around the world. It is not merely an alternative to leasehold ownership, but a radical improvement on it.
At the heart of the commonhold model is a simple principle: the people who should own buildings, and who should exercise control over their management, shared facilities and related costs are not third-party landlords but the people who live in flats within them and who have a direct stake in their upkeep.
Our Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill will reinvigorate commonhold through the introduction of a comprehensive new legal framework, based on the vast majority of the recommendations made by the Law Commission in their 2020 report, that rectifies the flaws of the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002. Subject to passage of the Bill, we expect the new commonhold model to be available for use before the end of the parliament.
Informed by the detailed feedback we received to our Moving to Commonhold consultation, the Bill also contains a clear and workable legal framework for banning the use of leasehold for new flats. I am grateful to all those individuals and organisations who took the time to submit their views on the practical implications of a ban.
We want to ensure that as many people as possible benefit from our reforms. That is why we have determined that the ban will cover a wide range of flatted properties, including new build flats, conversions of buildings to flats, and repurposed buildings. On the basis of the evidence submitted, we have made some limited adaptations to commonhold to tailor its use to smaller blocks and some forms of affordable and older people’s housing. Recognising that there are limited circumstances where leasehold ownership structures remain necessary or where legal or practical barriers make the use of commonhold unworkable, we have also provided a limited number of exemptions to the ban.
Our aim has always been to facilitate a smooth and effective transition away from leasehold. To ensure that the move to commonhold for new flats does not cause harmful disruption, we have decided to provide a two-and-a-half-year transitional period between the new commonhold regime being available for use and the flat ban coming into effect as well as an exemption for pre-commencement planning applications and consents to safeguard the delivery of live sites and the immediate pipeline of new supply. We will continue to work with consumers and industry before and after the Bill receives Royal Assent to ensure a successful and sustainable transition to commonhold as the default tenure for new flats.
This is how leasehold ends - not through an abrupt and chaotic single moment of disruption, as superficially attractive as that may seem to some, but by taking a methodical approach, firmly shutting the door on leasehold’s future use and opening easy and effective escape routes for those living under it today so that we rapidly reduce the prevalence of existing leasehold. Change that is carefully considered, properly sequenced, proportionate and fair, but also real and irreversible.
Matthew Pennycook MP
Minister of State for Housing and Planning
Introduction
1. Commonhold offers full freehold ownership for flats and other interdependent properties. Each homeowner owns their home outright with no time limit on ownership, and all owners have a say over how the building is managed, including the costs and responsibilities that come with it. Commonhold is specifically designed for managing shared blocks without a third-party landlord. It provides opportunity for democratic decision-making among owners, and the flexibility to meet the changing needs of residents and the building itself.
2. Commonhold-type models are widely used around the world. They are the default tenure for owning flats in most comparable countries. Where long residential leases do exist internationally, they tend to be the exception and used for specific purposes rather than as a standard form of homeownership.
3. In contrast, in England and Wales leasehold has long been used as the main form of flat ownership, despite its well-documented flaws and evidence of dissatisfaction with the tenure. Under leasehold, homeowners do not own their homes outright. Ownership is time-limited (for example, a 99-year lease) and over time reverts to the landlord. Leaseholders are bound by the terms of their lease, which are difficult to change. Control over the building and its management are restricted by the landlord, leaving leaseholders with little say over the management of the building, associated charges or decisions affecting their homes.
4. A ‘share of freehold’, where owners are both leaseholders of their flat and collectively hold the freehold of the wider building, can bring homeowners the security and control that they want. However, it lacks both a structure for democratic decision-making and the flexibility of commonhold, as it remains reliant on its underpinning leasehold legal framework. For many people, leasehold or a share of freehold can feel like an adequate and workable form of tenure, but when issues arise, they can often highlight the limitations to the control and influence homeowners have.
5. The UK government’s long-term vision is for a housing market that works for people and communities. One that expands and improves the experience of homeownership and supports the creation of thriving neighbourhoods. We believe homeowners should have genuine control over their homes and the buildings they live in and are best placed to decide how they are looked after, and this is what commonhold offers.
6. Commonhold was first introduced in England and Wales through the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, with supporting regulations in 2004. It failed to gain traction for a variety of reasons, such as competition with the entrenched leasehold system and legal limitations that restricted its use, for example, in mixed-use buildings.
7. That landscape is now set to change. Our legislation will update and strengthen the commonhold legal framework and build on recent reforms which have levelled the playing field for commonhold. The Leasehold Reform (Ground Rent) Act 2022 prohibited ground rents in most new leasehold properties, and now more flats are being built and sold without a third-party landlord as a share of freehold. Commonhold builds on this by offering a tenure specifically designed for homeowner-led ownership and management of buildings. Following recommendations from the Law Commission, and as set out in our Commonhold White Paper published in March 2025, the UK government has now brought forward a comprehensive new legal framework so commonhold can work across all types of development.
8. The Draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill was published for pre-legislative scrutiny on 27 January 2026. The Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee examined the detail of the Bill in depth, drawing on detailed evidence submitted from across consumers and the property sector, and made a number of recommendations to government in its inquiry report. The Committee recognised the advantages of commonhold over leasehold and called for the government to continue with its fundamental aim of introducing the ban on new leasehold flats as soon as possible. The UK government responded to the Committee’s report on 19 November 2026.
9. The UK government wants as many homeowners as possible to benefit as quickly as possible from commonhold ownership. Reforming the commonhold system alone will not be sufficient. Once a reformed commonhold model is available, we can see no good reason, other than in exceptional circumstances, why new flats should continue to be provided as leasehold. That is why the UK government will take decisive action to deliver its manifesto commitment to ban the use of leasehold for new flats, to ensure that commonhold becomes the default tenure.
10. Alongside publication of the draft Bill, the UK government also launched the ‘Moving to Commonhold’ consultation.
11. This sought views on parts of the proposed legal ban that were set out in the draft Bill as well as further detail on the design of the ban. This included consideration of key definitions, necessary to ensure clarity as well as to minimise the risk of error or evasion. It also sought views on the case for any exemptions to the ban where leasehold may still be justified, as well as thoughts on the appropriate balance of timing and any transitional arrangements to enable industry to prepare for a smooth transition to use of commonhold, while helping as many homeowners and prospective homeowners benefit from commonhold ownership as quickly as possible.
12. In this consultation response, the UK government confirms the scope and key definitions of the ban as well as a limited number of exemptions that will be provided. The ban will control the marketing, sale and registration of new leasehold flats , as well as provide a redress mechanism to remedy errors, coupled with enforcement powers.
13. The UK government wishes to balance speed of implementation, with the care needed for the market and consumers to prepare for the move to commonhold, while also protecting the pipeline of new homes. As such, the UK government intends to commence the ban on a single date after a period of two and a half years following the new commonhold model being available for use (following commencement of both primary and secondary commonhold legislation) and we will work with consumers and industry to ensure a smooth transition. The UK government’s intention is that the new commonhold model will be available for use before the end of the Parliament, and we will set out an Implementation Roadmap closer to Royal Assent of the Bill. A transitional exemption will also be provided to allow live developments to continue to have the option to use leasehold for new flats subject to a live planning application or planning consent that has been granted (see Box 9).
14. The ban on the use of leasehold will not apply to existing leasehold flats. However, existing leaseholders will not be left behind. The UK government is committed to tackling unfair practices and improving leasehold as far as possible, as well as helping existing leaseholders to take greater control of their buildings and to move away from leasehold. We are implementing the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 in full, delivering greater transparency of fees and charges, rebalancing litigation costs and enhancing rights to redress. Further reforms will tackle unregulated and unaffordable ground rents, remove the disproportionate threat of forfeiture, protect leaseholders from abuse and poor service at the hands of unscrupulous managing agents, and enact remaining Law Commission recommendations on enfranchisement and the Right to Manage.
15. We will also make it easier for existing leaseholders to voluntarily convert their buildings to commonhold. Through the Bill we are bringing forward reforms to make converting existing leasehold buildings to commonhold much more accessible, by reducing the leaseholder consent threshold from the current 100% requirement to 50%. We are making further changes so that should a block convert to commonhold and retain a mix of commonhold unit owners and leaseholders, it can be managed effectively, enjoy the full benefits of commonhold and provide a fair balance of rights and protections for all homeowners as well as providing a clear pathway to ensure that the block becomes fully commonhold over time.
16. The UK government has also recently announced it will legislate to give the Secretary of State powers to introduce caps on permission fees and administration charges that leaseholders and homeowners on freehold estates may currently face, so that they are genuinely reasonable. The UK government has also confirmed that it intends to introduce independent regulation of property agents, giving leaseholders, tenants, commonhold owners and homebuyers a route towards better protections for the management and property services on which they rely. Regulation will give homeowners greater assurance, in particular, where they are in charge of the management and upkeep of their homes, that they can get the professional help and services that they need to support them.
Consultation process and responses
17. Our public consultation ‘Moving to commonhold: banning leasehold for new flats’, ran between 27 January and 24 April 2026. Respondents could respond via an online survey on Citizen Space or by email.
18. The consultation received 447 responses.
19. Of those respondents who provided background information, 281 respondents were representing the views of consumers, as existing or prospective homeowners, representative bodies, or other respondents who have been classified as consumers. There were 153 responses replying to the consultation on behalf of individuals or organisations who have been classified as representing parts of the property sector, such as developers, lenders, property agents and legal professionals such as conveyancers, as well as private landlords, operators of older people’s housing, local authorities and housing associations. There were 13 remaining respondents representing other individuals or other bodies. A full breakdown of respondents and responses to questions can be found at Annex A.
20. In addition to this consultation, we engaged with a range of stakeholders, including representative organisations, to ensure they had the opportunity to hear from government and to give their views on the proposals. We also considered related evidence that was submitted to the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee as part of their pre-legislative scrutiny of the draft Bill.
21. The consultation asked 38 questions in total, including questions using a response scale and free text box responses. Throughout this consultation response, we will use the term “the ban” as shorthand for the ban on use of leasehold for new flats, as well as “the Bill” referring to the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, and “the 2024 Act” for the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. We also refer to “the tribunal” in reference to the First-tier Tribunal (or Upper Tribunal) in England or the Leasehold Valuation Tribunal in Wales.
22. This report summarises respondents’ views by considering comments made in relation to the questions included in the consultation document. It also sets out the government’s proposed response.
Geographical scope
23. These proposals relate to England and Wales.
Summary of UK government response
24. The UK government in this response sets out the following positions with regards to the ban on the use of leasehold for new flats in the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill:
Defining development captured by the ban
the ban will apply to “flats” as defined by the criteria set out in Box 1 (as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill (paragraph 28)). The ban on the use of leasehold will only apply to “new” flats as defined by the criteria set out in Box 2 (as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill (at paragraph 48)).
The ban will not apply to existing leasehold flats
leases of flats granted or acquired prior to the commencement of the ban will be unaffected by the ban. This means existing leases can be extended, varied or regranted following a surrender, expiry or termination (paragraphs 34 to 41).
Effect on commercial leases
existing commercial leases will not be affected by the ban. However, where the owner of a building with a commercial tenant and no long residential leases wishes to sell a flat in that building, they cannot do so on a leasehold basis and the building must therefore, first be converted to commonhold (paragraphs 56 to 72).
Permitted leases within a commonhold
will be provided for shared ownership leases (including specialist variants of shared ownership to be set out in secondary legislation, such as Older Persons Shared Ownership, and Home Ownership for people with a long-term disability (or HOLD), Grenfell Assisted Home Ownership leases, home purchase plans (including lease-based Islamic finance products), certain equity release products that require a lease (paragraphs 77 to 90), and lifetime leases.
Existing restrictions on the use of commonhold
will be retained, such as flying freeholds, agricultural land and tenancies and contingent land (see Box 6) and where appropriate we are providing equivalent exemptions to the ban so that continued use of leasehold is possible in such circumstances (paragraphs 98 to 105).
Exemptions to the ban (permitted leases outside commonhold)
will include inalienable National Trust land and Crown land, as well as community land trust leases outside of a commonhold. We intend that where National Trust land is not inalienable, or new flats are built on Crown land outside of the excepted sites, the proposed ban will apply and commonhold will need to be used instead of leasehold for new flats (paragraphs 106 to 117, also see Annex B).
Treatment of older people’s housing
will be tailored to reflect the distinctive features of the sector and the needs of older homeowners. New ‘age restricted housing’ will be provided as commonhold in future (paragraph 126). Commonhold will be adapted, where appropriate, so that it can operate effectively in service-led models, including ‘retirement living’ and ‘housing-with-care’ (paragraph 129). However, given the specialist nature of housing-with-care, an exemption from the ban will also be provided, allowing new homes in this category (including Integrated Retirement Communities (IRCs) to be developed as either leasehold or commonhold in future (paragraph 131).
Structure of the ban
we will retain the design and structure of the ban as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill (see Box 8), which included key features concerning restrictions on the marketing, sale, and registration of new leasehold flats, as well as the provision of a redress mechanism to remedy errors and enforcement powers (paragraphs 133 to 147).
Timing and transition
we intend for the ban to commence on a single date after a period of two and a half years following the coming into force of both primary and secondary commonhold legislation; and a transitional exemption will be provided to allow live developments where either a planning application has been submitted or granted prior to the ban commencing to have the option to use leasehold for new flats. There will be no separate exemption for owners of historical leasehold land (see Box 9 and paragraphs 191 to 195). A condition of the exemption will require buyers to be given at least 990-year lease term, or if the developer only has a lease, the maximum term permitted under that lease.
Further refinements to commonhold for social housing
the new commonhold model has been designed to meet the needs of homeowners, tenants and providers of social housing. In response to consultation feedback, we have made additional provisions in the Bill to provide further flexibilities for providers of social housing in commonhold, concerning directorships of commonhold associations and means of ensuring regulatory obligations are met (paragraph 203 to 212).
The use of professional managing agents
government think that it should be for homeowners to determine whether they or their building would benefit from professional assistance, though through guidance we will recommend that it would be beneficial especially on larger or more complex sites to do so. (paragraphs 213 to 224).
Micro-commonhold
some flexibilities will be provided for micro-commonholds, defined as four or fewer residential units. But it should be for those commonholds to decide if they wish to be designated as a ‘micro-commonhold, and exercise some or all of the permissible flexibilities. This includes flexibilities around the appointment and retirement of directors, and with regards to processes concerning meetings and votes (paragraphs 225 to 232).
Costs and benefits
we are grateful for the evidence provided on costs and benefits of the move to commonhold which has been drawn on for the development of policy, the Bill’s impact assessment, and to inform ongoing work on implementation planning (paragraphs 233 to 244).
Ban on use of leasehold for new houses
we will make changes to the 2024 Act to provide greater alignment between the house ban and the flat ban. We will for example dispense with the role of the tribunal in pre-approving certain exemptions to the house ban, as well as provide greater alignment concerning exemptions between the two bans (paragraphs 245 to 252, and see Annex B regarding a comparison of exemptions). We intend the ban on new leasehold houses to come into force at the same time as the ban on new leasehold flats.
Implementation planning
we have published our ‘Direction of Travel’ plan of initial implementation work and forward plan. This will be followed by the publication of an ‘Implementation Roadmap’ with detailed implementation plans, including timeline for delivery and commencement of secondary legislation and delivery of delivery partner and sector implementation activity. Work will be informed by the establishment of a ‘Commonhold Implementation Steering Group’ (see Box 10 and paragraphs 253 to 262).
Scope of the ban
Defining developments captured by the ban: “flats”
25. With a reformed commonhold model in place, the UK government sees no compelling reason why new flats should continue to be sold on a long lease, other than in exceptional circumstances.
26. Instead, commonhold will become the default tenure for new flats, enabling more homeowners to benefit from commonhold homeownership. Our manifesto commitment was clear both to reform the commonhold framework and to take steps to ban new leasehold flats (“the ban”). To deliver this objective, in the consultation we proposed a broad scope for the ban, where the vast majority of new flats intended for homeownership should be sold as commonhold instead of leasehold.
27. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we proposed a broad definition of a “flat” so that the ban should apply not only to conventional flats, but also to more unusual forms of flatted development. This ensures the flat ban aligns with the house ban, avoiding gaps in coverage.
28. This was set out as follows (at Box 1) in both the draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill published for pre-legislative scrutiny, as well as the Moving to Commonhold consultation:
Box 1
“Flats” for the purpose of the ban
A “flat” is a separate set of premises (on one or more floors):
which forms part of a building
which is constructed or adapted for use for the purposes of a dwelling; and
the whole or a material part of which lies above or below some other part of the building
29. The majority of respondents to the consultation, both consumers and property industry professionals agreed with this proposed definition. Overall, 9 out of 10 respondents that responded to this question agreed. There was almost unanimous support among consumer respondents and 8 in 10 property sector respondents agreed (see Table 1)

30. The consultation provided the option to offer written feedback on the definition A number of respondents, including both consumers and developers were keen to ensure that the definition was suitably broad to cover certain irregular building types such as flats over garages, properties of a ‘coach house’ type design, flats above garages, or properties where there was a degree of over or underhang with other land or property.
31. Separately, some respondents (including a number of housing associations) suggested that a definition should instead be based around the characteristics of a building as a whole, such as its complexity, rather than an individual flat.
32. In light of this feedback, we have further tested the legal definitions against a broad range of property typologies and are confident that the definition provided in the Bill for flats combined with that for houses in the 2024 Act will provide comprehensive coverage of all permanent residential dwellings, whether houses or flats.
33. We recognise that there are a number of ways the scope of the ban could be defined. The proposed ban takes account of various aspects of building design and complexity by defining which ‘relevant buildings’ will be captured by the ban. Further, in recognition of the complexities of certain types of building and how they will need to be managed, we have committed to some further adaptations to the reformed commonhold model, such as allowing for alternative mandatory Articles of Association and Commonhold Community Statements for very small commonholds (see ‘Further refinements to commonhold’). As such, the UK government has retained the definition of “flat” as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill in the Bill (at Box 1).
Defining developments captured by the ban: “new” flats
34. The ban on the use of leasehold will apply to “new” flats but will not apply to existing leasehold flats.
35. We are not banning the sale of existing leasehold flats. Existing residential leaseholders will continue to be able to sell their leasehold flats as they would be able to do today. Where new flats are provided and can be commonhold, we are clear that they should be. But for existing flats, it should be for the existing homeowners to decide whether to convert to commonhold.
36. The Bill contains measures to make it more accessible and easier for leaseholders who wish to do so to convert their buildings to commonhold. We believe that the benefits of moving to commonhold will become compelling as consumer understanding of the tenure improves and the market matures, but as it is their home, it should be their choice.
37. We will also not ban the grant of new or replacement leases for leasehold flats that existed before the ban comes into effect.
38. Leaseholders may need to enter into new leases for various reasons, such as extending an existing lease, or making substantial variations that require a regrant. These arrangements are entered into voluntarily by existing leaseholders and their landlords. Prohibiting this would significantly curtail the rights of leaseholders at a time when we are working to enhance their protections.
39. This includes continuing to facilitate buy-back arrangements of existing homes, which can be a feature of certain types of older people’s housing for example, where a new lease is generated for every re-sale which would otherwise be prevented by the ban.
40. Furthermore, conversion to commonhold can only take place on a whole-building basis. This means it cannot provide a viable alternative where a new or replacement lease is needed on an existing individual leasehold flat within a block, without unfairly imposing costs on other leasehold owners in the block, who would also need to compensate the landlord if a single flat triggered a conversion to commonhold.
41. Similarly, we do not intend to ban landlords from granting new leases on existing leasehold flats where a previous lease has expired, been voluntarily surrendered, or terminated due to a breach of lease terms. In such scenarios, other residential leaseholders in the building may be unable or unwilling to convert the building to commonhold. Expecting the landlord to bear the full cost of converting the building to commonhold in order to sell the flat again would be unreasonable, particularly where the circumstances were beyond their control.
42. In the draft Bill and consultation, we proposed that the ban should apply to both new flats for sale in newly completed developments as well as flats that are newly created for sale in certain existing buildings, such as an existing commercial building or house newly converted into flats.
43. This is because reforms in the Bill will allow commonhold to be used in a much broader range of settings than is currently possible, including more complex buildings such as mixed use or mixed tenure blocks and also to close a potential loophole where an existing building could be created and later converted into flats to evade the ban.
44. For the purpose of the ban, we therefore, proposed a definition of “new” flats, as follows in the consultation and draft Bill at Box 2 below.
45. Overall, the large majority of respondents to the consultation agreed with all 5 categories of the proposals (Box 2, A to E) to define “new” flats which should be captured by the ban.
46. For each of the categories, over 9 in 10 consumer respondents agreed with the proposals. Of the small number of consumer respondents who disagreed, their dissatisfaction was often based on wanting to go further and for the ban to additionally apply to all existing flats too. For respondents from different parts of the property sector, there was a majority in favour, albeit a smaller majority. Across each category, between more than half and just under two thirds of property sector respondents agreed (see Table 2).
47. The data showed that if a respondent disagreed with applying the ban to purpose-built new flats (Category A), in most cases they disagreed with all of the other categories (B to E) too. It also showed the reverse – that where respondents agreed with capturing new builds, in most cases they agreed that all categories should be captured.
48. In considering specific categories, 7 of the 14 organisations identifying themselves as, or a representative of, developers who responded to the consultation did not want the ban to apply to new leasehold flats. Whilst this was driven by providers of Integrated Retirement Communities (IRCs), 6 of whom disagreed with the proposed scope of the ban on the basis of calling for a general exemption for retirement housing, written comments from a number of volume developers suggests that they wanted to be able to choose between use of commonhold and leasehold for new homes, and for commonhold to have proven its efficacy in a range of settings before a ban is implemented.
Box 2
“New” flats for the purpose of the ban:
A. Purpose-built new developments: including flats for sale, and which may or may not include other uses, such as commercial units and light industrial space, or homes for rent.
B. A house newly converted into flats where at least one flat is for sale on a long lease.
C. Commercial buildings newly redeveloped to contain flats where at least one flat is for sale. For example, converted office space or a shopping centre with flats on upper floors.
D. Purpose-built rental blocks whose owners have later opted to sell flats for homeownership, for example, a block entirely made up of Build to Rent flats where one or all flats are subsequently marketed for sale to prospective homeowners.
E. Other residential buildings where there are no existing registered long residential leases, such as an existing block of flats in vacant possession, that is refurbished so the flats can be resold.
49. Despite a general trend of either favouring or opposing all categories, there was variation between levels of support, especially in relation to certain existing buildings. For instance, capturing house conversions was more widely supported by the property sector than capturing office-to-residential conversions.
50. In the written comments, the slightly lower levels of support for office-to-residential conversions were often linked to concerns - from a range of property sector interests - around whether commonhold would be attractive for, and work in, mixed-use settings, and what ability a commercial unit owner or tenant will have to influence the building. We discuss this further in the section below. A number of respondents wanted to see commonhold work effectively on large or mixed-use sites (such as the introduction of ‘sections’ to delegate decision making within a block) before a ban on leasehold should be introduced.
51. With respect to purpose-built rented blocks, local authorities and housing associations raised specific concerns around the application of the ban to existing buildings, and potential unintended consequences in connection to the Right to Buy or Right to Acquire forcing existing stock or new blocks into using commonhold.

52. As we stated in the consultation, the grant of a new lease may be necessary for certain flats that were not previously leasehold, including when a social tenant exercises their Right to Buy, or Right to Acquire, or where a tenant wishes to purchase the flat in which they live through ‘Right to Shared Ownership, Social HomeBuy or after using Rent to Buy, or London Living Rent or equivalent schemes in Wales. In addition, the grant of a lease may also be necessary for the Grenfell Assisted Homeownership Scheme.
53. We recognise that requiring landlords to convert the building to commonhold in these circumstances would place an inappropriate burden on them for circumstances beyond their control. Nor should routes to homeownership through such schemes be restricted by the ban. Like other leaseholders, these leaseholders will benefit from our wider reforms to extend rights and protections as well as make the right to manage, enfranchisement and conversion to commonhold.
54. The UK government confirms that where a lease is granted pursuant to a tenant exercising their Right to Buy, or Right to acquire, or pursuant to an existing social or assured tenancy under certain affordable homeownership schemes, the grant of that lease will continue to be permitted without requiring the building to convert to commonhold. The Bill includes a power to specify qualifying affordable home ownership schemes in regulations. We intend this to include schemes such as Right to Shared Ownership, Social HomeBuy, Rent to Buy, London Living Rent and equivalent schemes in Wales, while providing flexibility to accommodate future affordable homeownership schemes.
55. We note the calls from a range of parties that commonhold must work effectively across the range of development types, and also the calls for certain exemptions from the ban (see ‘Exemptions to the ban’). We agree with the need for effective implementation (see ‘Implementation planning’) as well as a smooth transition to the new commonhold regime ahead of the leasehold ban commencing (see ‘Timetable for moving to commonhold’). Following the positive responses to this consultation on the scope of the ban for both new and existing buildings, the UK government will retain the approach to what constitutes a ‘new’ flat for the purposes of the ban as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill (at Box 2).
Commercial leases
56. Commonhold is primarily intended for buildings that involve an element of residential ownership, but it also provides a broad legal framework for ownership and management that could also be used for exclusively commercial buildings. Nevertheless, we are not proposing to ban commercial leases, nor change how or when they can be granted in this legislation; nor will entirely commercial buildings be expected to use commonhold in the future, though they could adopt commonhold if they wish.
57. Changes to the commonhold framework in the Bill mean that commonhold will now be better suited for use in mixed blocks of residential and commercial developments, for example, by introducing ‘sections’ to allow for delegated decision-making and new ways to apportion costs.
58. The use of buildings may of course change over time, and through the consultation we wanted to consider the best approach for the consumer, landlord and commercial arrangements should a portion of floor space in an existing commercial building be converted to residential use for homeownership. We sought views on the approach to where the owner of a commercial leasehold building, with no long residential leases, wished to change the building’s use by offering a new flat within that wider commercial building for residential homeownership.

We recognise that familiarity and understanding of the new commonhold regime will be crucial, as many of the concerns raised have been specifically accounted for in the updated commonhold legal framework set out in the Bill, such as use of ‘sections’ to delegate decision making (for example, to separate out the interests of commercial and residential unit owners so one party cannot negatively influence the interests of the other on defined matters), apportionment of costs (for example, so that the budget for an asset which only benefits one party, such as CCTV for commercial premises is only paid for by the relevant unit owners and also cannot be amended or withdrawn by other unit owners), and new enforcement and redress powers, such as the backstop of the property tribunal (to protect the interests of a minority party outvoted on a matter which materially negatively affects their interests, like a vote to change local rules around use, or hours of use of the property or wider site).
68. For commonhold to work effectively at scale the Law Commission made a number of these specific recommendations to protect the interests of both residential and commercial owners, which we are taking forward in the Bill, drawing on international experience where a mix of residential and commercial uses in developments has long been established through use of commonhold type models such as strata and condominiums.
69. We do not think it is proportionate, for example, for a residential tenant to force a landlord to convert the entire building to commonhold in the case of exercising the Right to Buy or Right to Acquire. However, if a landlord (that is, the freeholder) chooses to offer a new home intended for homeownership as part of a previously exclusive commercial development, that is their choice and the new homeowner should expect to benefit from commonhold ownership as the owner of a new flat in another development would be required to have. Whether the landlord determines that the number of flats in question is proportionate to require the building to be converted to commonhold is a matter for them.
70. It should also be incumbent on the landlord to design the commonhold in such a way to make use of new features such as ‘sections’ where appropriate, to support the smooth running of the building and protecting the interests of all owners. It is our intention that a change of the building from leasehold to commonhold would see the commercial lease continue, and the leaseholder’s landlord would remain the same, but as a unit owner of the commercial space.
71. The new commonhold model is specifically designed to manage complex and mixed-use buildings. Other than in exceptional circumstances all new flats should be provided as commonhold, including new flatted developments, within a mixed residential and commercial development and we see no reason why this should not extend to existing commercial buildings that become a mix of commercial and residential over time.
72. The UK government confirms that where the owner of a building with a commercial tenant and no long residential leases wishes to sell a new flat, the building must first be converted to commonhold.
Exemptions to the ban
73. Countries across the world have commonhold-type models by default. For example, France uses a model called copropriété, in Germany, Wohnungseigentumsgesetz. The USA and Canada’s version of commonhold is called condominium, and in Australia and New Zealand, strata title.
74. Many of these same countries do, however, continue to use leases in certain narrow circumstances to cater for specific homeownership products, land ownership arrangements, or to accommodate wider public policy goals. For example, in 2014 the French government promoted a long leasehold model as a means of encouraging private investment into redeveloping existing homes into affordable housing. Australia has an advanced legal framework for freehold flat ownership under the strata system, but in Australia’s Capital Territory (ACT) to control how land is used and developed, the government retains the freehold and homes within the ACT are only sold on a leasehold basis (typically 99 years).
75. While our ambition is to make commonhold the default tenure for new flats, we recognise that there may be certain circumstances where a lease may be appropriate or even preferable for the benefit of particular groups of consumers or to support certain policy objectives. This could be either a lease of a commonhold unit, or a leasehold structure outside commonhold.
76. Through the consultation we sought views on the case for exemptions from the ban across three categories (see Box 3):

Permitted leases within a commonhold
77. Under existing legislation, long residential leases cannot be granted within commonhold. In the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, we are changing this to broaden the scope for commonhold’s use. The consultation proposed exemptions for several types of long residential leases within commonhold, specifically shared ownership leases (including specialist variants of shared ownership, such as Older Persons Shared Ownership, and Home Ownership for people with a long-term disability), home purchase plans (including Islamic finance products), and certain equity release products that require a lease.
78. Permitting these lease-based arrangements within commonhold will give consumers greater flexibility in how they finance their homes, such as helping them get onto the property ladder or releasing equity from their current home. For example, shared ownership uses a lease to enable homebuyers to purchase a “share” of the equity of a property, helping make homeownership more affordable. The lease governs the relationship between the buyer and the provider and enables the buyer to acquire additional “shares” over time.
79. The most common substantive response regarding permitted leases within a commonhold, raised by a quarter of respondents, was that no further exemptions should be provided for use of leasehold within a commonhold beyond the list proposed.
80. As one managing agent respondent suggested, expanding beyond the list of proposed permitted leases within commonhold could risk reintroducing complexity and introducing loopholes that could undermine the purpose of commonhold as a simpler and more transparent alternative to leasehold. Whilst this point is acknowledged, we have made a small number of targeted additions and amendments to the list (outlined below) to incorporate certain specific lease arrangements highlighted by consultees which we consider will provide ongoing consumer benefit and support our policy objectives.
81. The UK government confirms that we will provide an exemption to the ban allowing permitted leases within commonhold for shared ownership leases, including specialist variants of shared ownership to be set out in secondary legislation, such as Older Persons Shared Ownership and Home Ownership for people with a long-term disability; home purchase plans, including Islamic finance products; certain equity release products that require a lease; lifetime leases; and Grenfell Assisted Homeownership Scheme leases.
82. This list reflects a number of important points of detail that were raised in consultation responses.
83. For example, a number of respondents suggested that the definition of shared ownership within the draft Bill was too restrictive as it included provisions around staircasing to 100% ownership, which would exclude certain shared ownership products such as Older Persons Shared Ownership schemes and certain schemes developed by Community Land Trusts, as well as homes acquired through accessing the Grenfell Assisted Homeownership Scheme.
84. We do not want to limit access to shared ownership schemes. The Bill provides for a broad and inclusive exemption for shared ownership within commonhold. While the revised Bill continues to make reference to conditions regarding staircasing to 100% ownership, this is to confirm that, on acquiring 100% equity, a shared owner can acquire the commonhold unit. The Bill includes provisions to specify in regulations types of housing products that are not required to include staircasing provisions in order to qualify for the exemption and to be a permitted lease within a commonhold. This is intended to include Older Persons Shared Ownership and Home Ownership for people with a long-term disability. These schemes have restrictions on staircasing in order to ensure such homes remain available as affordable housing for the particular groups they are intended to support. Use of regulations allows flexibility to extend the exemption to new or innovative forms of shared ownership housing that may be developed in the future.
85. Respondents to the consultation also sought clarity about how other affordable housing products might operate within a commonhold.
86. As discussed earlier (see paragraph 69), a social tenant exercising the Right to Buy or Right to Acquire, would not trigger the conversion of their building to commonhold and these leases will still be permitted outside of a commonhold. Should a building containing such leases convert from leasehold to commonhold, their landlords could either participate in the enfranchisement and conversion process to become commonhold unit owners or be ‘non-consenting leaseholders’ as part of the conversion (see Box 4).
87. The UK government confirms that where a building is an existing commonhold, social tenants newly entering into a Right to Buy or Right to Acquire will acquire the freehold of the property through ownership of a commonhold unit, and leases in these circumstances will not be permitted within a commonhold.

88. While there was agreement among consultation respondents for an exemption to the ban to allow for use of leases as part of Home Purchase Plans within a commonhold, respondents raised helpful technical points of detail.
89. A number of respondents made suggestions about the definition proposed in the draft Bill regarding home purchase plans. In particular, they noted that it required them to be regulated as defined in the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000, meaning that the exemption was too restrictive and would exclude home purchase plans used in practice, including Buy-to-Let home purchase plans. Respondents also noted that the definition did not account for home purchase plans which give the customer a right, rather than an obligation, to acquire the finance provider’s interest over time.
90. The UK government has amended the definition of home purchase plans for the purpose of the exemption in the Bill to better reflect the range of products used in practice. In particular, the revised definition no longer excludes Buy-to-Let home purchase plans and includes arrangements where the customer has a right, but not an obligation, to acquire the provider’s interest. Corresponding amendments will also be made to the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 to ensure consistency between the house and flat ban regimes.
91. Another arrangement highlighted through the consultation exercise was lifetime leases.
92. Lifetime leases are a form of later-life housing finance product which give the consumer the legal right to live in a property for the rest of their life, without giving them freehold ownership of that property, which is held by the provider. These are non-assignable leases, meaning they cannot transfer to another party. Consumers may port their agreement to another property, but the product does not create a route for new leaseholds to be provided on to the open market. To maintain consumer choice, the UK government has included lifetime leases as a permitted lease within commonhold in the Bill. We have also amended the 2024 Act to allow use of lifetime leases for houses.
93. There were calls for exemptions to be tightly defined to provide clarity and seek to avoid the opportunity for error or evasion from the ban. Also, given the nature of an additional party within the commonhold structure, respondents supported the provision of clear guidance around roles, responsibilities and rights and protections within the commonhold community for all parties.
94. The Bill makes provision for permitted leaseholders to have the same core rights as commonhold unit owners (see Box 5).
95. This includes the right to participate in the governance of the commonhold association, such as electing directors (including being a director) and voting on the annual budget and other decisions made by the commonhold association. Permitted leaseholders will also benefit from commonhold consumer protections such as minority rights to contest decisions at the property tribunal in place of leasehold rights, such as to challenge the reasonableness of service charges.
96. For these permitted leases, the provider of the shared ownership lease (typically a housing association, local authority, or other provider), or provider of the home purchase plan, will retain the freehold or headlease of the commonhold unit. These providers will be able to vote ‘in place of the permitted leaseholder’ for certain significant decisions (limited to where they may be liable for certain costs during the Initial Repair Period or in votes over the termination of the commonhold). They may not elect a director but could stand as a director, and can access the property tribunal to contest decisions of the commonhold association which negatively affect their interests. Where the permitted leaseholder’s equity is paid back to the provider in full, the provider will transfer the unit to the homebuyer to acquire the commonhold title to match the commonhold rights they already enjoy.

While there was broad agreement with providing corresponding exemptions where there are restrictions on commonhold registration among a number of property sector professionals, including legal experts, there was some debate around the treatment of flying freeholds. Respondents considered that the restriction should not be extended to be an outright exemption for any flying freehold, with a number of respondents cautioning that such an exemption could create a loophole whereby developments are designed in such a way as to evade the ban. 101. Agricultural land includes tenancies granted under agricultural tenancy legislation, typically as part of a wider farming business. They often include residential accommodation connected to the farm. Consultation respondents supported permitting continued use of residential leasehold on land subject to agricultural tenancy arrangements, with around 6 in 10 consumer respondents agreeing and around 9 in 10 property professionals also agreeing (see Annex A, Question 10 for a full breakdown by respondent type). Respondents considered leasehold a useful tool to enable farm businesses and agricultural landlords to negotiate tenancy lengths to suit their business needs, and that longer-term tenancies for example, could help provide the certainty needed for investment in agricultural operations. Providing an exemption is also consistent with provisions in the 2024 Act for the ban on use of leasehold for houses.
102. The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002 prohibits registering commonhold on various types of land with a contingent title (that is, where the land may revert to another person or body in certain specified circumstances). This includes land subject to statutory reverter provisions under the School Sites Act 1841, the Literary and Scientific Institutions Act 1854 and the Places of Worship Sites Act 1873.
103. The Acts apply where land has been provided for schools, museums, libraries, universities or places of worship, and may revert if it ceases to be used for those purposes. This means the title to the land lacks the permanence required for commonhold as the freehold could be pulled away from the commonhold association and unit owners if the statutory reverter was ever triggered. Without an equivalent exemption from the flat ban, flats on these sites could not be sold to consumers because commonhold registration would be prohibited and leasehold sale would also be banned. Consultation responses, from both consumers overall, and overwhelmingly from the property sector, supported carrying these exclusions forward into exemptions to this ban.
104. The 2002 Act also prohibits the registration of commonhold on sites subject to Compulsory Purchase Orders (CPO), and again in the consultation we proposed providing an exemption to the ban in the limited circumstances a new flat is sold while subject to a live CPO. There were concerns raised by a small number of respondents that CPO s could be misused and for example, provide a loophole whereby a body such as a local authority could seek to gain land for leasehold development. Others suggested that it was unclear why land subject to a CPO was treated differently to land subject to an option agreement or conditional contract. Our view, however, is that compulsory purchase is a statutory process subject to public and ministerial oversight and therefore difficult to exploit for the purposes of an exemption. A CPO is therefore also suitably distinct from private arrangements such as options or conditional contracts which could be created by private parties deliberately to avoid the ban.
105. Respondents also suggested that clarity should be provided to make clear that land that has historically been subject to a CPO should not be restricted in perpetuity from being used for a future commonhold development. The UK government has amended provisions in the Bill to provide an exemption where a flat is subject to a live CPO, and to also allow subsequent sales of leasehold flats in a block where an initial sale of a leasehold flat has taken place under a CPO.
Continued permitted use of leasehold
106. The reformed commonhold model is designed to accommodate all types of residential and mixed-use development. We recognise however, that there may be some exceptions or particular circumstances where commonhold may not be appropriate and continued use of leasehold may be justified
107. There may be justifiable reasons to protect certain tracts of land from being sold on a freehold basis. In the UK, this is commonly termed ‘inalienable’ land, but preventing the transfer of freehold title is a feature of many countries where commonhold-type models are the default tenure. For instance, New Zealand, Canada, and Australia all have systems to protect the sale of certain categories of land on a freehold basis, whether to prevent land speculation, to protect wildlife and habitats, or to preserve land so that it can be enjoyed for wider public benefit in perpetuity.
Inalienable land
108. Respondents identified specific examples of the need for exemptions regarding inalienable land, for land held inalienably by the National Trust and excepted sites on Crown land.
109. The vast majority of National Trust land is held inalienably and cannot be sold on a freehold basis. This is so the National Trust can retain ownership of such land and properties to look after them on behalf of the nation. The National Trust rarely develops new leasehold housing on this land, but for example, does sometimes seek to convert existing buildings into leasehold properties (houses and flats) so that the buildings can be protected and maintained, but where ownership can be retained by the National Trust. On this land, without an exemption the Trust would be prohibited from developing any flats for home ownership.
110. A number of respondents also noted restrictions on how certain land owned by the Crown can be disposed of. This is recognised in the 2024 Act ban on new leasehold houses, where the Crown bodies are exempt from the ban but have agreed to use the exemption only in defined circumstances known as ‘excepted areas’. This includes land and properties around Royal Parks and Palaces or other locations with a long historic association with the Crown. Freehold ownership is restricted in these areas as land may be held inalienably, for security reasons or as part of longstanding arrangements connected to the provision and funding of local services and infrastructure. New development rarely takes place in such excepted places where homes cannot currently be provided on a freehold basis.
111. In these cases, where freehold development is not possible, a ban would restrict any form of flatted residential development. The UK government has made provisions in the Bill to provide an exemption to the ban for new leasehold flats on inalienable National Trust land or excepted areas on Crown land. Where new flats are provided on non-inalienable land held by the National Trust, the proposed ban will apply. We also intend that new flats on Crown land outside of the excepted areas comply with the ban.
Community-led housing
112. There are circumstances where long leases remain appropriate because they help support particular land ownership arrangements and wider policy objectives.
113. Community Land Trusts (CLTs) are a form of community-led housing where the ownership of the homes by the community, for the benefit of the community, is core to the purpose for which the housing is provided, with the objective of ensuring that homes remain affordable and available for local people in perpetuity.
114. A number of respondents to the consultation argued that banning use of leasehold for new flats could damage the operation of established CLT models that rely on leasehold to achieve long-term community participation. Respondents suggested that without continued community buy-in and ownership there was a risk that land would not be made available for new supply. This could harm the development of small, often difficult sites, which are normally off limits or of no interest to mainstream developers.
115. Commonhold and community-led housing share a number of characteristics and values around democratic ownership and decision making. However, in the specific case of CLTs respondents suggested that the key difference was that CLTs have a duty to consider the wider public benefit, balancing the interests of the current occupants with those of their neighbours and of future generations. We agree that this is fundamentally distinct from commonhold which is intentionally designed to transfer control strictly to those who own the homes and commercial spaces in the development.
116. Some respondents also suggested that community housing type leases should also be permitted within a commonhold. For example, where a community land trust is the third-party owner of a shared ownership home, with associated restrictions on staircasing to regulate resale and provisions to keep the property affordable at a discounted market rate.
117. The UK government will provide an exemption for community land trust leases outside of a commonhold. However, we do not agree that a community-led housing exemption is necessary for leases within a commonhold. This is because conditions relating to a property’s discounted market sale can be facilitated within commonhold without the need for a lease. In addition, the amended definition of shared ownership provided in the Bill will provide flexibility to accommodate community land trust shared ownership arrangements.
Housing for older people
118. Suitable housing for older people is crucial to supporting health, wellbeing and independence. A number of respondents considered that commonhold was well suited both to mainstream housing occupied by older people and to more specialist forms of older people’s housing.
119. Flexibility and democracy are key features of commonhold which can help tailor the management and use of buildings to their residents’ needs and also meet the changing needs of buildings and the people that live in them over time. Respondents also recognised the invaluable experience and contribution that many older people could bring to supporting homeowner led management of buildings.
120. Specialist older people’s housing covers a broad range of housing options. In particular, the extent of ongoing operator involvement, the provision of care and support services and the complexity of communal facilities and services can vary significantly between different forms of older people’s housing. These are typified by three broad categories of housing (see Box 7 below, and based upon National Planning Policy Guidance on housing for older and disabled people).
121. Each has a valuable role to play in supporting our ageing population and we want to see more of all of them across the country. We want the sector to grow and thrive, offering older people a wider range of housing, care and support options that meet their needs and preferences.
122. These models operate with materially different business models, cost structures, service offers and funding approaches, including varying reliance on event fees (including deferred management charges), communal facilities and long-term operator involvement. It is not surprising therefore, that consultation respondents tended to differ in their views on the workability of commonhold and the justification for the continued use of leasehold across different parts of the market. Indeed, there was consensus among respondents to avoid a one-size-fits-all approach for the sector.
Box 7
Categories of specialist older people’s housing
A. Age-restricted housing: limited shared amenities, such as communal gardens, with no support or care services. Developers may have little or no long-term involvement following initial sales.
B. Retirement living: more communal facilities, which might include a communal lounge or laundry facilities and full or part-time warden staff. Developers or operators may act as long term operators of the development.
C. Housing-with-care: residents live independently but have access to care and support services provided as part of the scheme, typically available on site and on a 24-hour basis. Developments often include more extensive communal facilities and amenities such as restaurants, leisure facilities such as a pool or gym, alongside personal care services where required. This category includes Integrated Retirement Communities (or IRCs). Developers may act as long term operators of the development, and some business models rely in part on deferred management fees.
123. Many older people’s housing developers and operators provide an excellent home and service for their residents. The sector has however, attracted some criticism in recent years, reflecting both problems common to leasehold more generally and issues that are specific to older people’s housing. Concerns have been raised around issues such as consumers facing high and often poorly understood service charges, a lack of transparency around ongoing and exit costs, limited routes to challenge poor management, and evidence of weak resale outcomes, with some properties taking extended periods to sell or achieving lower-than-expected values. This can undermine consumer confidence and may have knock on effects on investment in new supply. At the same time, many of these models provide significant lifestyle, wellbeing and care benefits for residents and will continue to play an important role in meeting future housing need.
124. Commonhold itself offers potential solutions to some of these issues through freehold ownership, greater transparency around costs and services, and stronger resident oversight and accountability. The UK government’s wider leasehold reform and home buying and selling reforms will also help improve consumer rights and protections and improve transparency of charges and help consumers to make more informed choices. We are grateful to the work of the Older People’s Housing Taskforce led by Professor Julienne Meyer for their recommendations on both boosting the supply of housing for older people as well as improving the experience of consumers living in specialist older people’s housing. On 11 November, the UK government formally responded to the Taskforce’s report, outlining its intention to publish a roadmap in due course setting out the steps that government will take to improve housing options for older people.
125. Among the Taskforce’s recommendations was a call to develop more evidence around the drivers of poor resale values in parts of the market, as well as to learn lessons from places like New Zealand (where the older people’s housing market is larger and more mature), and to consider alternative models of provision beyond leasehold and commonhold, a point also raised by a number of respondents to the consultation. As we take forward our wider leasehold and commonhold programmes, we will continue to consider how different tenure models can operate effectively in this sector, drawing on international experience where relevant and informed by ongoing engagement with the sector.
126. For the Bill, the UK government will adopt a tailored approach to older people’s housing, recognising that different parts of the sector have different characteristics and requirements. This includes adapting commonhold for some forms of specialist older people’s housing while retaining leasehold for housing-with-care schemes.
127. Age restricted housing is closest to mainstream residential flats with limited shared services and little reliance on long-term operator involvement, deferred fees or complex service arrangements. Most consultation respondents considered that the reformed commonhold model would be well suited to this type of housing and be welcome in delivering the core benefits of commonhold: freehold ownership, collective control and clearer accountability and transparency around costs. The UK government believes that the reformed commonhold model will provide a workable and preferable alternative to leasehold for age-restricted housing, and new age-restricted homes for sale should be provided on a commonhold basis. Existing homeowners in age restricted housing may also be attracted to converting their existing leasehold properties to commonhold.
128. For retirement living and housing-with-care schemes, many respondents to the consultation highlighted the important role played by long-term operators in many developments. They noted that operators play a vital role in delivering and coordinating communal services and facilities, and support residents to live independently. Respondents also emphasised that these arrangements can provide important consumer benefits, including access to facilities and services that might not otherwise be affordable, as well as underpinning investment in the development and operation of new schemes.
129. A number of respondents argued that commonhold should be capable of accommodating these features. They considered that commonhold should support appropriate long-term operator involvement, whilst retaining the core benefits of commonhold ownership and resident control. Respondents suggested mechanisms to support proportionate ongoing operator involvement in the delivery of services, facilities and management functions where this delivers value to residents and supports the effective operation of schemes. The UK government will adapt the commonhold model so that it can operate effectively in service-led forms of older people’s housing, including retirement living and housing-with-care schemes. These changes will support continued investment and growth in the sector while ensuring consumers can benefit from commonhold ownership, without having to take on greater day-to-day management responsibilities than they might wish to assume.
130. We also recognise that housing-with-care schemes, including IRCs, are distinct from other forms of specialist older people’s housing. These developments typically involve the provision of care and support services as part of the scheme offer and often rely on more complex operational and funding arrangements. They can play an important role in supporting independence, wellbeing and choice for older people, and can help people remain in suitable accommodation as their needs change over time.
131. In light of these factors, and to support continued consumer choice through greater investment and supply of older people’s housing the UK government will provide a targeted exemption from the ban on new leasehold flats for housing-with-care schemes, allowing these homes to be developed as either leasehold or commonhold. This will allow consumers access to different models of housing, ownership and service provision, while ensuring that developers and operators can continue to bring forward new schemes.
132. At the same time, we remain committed to strengthening consumer protections across all forms of older people’s housing, regardless of tenure. Alongside wider leasehold and home-buying reforms, we will continue to work with the sector to improve transparency around fees and charges, support informed consumer choice, strengthen accountability and address concerns about consumer outcomes, including the resale experience in parts of the market.
Structure of the ban
133. We will deliver our manifesto commitment to make commonhold the default tenure by bringing the ban on new leasehold flats into law. Key components of the ban were set out in the Draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill published for pre-legislative scrutiny in January this year. A legally binding ban will ensure it will apply consistently across the market, creating a level playing field for new housing supply and maximising the number and speed at which consumers will benefit from commonhold homeownership. This approach provides clarity, certainty and enforceability, helping to drive the transition away from leasehold.
How the ban will operate
134. We proposed that the ban on new leasehold flats should take a similar approach to Part 1 of the 2024 Act, which prohibits the use of leasehold for new houses, other than in defined exceptional circumstances.
135. This approach to the bans puts the onus on developers and vendors to demonstrate compliance with the ban before a prospective buyer has handed over any money. Consumers should not only have better upfront information when buying a home, a key focus of our wider reforms to the system of home buying and selling, but they should also be assured that the legally correct tenure is being marketed in the first place.
136. Aligning the approach to banning use of leasehold for flats and houses will make compliance simpler for industry and provide clarity for consumers, particularly where it may be unclear whether a new property for sale is a flat or house.
137. There are multiple checkpoints built into the proposed ban to protect the consumer from being wrongly mis-sold a leasehold flat at the marketing, sale, and registration stages of a purchase. These stages are set out below (at Box 8) and are needed because there are potential situations where legitimate new leases may be created after the property has been marketed for sale. For example, buying a new commonhold unit where the consumer wished to use a home purchase plan (a permitted lease) to finance the purchase, which relied on a lease. A lease of the commonhold unit is legitimately created by the provider of the home purchase plan to enable the buyer to complete the sale. Checks after the marketing stage are therefore designed to reassure buyers that what they are purchasing is compliant with the ban.
138. If it transpires that these checkpoints have been evaded by a vendor, consumers will have a backstop redress right, enabling them to acquire what they should have been sold in the first place. Collectively, this system of up-front checks underpinned by financial penalties, and the backstop redress right will provide a clear and robust framework to ensure compliance and disincentivise those looking to get around the ban.
139. Consultees were invited to provide any written feedback on the structure of the ban overall. Around one-third opted to do so. The most common theme among written feedback was general agreement with the ban, reflected in written responses from both consumers and representatives of the property sector.
140. A second key theme, emerging particularly from property sector respondents, focused on practical and implementation considerations, ranging from clear guidance and simple standardised forms to support compliance, to ensuring that existing leasehold flats remain freely marketable, and to ensuring that accompanying the structure is a transitional period that allows the market the time they need to prepare to design, build and market commonhold properties.
141. Some consumer respondents felt that the ban did not go far enough and should also apply to existing leasehold properties and require mandatory conversions to commonhold. Others supported the ban but were concerned that a two-tier market could emerge, with new commonhold properties being viewed more favourably and existing leasehold properties by contrast, becoming harder to sell or finance.
Box 8
The structure of the ban:
A. Marketing: Advertising for sale a new leasehold flat will be banned, unless the flat qualifies for an exemption. Where an exemption applies, marketing materials must show the basis for exemption.
B. Sale: Granting a new lease of a flat will be banned, unless the flat qualifies for an exemption. For exempt leases of flats, the landlord will be required to issue a prescribed ‘Warning Notice’ to the prospective buyer and their solicitor. This must be issued at least 7 days before exchange of contracts, detailing the exemption and where necessary providing further evidence of compliance. The minimum 7-day period will provide the prospective buyer with the opportunity to ask questions and seek further evidence if required.
C. Registration: Assurance for first and subsequent buyers will be provided through the land registration system. All new long leases of flats registered with His Majesty’s Land Registry (HMLR), will have to include a new ‘Prescribed Statement’ confirming their compliance with the ban. Failure to comply will trigger a temporary restriction on title, preventing the flat from being resold until the property’s status is resolved.
D. Redress: Buyers will have rights to redress if mis-sold a leasehold flat in breach of the ban. Remedies will vary depending on the nature of the breach:
if a consumer was unlawfully granted a lease in a commonhold building, they will have the right to obtain the commonhold unit, at no cost to them
if one or multiple flat owners were unlawfully sold a leasehold flat in a relevant building, they will generally have the right to require the building be converted to commonhold and to acquire their flat as a commonhold unit, at no cost to them
if a consumer acquired a leasehold flat that qualified for a legitimate exemption, but the required evidence was not provided, they will have the right to have this corrected in the lease at the landlord’s expense
if the landlord contests the redress right, or is otherwise missing or non-responsive, the consumer will have the right to apply to the appropriate tribunal to determine the appropriate outcome
where a lease is granted in breach of the ban, a limited right to terminate the unlawful lease will be available for the small number of cases where standard redress may not be available or suitable
E. Enforcement: will occur through financial penalties, scaled according to the level of consumer harm. For example, wrongly advertising a new leasehold flat would incur a lower penalty than completing a prohibited sale. The enforcement of the ban will be led by a lead enforcement authority appointed by the Secretary of State.
142. Our view is that banning existing leasehold flats would severely impact the rights of leaseholders and their landlords. Instead, we are making it easier for people to convert their existing leasehold building to commonhold, should they wish to do so. We do not agree that bringing forward a modern and improved homeownership model in the form of commonhold will create a two-tier market.
143. There are around 5 million leasehold dwellings (flats and houses) in England and Wales and leaseholds will continue to be a feature of the housing market for many years to come. That is why we are also improving the rights, powers and protections for existing leaseholders. This includes tackling ground rents, abolishing forfeiture, and making service charges more transparent and easier to challenge. It also includes helping leaseholders to take control of their buildings by making enfranchisement and the right to manage more accessible. The government has also committed to regulate property agents through an independent statutory regulator, giving leaseholders, tenants and homebuyers a route towards better rights and protections, including those taking on responsibility for managing their homes who rely on managing agents, as well as those moving to commonhold.
144. We also received a small number of responses on technical points of detail. Some suggested that the warning notice period as part of the ‘sales’ stage of the ban (see Box 8, B) was too short and did not give sufficient time for buyers to make additional queries. We want to reassure consumers that there will be enough time to make enquiries. The design of the legislation means that the developer cannot grant the lease any quicker than 7 days of having issued the warning notice to the buyer, and they cannot grant the lease until the buyer or their solicitor has provided a notice of receipt. This puts the timeline for making any further enquiries or clarifications firmly in the hands of the buyer and their solicitor. Nevertheless, we do not expect this to materially delay transactions. Few transactions occur within 7 days, and a warning notice can be included in wider sales packs sent to consumers and their solicitors. We will publish a copy of the warning notice as part of our package of regulations.
145. A few respondents wanted to see stronger financial penalties for breaches as part of the ban (see Box 8, E). We consider the financial penalties proposed to be both a proportionate and robust deterrent. The fines scale in accordance with the severity of the offence, and multiply in accordance with the number of offences committed by the seller. So, the mis-sale of one leasehold flat could attract a penalty of £30,000, but the mis-sale of a whole block of ten could leave the vendor with a bill of £300,000. Critically, there will be nothing for the seller to gain. The redress system will entitle consumers to acquire what they should have been sold in the first place. In most cases a commonhold flat in a commonhold block and any legal costs associated with exercising redress rights can be claimed back from the seller.
146. To ensure there are no unintended consequences to the redress provisions, we have also introduced a limited right to terminate an unlawful lease. This is intended to deal with a small number of situations where the proposed redress would not be suitable, for example where land cannot be registered as commonhold or where a leaseholder grants an unlawful sublease out of a lawful lease and the standard redress could unfairly deprive the freeholder who has complied with the ban.
147. The UK government confirms that it will retain the design and structure of the ban as proposed in the consultation and draft Bill (as at Box 8) and these measures have been taken forwards into the substantive Bill.
Timetable for moving to commonhold
148. The UK government is committed to making commonhold the default tenure for new leasehold flats, and we want to see consumers enjoying the benefits of commonhold in new buildings as soon as possible.
149. We were clear in our White Paper published last year that this consultation on moving away from leasehold to commonhold would consider carefully how to minimise disruption to housing supply. The UK government remains committed to its stretching target to deliver 1.5 million new homes in England over the Parliament, and we want to see the supply of new flats playing a vital part in that effort.
150. The goals of increasing housing supply and a better homeownership offer are not in conflict. They can and should reinforce one another. The leasehold system has long been associated with complexity, unfair practices, and market friction, issues that have deterred buyers, lenders, and also developers. By promoting a fairer and more transparent tenure in the form of commonhold, we have the opportunity to restore confidence in the flat buying market, and over the longer-term unlock greater demand for, and investment in, new flats.
151. Commonhold-type arrangements will be familiar for many of the major housebuilders who operate in Scotland, in parts of Europe or further afield alongside their operations in England and Wales. For those that do not, they will nevertheless likely have experience selling blocks of flats on a share of freehold basis, where resident management companies are established and in charge of decision-making from the outset.
152. Developers and supporting parts of the sector will, however, be less familiar with the more technical aspects of the new England and Wales commonhold regime and understandably will need time to familiarise themselves with the reformed commonhold model and adjust accordingly.
153. There will also be some developers who have either started selling leaseholds on a site when the ban is commenced or own a plot of leased land. Changing such sites immediately to commonhold could be straightforward depending on the nature of the development, or may be more difficult. For these reasons, as well as carefully listening to industry on the timetable for moving to commonhold, we also sought views from industry through the consultation about the treatment of projects underway when the ban is commenced, so we can put in place transitional arrangements that encourage the market to move quickly to commonhold, without interrupting the construction and sale of much-needed new homes.
Staging posts to commonhold by default
154. To support a smooth transition across the housing market, the UK government intends to proceed with care and in a logical sequence towards making commonhold the default tenure for flats. This direction of travel has been clear and consistent, signalled through our manifesto commitments in June 2024, the publication of our Written Ministerial Statement on leasehold and commonhold reform in November 2024, and followed by the Commonhold White Paper in March 2025 and the Moving to Commonhold consultation in January 2026. These documents set out our ambition for commonhold to become the default model for new flats.
155. Alongside the consultation in January 2026, we also published our proposed commonhold reforms in the Draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill. This was to help consumers and industry stakeholders to understand how commonhold will operate in practice, and to begin preparing for its adoption. Publishing the draft Bill for pre-legislative scrutiny has also enabled Parliament, consumers and industry to consider the detail of the reforms and where necessary suggest refinements, helping to ensure commonhold can work across a wide range of developments. We are most grateful to members of the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee for their detailed and considered review of the draft Bill, as well as to the many stakeholders who submitted evidence to them.
156. Following this period of scrutiny, we have responded to the Committee’s recommendations alongside the publication of this consultation response and the formal introduction of the new commonhold legislation into Parliament. The UK government will implement the ban on new leasehold flats only after the new commonhold framework, including any necessary regulations, has been brought into force. We sought views through the consultation about the appropriate timing thereafter, for the ban on new leasehold flats to come into force, which we set out below.

Separately, lenders also submitted helpful written evidence to the HCLG select committee calling for cautious, phased transition. Lenders are a crucial part of a functioning market. 167. A common developer reason for a longer transition was the need for assurance that other parts of the sector on which they rely would also be sufficiently prepared, including lenders, legal professionals, property agents and consumers. Some developers also explained that they gave a more cautious estimate on the basis that they wished to see more detail on the final commonhold design.
168. Five years was also favoured by a number of housing associations, though the sector’s most commonly-expressed preference (like that of local authorities) was for ‘other’. For housing associations, the choice of ‘other’ was generally expressed on the basis that they wanted more information on how commonhold would work in practice before reaching a judgement on what an appropriate transition period would be. Views amongst the local authorities opting for ‘other’ varied, with a couple suggesting a three-year period, and the remainder not committing to a preferred date.
Phased implementation of the ban
169. We recognise that the complexity of transitioning to commonhold may differ across types of development. For example, some respondents suggested that the ban could start with smaller blocks that are generally simpler to manage and move to larger more complex blocks once familiarity with commonhold has increased. Others suggested that it may be better to have a single point from which commonhold is required to support implementation and avoid confusion for consumers and industry.
170. A single implementation date for the ban would offer clarity and simplicity for consumers, industry and enforcement bodies. Through the consultation we signalled that we were open to considering a phased approach, for instance starting with smaller, simpler developments and gradually expanding to more complex sites. This would be with a view to helping build momentum for commonhold uptake, while allowing additional time for parts of the market that face greater challenges to make the transition.
171. Responses to the consultation showed contrasting views between consumers and the property sector. Among consumer respondents, 9 in 10 called for the ban to commence at a single point in time, while nearly 6 in 10 in the property industry called for it to be staggered (see Table 7).

There were also some calls for third-party determination to consider the circumstances of developments on a case-by-case basis, such as by making an application to the property tribunal.
Flats built on historical leasehold land
184. Commonhold can only be built on freehold land. We know most developers acquire land on a freehold basis. Owning the land on a lease restricts a developer’s ability to construct and sell the homes on the site with the flexibility they would enjoy if they owned it on a freehold basis.
185. We note that in more limited cases, development sites may be acquired on a leasehold basis, and that there are circumstances where the freehold cannot be acquired, such as where the freeholder cannot be identified, or will not release the land on a freehold basis. These are distinct from a “building lease” where a lease is used to require the timely build out of a development, but which falls away so that freehold titles are provided to the consumer on first sale.
186. Therefore, in the consultation, we stated that we were minded that where a landowner owns land on a leasehold basis prior to the commencement of the ban and has firm plans for the development of leasehold flats upon it, that they should not be captured by the ban. This would allow the delivery of those homes to proceed. We do not, however, want to encourage the stockpiling of leased land, and nor do we think such an exemption should be open ended and allow for the provision of leasehold homes on the site long into the future.
187. We therefore asked consultees what conditions should be applied to owners of leased land to qualify for an exemption to the ban. Response rates were relatively low, with the property sector marginally favouring that the leased land has been acquired by a certain date and that the site has planning consent (see Table 9). Consumers on the other hand signalled first a preference for ‘other’ (the clear majority of whom wrote that there should not be an exemption for leased land), and secondly for fixed time periods.

Box 9
Proposed timing and transition for the ban:
A. The ban will commence on a single date after a proposed period of two and a half years following the commencement of both primary and secondary commonhold legislation;
B. An exemption will be provided to allow live developments prior to the ban commencing to continue to have the option to use leasehold for new flats subject to a live planning application made or planning consent that has been granted prior to commencement.
C. To ensure consumers are offered the best possible leasehold, buyers acquiring a home under this exemption must be offered a term of at least 990 years, or if the developer only has a lease, the maximum term permitted under their lease.
192. We believe this approach to the timing of the commencement of the ban on leasehold for new flats, coupled with the transitional measures, strikes the right balance. It will enable consumers to benefit from commonhold by making it the default for new flats at an appropriate pace, while minimising disruption to new housing supply.
A single commencement date rather than staggering the ban, will provide clarity to consumers and industry, and reduce the risk of unintended breaches of the ban.
Allowing the market two and a half years once the reformed commonhold regulations have come into force will provide time for both consumers and industry to familiarise themselves with the new regime and prepare for the ban, as well as time to draw on the experience of early adopters to the new commonhold regime from those providing commonhold homes and services in advance of the ban.
An exemption for sites with a planning application made or planning permission granted prior to commencement of the ban will provide the property development market the necessary flexibilities to continue developments that are already in train, while switching their pipelines to commonhold ahead of the ban where they wish to do so. We will provide further detail in due course as to which types of planning applications and consents will be covered by this exemption.
193. Recognising that tenure is often fixed early in the design of a site, and that requiring a tenure change in a live project may cause significant disruption to a development, providing an exemption for developers where a planning application is made prior to the ban being brought into force will protect the pipeline of homes, while allowing a smooth transition to commonhold. This will also cover situations envisaged in the consultation where developers had sold flats off-plan, and so will be able to complete these homes as leasehold.
194. This exemption will also apply where the developer only has a leasehold interest in the land. On the basis that a developer must be able to demonstrate a clear and firm commitment to development on that site, where developers have a leased land interest, they will need to make a planning application prior to the commencement of the ban if they wish to sell leasehold flats after the ban has come into force. This condition makes a discrete exemption for leased land unnecessary, and so the UK government determines therefore, that there will be no separate exemption to the ban on new leasehold flats for leased land.
195. Finally, following feedback from consumers that acquiring the freehold interest and converting to commonhold should be made as easy as possible to ease the path to commonhold and minimise enfranchisement costs, developers will have to offer consumers the best lease terms they can offer in order to benefit from the planning exemption. Where a developer owns the freehold of the site, the exemption will require that a lease term of at least 990 years be granted. Where the developer only holds a leasehold interest, the consumer must be granted a lease with the maximum term permitted by the developer’s lease.
Further refinements to commonhold
196. The Law Commission’s proposals for reinvigorating commonhold were developed following extensive consultation with industry and stakeholders. These recommendations, which we are now taking forward, lay the groundwork for commonhold to be adopted much more widely across the housing market.
197. Following the Commonhold White Paper in March last year, the publication of the Draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill in January provided stakeholders with a detailed view of how the reformed commonhold framework will operate. The Bill introduces new flexibilities designed to support adoption across a broad range of development types and ownership products. However, the Law Commission’s work was undertaken in the context that commonhold would remain optional. The UK government recognises that mandating commonhold as the default tenure for new flats represents a significant shift in policy. In light of this change, we sought views through the consultation as to whether any further technical refinements to the draft legislation may be needed to ensure commonhold works effectively.
198. Our aim remains to limit the number of exemptions to the ban so as to maximise the number of consumers who will stand to benefit from commonhold. We therefore consider that making any necessary adjustments to the commonhold laws to provide further flexibility will reduce the need for exemptions in certain circumstances, and support broader, more inclusive adoption.
199. A small number of respondents offered suggestions around minor technical changes or recommended best practice. These included calls for standardised documentation and forms to improve transparency for commonhold owners and prospective buyers, such as financial information concerning the annual budget. There was also a desire to mirror, where possible, improvements around the provision of information for leaseholders being implemented following measures in the 2024 Act (and the Strengthening Leaseholder Protections over Charges and Services consultation).
200. Other suggestions focused on the need for comprehensive guidance, for example on local rule changes within a commonhold, to provide clarity around scope, processes, and redress.
201. The UK government agrees that the timely provision of accessible, comprehensive and standardised information for owners and prospective buyers of commonhold properties is crucial for the smooth running and management of commonhold buildings, and can help to support collective decision making and minimise disputes. We will work with the Leasehold Advisory Service and other stakeholders to provide advice and guidance on how to set, interpret and modify commonhold rulebooks. The Bill also creates a general right for unit-holders to request key sales information from Commonhold Associations when selling their property in a standardised format, for a capped fee, and within a set timeframe. This will help prospective buyers to make informed decisions when seeking to purchase a commonhold unit. We will work with consumers and industry to design the content and format of standardised information requests for consumers through secondary legislation.
202. A number of respondents also called for sector-specific adaptations to the commonhold framework to tailor it to their specific needs. This included many responses relating to housing for older people, discussed above, and the provision of affordable or social housing, discussed below. Other responses related to the treatment of smaller blocks with very few units, which we discuss further in relation to our proposals around ‘micro-commonholds’.
Supporting use in the social housing sector
203. We want to extend the benefits of commonhold ownership as widely as possible, including within the social housing sector.
204. The UK government is committed to delivering the biggest increase in social and affordable housebuilding in a generation and supporting councils and housing associations to make a greater contribution to affordable housing. In addition, we are going further on council housebuilding as the Prime Minister has committed to deliver the biggest council housebuilding programme since the post-war boom ended in the early 1980s.
205. This will mean both many more homes in general and more in mixed-tenure developments. The new commonhold model has specifically been designed to cater for more complex buildings and sites, including those which incorporate a range of uses and tenures, to support affordable housing and blocks with homes for rent. Measures in the Bill will provide greater flexibilities to apportion costs and delegate decisions, open the model up to use of shared ownership, and provide rights and powers for both providers and tenants to influence the governance of buildings. It also provides new routes to redress if things go wrong.
206. The consultation received a number of detailed responses from, or on behalf of, registered providers of social housing, including both local authorities and housing associations. Providers were broadly supportive of the move to commonhold and noted that it aligns closely with values that the social housing sector seeks to uphold: transparency, resident involvement, and empowerment. Understandably, given the false start to the original commonhold model and live changes to the regime through the Bill and secondary legislation to follow, a number of registered providers cited the need to become more familiar with the detail of the new commonhold regime to understand its practical implications.
207. Some concerns were raised about providers’ ability under commonhold to protect their tenants’ interests and meet regulatory requirements in a mixed-tenure building where decision-making is shared across all the unit owners in a block. A recurring issue was the perception that lay directors may not have sufficient expertise to safely and efficiently manage commonholds, especially large and complex sites and buildings that fall under the Building Safety regime. Other key concerns included the risk that a commonhold association could decide to ‘gold-plate’ budgets and unnecessarily raise costs or fail to oversee the required pace or standard of repair for tenants and be compliant with consumer standards overseen by the Regulator of Social Housing. There were also queries about potential administrative burdens, for example if providers were obliged to attend all annual general meetings of commonholds across their portfolio.
208. We recognise that the new commonhold model requires careful design and implementation to meet the needs of registered providers, however to an extent we are able to build on existing arrangements and practices. Many registered providers of social housing already own units within blocks where some level of resident control is exercised. This can occur through a share of freehold arrangement, either following a collective enfranchisement or where a new build property has been provided without a third-party landlord, or where residents have exercised the Right to Manage. Here, providers already navigate practical, administrative, financial and regulatory implications of being part of a building in which they share decision-making with residents.
209. A clear advantage of commonhold over these leasehold arrangements is that it has been specifically designed to manage blocks without a third-party landlord and has purpose-built processes to involve all relevant parties in key decisions such as the approval of budgets. It also offers minority protections which safeguard owners’ individual interests and enable decisions to be challenged.
210. The reformed commonhold model has a number of features which will address some of the specific concerns outlined above. Measures in the Bill will give providers the opportunity to be a director of a commonhold association themselves alongside their fellow unit-holders. They will also be able to step in and require via the property tribunal that a professional director is appointed should a position be left unfilled, or apply for a director who is failing in their obligations to be replaced. Providers’ level of influence on decision-making within a commonhold will also scale in proportion to the number of units they own. It is our expectation that in the vast majority of commonholds, especially those of a more complex nature, a professional managing agent would be appointed by the commonhold association to assist with the management and potentially also the governance of the building.
211. To curb excessive charges for costs relating to alterations, improvements or enhanced services, unit owners can choose to set an index-linked threshold on the amount of expenditure that could be incurred annually. This can help to prevent excessive costs being charged for superficial improvements or services which are beyond what is expected. Unit-holders will also have the ability to challenge any expenditure above their set threshold. We anticipate that as providers become more familiar with the new commonhold regime, or when they adopt new ways of working, concerns around burdens of engaging in activities such as a commonhold annual general meeting will become subsumed into the engagement and oversight they already undertake across buildings within their portfolios.
212. There was no strong call among respondents to the consultation for an exemption from the ban for social housing. The UK government believes that commonhold will be well suited for the needs of homeowners, tenants and providers of social housing. We do, however, recognise specific needs of the sector and the vital importance of safeguarding tenants’ interests. That is why we have made new provisions in the Bill to provide some additional flexibilities for providers. This includes a power for the Secretary of State to provide adapted articles of association and Commonhold Community Statements for commonholds with a certain proportion of social housing. In these settings, we intend for providers to be given a ‘right of first refusal’ on director positions, and for the commonhold rulebook to mandate compliance with relevant social housing standards. We are keen to continue to work with social housing providers to shape the detail of these proposals, as well as to support a smooth transition to commonhold for the sector.
Professional management for commonhold.
213. Managing agents play a key role in the maintenance of multi-occupancy buildings. Their importance will only increase as commonhold becomes the default tenure for new flats and more leaseholders exercise their Right to Manage, collectively enfranchise, or convert to commonhold.
214. While self-management by lay directors may be suitable for small or simple buildings, stakeholders have suggested that more complex sites may require professional directors or agents to ensure effective governance and compliance. For buildings, subject to specific legal obligations under the Building Safety Act 2022 in England (separate requirements apply for Wales under the Building Safety (Wales) Act), we might also reasonably expect commonhold associations to engage specialist managing agents with fire safety expertise.
215. International approaches vary. For example, in Victoria, Australia, building managers are required for certain larger strata buildings (although these managers can be volunteers, and owners’ corporations can opt out of this requirement by special resolution), while in Western Australia their use is at the discretion of the building owners.
216. Through the consultation we sought views on the role of professional managing agents and whether there were any types of commonhold development that should be required to appoint professional managing agents as directors or managers of the building.
217. Among respondents, 6 in 10 considered that certain types of commonholds should be required to appoint professional managing agents, while 4 in 10 respondents disagreed (see Table 10). There was a considerable difference between respondent groups with 9 in 10 property sector respondents supporting such a requirement, compared with 4 in 10 consumers. In addition, a small number of those that disagreed did so on the basis of the currently unregulated market. They argued that requiring use of professional agents was a good idea in certain circumstances, but that it could be problematic to do so where minimum standards could not be assured.

Some suggested enhanced financial protections where larger sums of money may be involved concerning the annual budget, such as greater transparency regarding accounts or having requirements for client money protection. There were also calls for mandatory director training, or qualifications and specified levels of reserve fund contributions in complex buildings.
222. The UK government committed on 29 September to regulate professional managing agents and other property agents. This means a future regulatory framework potentially covering licensing, professional standards, monitoring compliance, investigations and enforcement. We think it is vital that homeowners that rely upon agents can be assured that they will provide a good service, adhere to a code of practice setting expectations and minimum service standards, and hold qualifications to practice. They should also have better rights and protections, including mechanisms through which agents may be effectively sanctioned for poor service or have their license taken away for serious or repeated breaches.
223. The new commonhold framework provides flexibilities for professional managing agents to be employed to both assist with the management and governance of buildings, and commonhold associations to have the power to collectively decide to appoint, replace, or dismiss managing agents as suits their needs. It is our expectation that most commonhold associations will choose to employ managing agents. Should a commonhold association fail to appoint sufficient directors, the tribunal can appoint a professional agent to undertake the role of director, who would also most likely appoint a managing agent to undertake management functions for the commonhold.
For new builds, we anticipate that developers of most new commonhold developments, particularly larger or more complex ones, will have managing agents put in place from the outset (though of course, the commonhold unit owners may choose to replace a managing agent or make their own appointment at a later point), as is common practice in -share of freehold arrangements.
224. The UK government thinks that it should be for homeowners to determine whether they or their building would benefit from professional assistance, though through guidance we will recommend that it would be beneficial especially on larger or more complex sites to employ or retain the services of a professional managing agent.
Micro-commonholds
225. Small buildings are typically simpler to manage, as they will involve fewer shared areas and infrastructure. Other jurisdictions which use commonhold-like models do not apply a one-size-fits-all approach, and we were conscious that some of the requirements of the general commonhold legal framework could be considered unduly onerous for very small, simple buildings (for example, those involving 4 or fewer units, where the only shared areas may be a hallway and parking). We have termed such developments as ‘micro-commonholds’.
226. Through the consultation we sought views on whether any obligations of the commonhold association should be disapplied or made voluntary for micro-commonholds so that they can consciously opt-out.
227. For example, under the articles of association that apply for all commonhold associations, a commonhold association is required to have a minimum of two directors. This is potentially onerous for a commonhold that has only a very small number of units. It might be more pragmatic to allow micro-commonholds to opt to be run with a single director (as is the case with other companies).
228. Among respondents, 7 out of 10 agreed with our proposed definition of ‘micro-commonhold’ as four or fewer residential units, whilst 3 out of 10 disagreed (see Table 11). Of those who disagreed, we asked them to consider what the preferred number of units a ‘micro-commonhold’ should have. The feedback was inconclusive, but the strongest preference was for ‘other’.
229. The written feedback underpinning these preferences illustrated a range of views. A common point of feedback was that there should be no distinction made for smaller commonholds; all obligations should equally apply to all commonholds, and that they all be managed and run to the same standards. Another view held by those who disagreed thought that there should be a distinction made to disapply obligations for lower maintenance commonholds, but the distinction be made based upon complexity of the building rather than the number of units. The remaining qualitative responses largely agreed with the proposal for a micro-commonhold distinction, but based on a definition of a different number of units than the 4 or fewer proposed.

We will also keep under review the need to add, amend or withdraw any of the specified flexibilities and make further changes via regulations if necessary.
Understanding implementation costs and benefits
233. Through the consultation, the UK government was also interested in understanding what activities industry would need to undertake to prepare for commonhold, and any potential costs and efficiencies that might arise from moving from leasehold to commonhold in new developments.
234. The evidence from the consultation and engagement with industry has helped to inform both policy and the Impact Assessment. This assessment will accompany the Bill, and set out the government’s analysis of the expected costs and benefits, together with the evidence and assumptions underpinning that assessment.
235. We are well aware that industry will need to familiarise itself with how the new commonhold regime works, and some may need to update various aspects of their processes, policies, training and guidance both to accommodate the new commonhold tenure, and in order to comply with the requirements of the ban on new leasehold flats. We also know that parts of the housing market will already be familiar with commonhold-like models. For example, where they operate in non-domestic markets, or will be familiar with resident-led buildings through working on share of freehold buildings.
236. Questions in this section of the consultation were aimed at those involved in the development, marketing, sale of new blocks in the past 3 years. Among these respondents, 7 in 10 said that they or their organisation would need to implement new or update existing systems for the use of commonhold, whilst 3 in 10 respondents suggested that they would not need to substantially change existing practices or prepare (see Table 12).

Of those responding, 7 in 10 reported that they would. The most common theme of written responses being the need for legal advice, followed by accountancy or financial advice. Around 6 in 10 respondents suggested that they would, while around 4 in 10 did not. Specifics on preparatory costs were generally not provided. Around half of written responses suggested costs would arise without providing detail about what they were. Of those who provided further detail, a key theme was that documentation and marketing materials would need to be updated to align with the ban, followed by updates to contracts and corporate processes.
241. Some respondents added that they expected any time or costs associated with preparing for use of commonhold would be up-front only or transitional. They suggested that, once implemented, commonhold would be part of a new way of working or service offer, and would not inherently cost more or take longer to process than with use of leasehold. An example cited by a legal professionals was the need for updates to existing IT systems (rather than an entirely new bespoke commonhold IT system). These respondents also suggested that further updates might be limited to internal guidance and knowledge management tools, which could be achieved with existing systems and resourcing.
242. We appreciate that the time and cost of preparing for commonhold will vary between different parts of the property sector, and even between different organisations within the same sector. It should also be noted that universal readiness across the property sector is an unnecessarily high bar for commonhold to be successfully supported. Today, in the leasehold market, not every lender offers finance on leasehold properties, and not every conveyancer has the specialist skills to support a leasehold transaction.
243. Respondents were also asked if they thought any efficiencies or longer-term savings might arise from the adoption of commonhold. Around 4 in 10 respondents thought that there would be, while 6 in 10 did not. The written feedback noted certain transitional and dual-running costs in implementing commonhold at the same time as continuing to support leaseholders, reflecting the feedback of others that if savings do occur, it may take time for these to materialise as industry and consumers familiarise themselves with how the new system works. Where sources of savings were identified in written responses, these stemmed from the use of standardised documentation, including the Commonhold Community Statement (or CCS), rather than use of individual and complex leases, and that the transparent and democratic features of commonhold could result in greater control over service costs, help avoid disputes, or the escalation of disputes and the costs associated with these.
244. The need for time to update systems, processes and procedures, as well in some cases more significant changes to IT systems and staff training has been heard clearly and factored into our consideration of the timing of the ban. The importance of implementation was also echoed by the Housing, Communities and Local Government Select Committee’s scrutiny of the draft Bill, who flagged the importance of preparing both consumers and the market for use of the new commonhold regime.
Ban on new leasehold houses
245. The 2024 Act includes a ban on the grant of new long residential leases of houses. This is an important part of the UK government’s wider programme to bring the leasehold system to an orderly end, by ensuring that new houses are provided on a freehold basis wherever possible and that leasehold is used only where there is a clear and justified reason.
246. We remain committed to implementing the 2024 Act ban on new leasehold houses. Freehold tenure is already the established default for new houses, and in recent years the market has already moved substantially away from the provision of new leasehold houses. The ban will lock in that shift, prevent the re-emergence of unfair leasehold practices for new houses, and provide clarity to consumers that other than in exceptional circumstances, new houses should be sold as freehold.
247. It is important that the house ban should operate effectively alongside the ban on the use of leasehold for new flats. Aligning the structure of the two bans where appropriate will make the regime simpler to understand for both consumers and industry and reduce the risk of error, opportunity for evasion, or properties falling between the two bans, and support a clear move away from leasehold for new homes. For that reason, the UK government will make changes in the Bill to streamline the operation of the house ban. In particular, we will remove the role of the tribunal in pre-certifying certain house ban exemptions. As with the ban on new leasehold flats, house ban compliance will instead be demonstrated through the marketing, warning notice and registration stages, backed by enforcement and redress for any breaches. This will avoid unnecessary additional pressure on the tribunal system and reduce the risk of delay for legitimate developments, while retaining clear checks to protect consumers.
248. The 2024 Act also did not include detailed provisions for redress where houses had been mis-sold contrary to the ban. Measures have now been provided in the Bill, aligned with the approach for the flat ban, allowing the consumer the right in most cases to acquire the freehold at no cost to them. Mirroring flat ban provisions, the Bill also provides consumers the right to correct a wrongly-recorded permitted lease, and the right to terminate their lease in certain narrow circumstances.
249. Through the Bill, the UK government will also adjust the exemptions included in the 2024 Act so that, where appropriate, they are consistent across the two bans, in particular the exemption for historical leased land. At present, the 2024 Act provides an exemption for historical leasehold sites. That current exemption is open-ended, and so the exemption could in theory be used many decades into the future. The government is concerned that this will unnecessarily prolong the sale of new leasehold houses on legacy leasehold land, and so will therefore retain an exemption in the 2024 Act for houses built on historical leased land, but narrow its application.
250. To bring the exemption into closer alignment with that of the flat ban, the exemption will only be available where the leased land has a planning application submitted or consent granted before the commencement of the ban. This will ensure that the exemption is targeted at real development pipelines, rather than providing an open-ended route for new leasehold houses to be brought forward long into the future. We will also ensure that on such sites, consumers are given a lease term equivalent to that held by the developer, to minimise the costs of a future freehold acquisition.
251. There will also be divergence between exemptions to the two bans. There will be certain sites where it is not possible to register a commonhold, for instance, where land granted under the School Sites Act 1841 reverts to freeholder once it ceases to be used for a school. Because of the complexities this poses to buildings in multiple freehold ownership, the ban on new leasehold flats will provide a narrow exemption so that leases can still be used in these circumstances. No equivalent restrictions exist for the registration of houses, and so an exemption is not necessary. A list of exemptions for both the house and flat bans is provided at Annex B.
252. Despite some narrow, appropriate divergences between the bans on new leasehold houses and new leasehold flats, it is intended that they will function in lockstep, so that in future in nearly all circumstances houses for sale will be provided on a freehold basis and new flats provided on a commonhold basis. In the interests of clarity and simplicity, the UK government therefore intend to commence the ban on new leasehold houses at the same time as the ban on new leasehold flats.
Next steps
253. The UK government has carefully considered the responses to this consultation, and associated feedback received as part of the pre-legislative scrutiny process. This has been used to inform our approach to final legislation, as set out in the Bill, and will also inform the detail and planning in support of the necessary regulations to follow.
254. We will continue to work with industry and consumer groups to ensure that developers, lenders and property professionals are continuing to prepare for commonhold to become the standard tenure for new supply.
255. As set out in the Commonhold White Paper, we would also encourage property professionals to start thinking now about how they will support consumers through the process of buying, owning or managing a new commonhold property. In particular, they should consider which processes need to be changed, and how they will ensure people in consumer-facing roles have sufficient knowledge and training. As we said alongside publication of the draft Bill and publication of this consultation, now is the right time to start preparing for commonhold by default. Following the publication of the Bill and this consultation response, the impetus is even greater.
256. Commonholds can be built and sold now but we do not expect supply to increase significantly until we have made the necessary changes to the legal framework. The Bill was introduced into Parliament on 19 November 2026. Secondary legislation will also be required. We cannot pre-empt the Parliamentary process, but it is the intention that the reformed commonhold model will come into force before the end of this Parliament in 2029. As we have set out in this UK government response, it is intended that the ban on the use of leasehold for new flats, subject to defined exemptions, will commence two and a half years after the commonhold regulations come into force, and we will work with consumers and industry to ensure a smooth transition. The ban will therefore commence in approximately 2031. We intend to set out an implementation roadmap closer to Royal Assent of the Bill which will set out further detail on timing of the secondary legislation and wider implementation activities.
Implementation planning
257. The UK government recognises the importance of getting the details right and preparing for a smooth transition to commonhold. We are hugely grateful for the underpinning evidence gathered by the Law Commission through engagement with consumers and industry in the preparation of their report on reinvigorating the commonhold framework. We are also grateful for the many individual and group discussions over a number of years between Ministers, officials, and both consumer and industry representatives, as well as delivery partners across government and the Welsh Government, on the detailed design of the new commonhold framework. We want to continue this conversation and collaborative approach as we turn to implementation planning (see Box 10 below).
258. During the intended two-and-a-half-year transitional period where the new commonhold regime is available, yet still optional before the ban commences, we would encourage as many new developments as possible to switch to commonhold. We will also work with partners such as Homes England and established strategic authorities to accelerate delivery of new commonhold developments in place of leasehold supply during this period where possible.
259. To ensure the new commonhold model can be used as soon as the secondary regulations come into force, the UK government is already working with key delivery partners including HM Land Registry, HM Courts & Tribunal Service, Companies House, and the Leasehold Advisory Service (LEASE) to ensure that the supporting infrastructure and processes needed for the greater use of commonhold is available on day one of the new model being available for use. This also includes consideration of the information, guidance, and support that consumers will require from government delivery partners to successfully navigate the new regime.
260. To support both consumers and industry to prepare for the optional use of commonhold following its reintroduction, and its use by default following the commencement of the ban, the UK government will publish an ‘Implementation Roadmap’ closer to Royal Assent of the Bill. This will set out a timetable for introduction of the necessary secondary legislation as well as wider support and guidance that will be made available for consumers and industry.
261. Alongside this consultation response and introduction of the Bill we have also published our ‘Direction of Travel’ plan. This sets out the implementation activities that are already underway to support the move to commonhold. These include the production of a statutory Code of Practice for Secretary of State Approval on commonhold contributions by the Royal Institute of Chartered Surveyors, work with The Conveyancing Association and other legal and property sector stakeholders to produce standardised commonhold sales information forms, and new training for property managers on the new commonhold regime led by organisations such as The Property Institute.
262. In addition, the UK government will establish a ‘Commonhold Implementation Steering Group’ chaired by the Housing Minister and comprising representatives of both industry and consumers. The group will help inform the development of the government’s ‘Implementation Roadmap’ as well as working across the sector to support the development of sector-led information, guidance, and support.

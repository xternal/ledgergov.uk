The Secretary of State for Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Affairs (Ed Miliband)
With permission, I will make a statement on Myanmar.
On 19 November, the International Court of Justice found that the Myanmar regime had committed genocide against the Rohingya people. The judgment represents the first finding of state responsibility for genocide since the 1948 convention on the prevention and punishment of the crime of genocide, and it lays bare what the actions of the Myanmar regime meant for the Rohingya: indiscriminate and targeted killing of civilians including those fleeing their villages, summary executions, widespread sexual violence, and the burning of people and homes. The court concluded that
“the only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar’s actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part of the protected group, namely the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State.”
The UK’s long-standing approach is that any determination of genocide follows the reasoned judgment of a competent national or international court, after a credible judicial process. With the ICJ’s judgment that test has now been met, and today I can confirm that the United Kingdom formally acknowledges the genocide committed against the Rohingya. For this Government, and, I would argue, for the whole international community, our response to a crime as profoundly shocking as genocide must reflect our values, and must be matched by clear action. We should also act cognisant of the fact that the appalling targeting of civilians by the Myanmar regime continues to this day, as do the discrimination against and persecution of Rohingya and other communities. Airstrikes are happening regularly across the country, hitting schools, hospitals, markets and places of worship, and there is also ongoing violence on the part of the Arakan army, which it is right to call out.
This morning I met courageous Rohingya campaigners, who emphasised to me the importance that they attach to the recognition of what has happened, and the fact that they had felt too long ignored by the international community. Today I am setting out a package of measures, against the Myanmar military, in pursuit of accountability, in support of the Rohingya and the people of Myanmar, and to build international pressure on the regime to end the suffering.
Let me deal first with action against the Myanmar military. On Thursday we ordered the expulsion of Myanmar’s defence attaché, because we will not tolerate a serving representative of Myanmar’s military remaining in post on UK soil. I can announce today that we have also imposed sanctions against four Majors General and a Brigadier General—senior military figures who oversaw troops who carried out egregious civilian killings and repression of the population across Myanmar.
We know that the Myanmar military has systematically used lethal air power against its own people. I do not believe that the British people want any UK companies to have any links to such practices, so we will introduce new legislation in the coming months to stop the export and supply of aviation fuel to Myanmar which is used in such horrendous attacks. These are our initial steps, but we will also scrutinise all major companies in Myanmar, particularly those linked to the military regime, and we will take further sanctions action where it is justified to deter or disrupt violations of international law and to stand against impunity.
Secondly, the United Kingdom, as United Nations Security Council penholder on Myanmar, will use our diplomatic influence to increase international pressure for accountability and to end the humanitarian crisis. To that end we will raise this as an urgent issue at the Security Council this week, and we have called a dedicated Security Council meeting in two weeks’ time to strengthen momentum for action. We know that the Security Council has too often been divided on Myanmar, but division should not become an excuse for UN inaction.
ASEAN, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, also has a crucial role, and we have consistently welcomed and supported its resolve to secure peace in Myanmar. I want to work with ASEAN partners to go further to ensure that its five-point consensus brings an end to the violence, ensures humanitarian access and supports inclusive dialogue. We urge resolve among ASEAN countries to help ensure compliance with the ICJ’s ruling, and I have already been in touch with my ASEAN counterparts in the past few days. We will also work with other countries that have supported this ICJ case, including the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, whose 57 members strongly welcomed the ICJ judgment.
Thirdly, we will pursue accountability and justice. The ICJ judgment concerns Myanmar’s responsibility as a state, but we also need individual criminal accountability. Those responsible must know that neither rank nor the passage of time will put them beyond that, so we will step up support for survivors in their pursuit of justice. Today I can announce a £3 million package for victims and survivors of human rights violations across Myanmar to help that process, together with a doubling of our support for the UN’s international investigative mechanism for Myanmar, which has provided critical evidence supporting the ICJ’s judgment, as well as support for the organisation Legal Action Worldwide to ensure that survivor voices are heard.
Fourthly, we will stand with the Rohingya not only in their pursuit of justice, but in their daily struggle for safety, dignity and hope. In its judgment, the Court is clear about what must happen next: Myanmar must stop ongoing violations; investigate, prosecute and punish perpetrators; and prevent such atrocities from ever happening again. The Court also says that it must take forward reparation and restitution, which includes enabling Rohingya people to return and reunite with their families, with proper compensation. We agree, and today, together with Canada and a range of international partners, we have issued a joint statement demanding Myanmar’s full compliance with the ICJ judgment.
As Britain, we will also play our part in helping the Rohingya. Today I can announce an additional £5 million of humanitarian assistance to support approximately 350,000 people from both Rohingya and Rakhine communities. This will strengthen the protection of women and girls, support maternal healthcare, provide food and safe water, and treat acutely malnourished children. That support builds on the UK’s role as one of the largest humanitarian donors to Myanmar, already providing more than £55 million a year in lifesaving assistance. I think it important to note that we will continue to work with the Government of Bangladesh, who support more than 1.2 million refugees from Myanmar who have fled the violence.
Finally, and ultimately, we need a political path towards sustained peace—an imperative that I discussed earlier today with the UN Special Envoy on Myanmar, Julie Bishop. That means an immediate pause in hostilities to enable humanitarian aid to get through and a stop to airstrikes on civilians and the release of political prisoners, as well as dialogue towards an inclusive political settlement. I promise the House that in the weeks and months ahead, the UK will do everything we can to reinvigorate the political and diplomatic efforts to secure a just and lasting end to this appalling conflict.
Let me end by paying tribute to the tireless efforts of the many people across our society who have kept the flame of justice burning for the Rohingya, including Members of this House. I pay particular tribute to the work of the all-party parliamentary group on Burma and its chairs, my hon. Friend the Member for Bethnal Green and Stepney (Rushanara Ali) and the right hon. Member for Godalming and Ash (Sir Jeremy Hunt), and to the work of the all-party parliamentary group on Rohingya. Above all, I pay tribute to Rohingya survivors, including those living in the UK. Many have relived horrific experiences so that the truth could be recorded and justice pursued. They refused to allow the world to look away.
Our message today is clear. We recognise what was done as genocide. We support the demand for justice. We will not look away. We will take action. The genocide convention embodies a solemn promise made in the aftermath of humanity’s darkest crime. That promise means something only if judgments are respected and the gravest violations have consequences. At a time when international law is under sustained pressure, Britain will defend it, because this Government are determined to play their part in building a world based on values of humanity, dignity and self-determination for people across the world. I commend this statement to the House.

Genocide requires a specific intent to destroy a protected group as a whole or in substantial part. As was emphasised recently in a report from Policy Exchange, broadening the definition of genocide risks undermining the clarity or seriousness of this international crime, and as Ukraine’s former Foreign Minister Dmytro Kuleba argues in his foreword, the charge must meet the legal test if it is to retain its authority.
As the Foreign Secretary has said, this is the first time the ICJ has found a state directly responsible for committing genocide, but the Court divided 9-6 on that central finding. It concerns me greatly that the dissenting judges raised substantial questions about proof of intent, the standard of proof, and the treatment of evidence. Those questions deserve careful consideration, and a clear response from Ministers.
We do not believe that the Government should have intervened in this case, given the risk of encouraging a broader interpretation of genocide. The precision of international law matters, because its credibility depends on it.

Ed Miliband
I had hoped that today would be a day for bipartisan consensus across the House. Before I respond to the substance of the right hon. Lady’s remarks, let me say that I do not doubt her condemnation, and the condemnation of those on the Opposition Front Bench, of everything that was done by the Myanmar regime, but I deeply regret her questioning of the role of the ICJ in this case. Let me explain why.
It has been a long-standing view on both sides of the House that the determination of genocide is not a matter for us to call out as politicians before a competent court has reached a judgment. That is a very important linchpin of the approach, which I think has united both sides. The most competent court—the apex court: the International Court of Justice—has now reached its view. It is true that it is a majority view, but a majority view is the view of the Court, just like it would be in our courts, so I deeply regret the right hon. Lady’s seeking to unpick this judgment. I have read through the summary judgment in a lot of detail, as I am sure she has. I am not a lawyer, but I do not think that my job is to second-guess the judgment of the competent court. At a time when international law and international courts are under threat across the world, it is so important that we respect the Court, and let me explain why. If the British Government come along and say, “The ICJ may have ruled that, but we just disagree in this case,” it gives carte blanche to every other Government in the world to start saying, “The ICJ may have reached this conclusion, but we ignore the judgment of the ICJ.”
This is the first response. I urge those on the Conservative Front Bench, the shadow Foreign Secretary and his colleagues to think hard about what it means to uphold international law. I would have thought a basic requirement of that—given that the judges have spent a number of years examining all the evidence, looking at the legal arguments and coming to a conclusion—is to respect the judgment.

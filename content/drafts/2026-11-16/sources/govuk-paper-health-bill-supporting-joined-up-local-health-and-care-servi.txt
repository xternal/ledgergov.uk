Health Bill: supporting joined-up local health and care services - fact sheet
Background
On 31 July 2026, the Prime Minister’s Rewiring the state cabinet statement set out the government’s plan for devolution. Power, accountability and decision making will be shifted away from central government to local leaders, strategic authorities and place-based public services.
This builds on the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026, which introduces new measures to expand devolution and set out 9 areas of competence for strategic authorities, one of which is health, wellbeing and public service reform.
The Health Bill (‘the bill’) provisions also support devolution and integration, including:
strategic commissioning
neighbourhood health planning
data integration
stronger collaboration across the health and care system
As set out in the 10 Year Health Plan, improving health will require shifting from analogue to digital, hospital to community and from treatment to prevention. This will require closer links with the services that shape the wider determinants of health, such as housing, employment and the environment. The bill supports this by strengthening collaboration between local authorities, strategic authorities, the NHS and other partners, helping to create a more joined-up and locally led approach to improving population health.
Empowering local leadership and integrating local evidence into decision making
The bill creates a framework that enables effective planning at place level alongside strategic planning at integrated care board (ICB) level. In both cases this will require close collaboration between the NHS and its partners including local authorities and strategic authorities. Given the importance of achieving the ‘3 shifts’ and of managing need that is increasingly complex and long lasting as the population ages, this framework for greater collaboration is needed to ensure:
more effective use of public resources
better outcomes for patients, families and communities
Mayors and strategic authorities: strengthening local representation and leadership
The bill strengthens local leadership by reinforcing the role of ICBs working in partnership with local authorities and strategic authorities. Together, they will shape local priorities and allocate resources to meet the needs of their communities.
Mayoral strategic authorities will play a strengthened role in the local system. They will help to support strategic alignment with the areas they hold responsibility for, such as housing or transport. The bill requires ICBs to ensure that their board must have at least one member nominated by the mayor of each mayoral strategic authority in their area. The bill also continues the approach for each ICB to have at least one board member jointly nominated by all upper tier local authorities within the ICB footprint. This will help to ensure that an important expert voice on social care and public health will continue to inform ICB discussions. ‘Rewiring the state’ also commits to establishing deputy mayor roles for major public services, including health. Under the bill, mayors would be able to nominate their deputy mayors to sit on their local ICB board. The aim of this is to help ensure strong and effective links are made between the NHS and work on the wider determinants of health at a strategic level. In addition, an approach is being trialled in Greater Manchester and South Yorkshire where the deputy mayor is the chair of the ICB.
These arrangements will help to strengthen alignment between the NHS, local authorities and strategic authorities on shared priorities. It also builds on the new health improvement and health inequalities duty conferred on combined authorities and combined county authorities through the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026.
Planning
The current system contains multiple planning requirements, committees and forums, which can:
lead to duplication
blur accountability
encourage a focus on process rather than delivery
By removing the requirement to establish integrated care partnerships (ICPs) and their associated strategy and streamlining planning requirements, the bill creates a:
simpler planning framework
stronger emphasis on improving outcomes for local communities
Under the new framework, the joint strategic needs assessment (JSNA) will become the foundation for health and care planning. Health and wellbeing boards will continue to publish this. The JSNA will provide a comprehensive assessment of local health and wellbeing needs. This will ensure that decisions are informed by local evidence and democratic insight from the outset. It will inform both locally developed neighbourhood health plans and wider system-level population health improvement plans. This will help ensure that health and care priorities are shaped by the needs of local communities. It also ensures that there is a clearer link between local evidence, planning and delivery.
Creating conditions for greater local autonomy and partnership working
To support the shift from hospital to community, the bill gives local partners greater flexibility to work together and stronger mechanisms for integration. This aims to create the conditions needed for greater local autonomy and strengthened partnerships.
Enabling system wide partnership through section 75 arrangements
Section 75 of the NHS Act 2006 allows NHS bodies and local authorities to enter voluntary arrangements which can include pooling budgets and sharing staff and resources. This may be either while carrying out the functions of another partner or jointly where this is likely to improve how those functions are delivered.
The bill enables strategic authorities to enter section 75 arrangements to carry out their health-related functions in partnership with NHS bodies. This reflects the growing role strategic authorities will play in the wider determinants of health. It also supports greater alignment between health and care services and wider local priorities such as housing, transport, employment and economic development. This will create further opportunities to pool budgets, share resources and align public spending towards prevention and tackling health inequalities.
Alongside these changes, the bill will also expand the power to issue guidance on section 75 arrangements to support a more consistent and effective use across local systems. We know that section 75 partnerships have not always been straightforward to enter or use effectively. This guidance will support local partners to make use of tools for better integration.
Successful local partnership working case study: WorkWell in Coventry and Warwickshire
The bill and wider system reforms support closer partnership working between local organisations. This builds on approaches such as WorkWell, which brings NHS and local authority services together through section 75 partnership arrangements. WorkWell is a health and employment support service providing integrated holistic early help for people experiencing health-related barriers to work.
Coventry and Warwickshire’s WorkWell has integrated health, employment, housing and welfare support for people facing health-related barriers to work. A dedicated WorkWell work and health coach acts as a single point of contact, co-ordinating support across a wider range of services and partners, including:
local authority housing services, housing organisations and Department for Work and Pensions officials to co-ordinate accommodation, independent-living and financial support
mental health service providers to connect crisis support with longer-term care, including referrals and access to talking therapies
employers and specialist voluntary sector partner Rethink, who support with sickness processes, reasonable adjustments, alternative roles and longer-term return-to-work support
These partnerships help provide the participant with joined-up support to achieve greater mental wellbeing and help them to remain employed.
Encouraging more flexible and local partnership working
The bill supports a more flexible approach to local partnership working by removing the requirement to establish ICPs. This will encourage local partners to develop arrangements that work best for them and for the people they serve.
Currently there is a mandatory requirement for ICBs and local authorities to pool their service integration budgets, even where it may not be the most effective delivery mechanism. The bill will amend this to give the Secretary of State discretion to set whether this budget should be pooled or not on an annual basis. The change addresses the inflexibility of the current arrangements where ICBs and local authorities are mandated to pool their integrated budgets in all circumstances, even where it may not be the most effective delivery mechanism. The amendment introduces flexibility, allowing the Secretary of State to determine when pooling is necessary to achieve better outcomes.
In cases where service integration pooling is not mandated, local systems could have more flexibility and autonomy in the way that they collaborate on integration. This could allow them to determine how best to align resources and deliver joined-up services around the needs of their population, while recognising that integration can be achieved through a range of local arrangements rather than prescribing a single financial mechanism. Regardless of whether pooled budgets are mandated or not, local areas can still voluntarily pool additional funding for service integration under a section 75 agreement.
Single patient record - stronger partnership working through shared data access
Data integration is an essential precondition to more integrated services and stronger partnership working. Health and social care services can become more integrated by sharing records. Access to shared patient data will enable closer local partnerships and allow cross-cutting health issues to be more easily addressed through joined-up working across different settings.
Improved access to patient information across care settings will enable social care professionals to help deliver preventative care as part of an integrated community-based care package. For example, frailty is a major health issue cutting across multiple care settings, which can be managed and often prevented. It requires multidisciplinary care, which can be enabled through shared care records. It has been identified as one of the priority care pathways that will develop and deliver a single patient record.
Taken together, the changes provided for in the bill will create a framework for greater devolution and more effective integration between health, care, prevention and other sources of social and economic improvement. The framework is designed to give local and strategic organisations the space and the agency to shape improvements and services in the interests of local people. Improvements in outcomes will be driven primarily by local decisions, learning and innovation. The statutory framework seeks to create the best possible conditions for local organisations to work together in the interests of their populations.

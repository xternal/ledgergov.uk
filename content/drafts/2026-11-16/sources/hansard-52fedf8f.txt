The Minister for Creative Industries, Media and Online Safety (Ian Murray)
With permission, I will make a statement on the Microsoft 365 outage. On Thursday 12 and Friday 13 November, an outage affecting Microsoft IT systems caused impacts around the world. Users of Microsoft products were unable to send and receive emails or to use a range of other Microsoft products and services. This was a serious incident that caused disruption for a lot of people and businesses.
Microsoft initially explained that the incident was caused by an elevated load on its infrastructure and later provided further details of the three main issues that led to the outage. The company said there is no indication that the incident was caused by malicious activity. In the UK, the incident affected individuals, businesses and the public sector. There were impacts on the health sector and other public services, but key services such as 999 remained operational. There were no major critical service impacts, and public sector resilience plans were in place.
The incident could have been more serious if it had lasted longer or affected wider parts of Microsoft’s infrastructure. It reinforces the importance of promoting and improving our digital resilience—something that the Government have already taken significant steps on. There was some organisational disruption in local government, where many organisations rely on Microsoft products. Multiple local resilience forums were stood up to provide support and co-ordination among local services.
The incident also caused disruption across businesses of all sizes. Staff were unable to carry out their normal duties, and firms were unable to complete their usual transactions. Members and staff in this place were also affected. We recognise the disruption that it caused to people and organisations, and we are still working to understand the full impact.
However, critical and important services remained available and operational across the country. Accident and emergency departments remained open. Transport services were unaffected by this outage. Borders remained operational. There were no known issues with UK mobile, broadband or landline connectivity. Major telecoms providers confirmed that their services were working as normal. We currently have no evidence of wider impacts on UK data centre infrastructure or other cloud operators, and no such impacts have been reported to us by industry at this stage. There were no reported impacts on water or waste water treatment processes. Businesses, including shops and services, remained open right across the United Kingdom. By Friday afternoon, Microsoft systems were back online, and services were returning to normal.
The Government take this issue seriously. Public sector organisations and Government Departments have plans in place to mitigate potential impacts and ensure the continuation of their operations in exactly these situations. The Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport is the lead Department for national digital resilience issues. As soon as we became aware of the problem on Thursday evening, we contacted Microsoft to start discussions on the issue, to assess the impact and to request details of how the problem was to be resolved. The Minister with responsibility for cyber security, who is in the other place, spoke to the chief executive officer of Microsoft UK on Friday. My officials in DCMS led the co-ordination of the response across Departments in collaboration with the Cabinet Office. We expect Microsoft to share the lessons learned from the incident. We will be engaging further on how it will prevent such incidents from happening in the future, and plan for swifter remediation of incidents to limit service disruption.
Digital services, such as the cloud computing services that Microsoft provides, are already regulated under the UK’s Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018, which require providers to manage security and resilience risks, prevent and minimise the impact of incidents, and report significant incidents, including non-malicious system failures. Our Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill will strengthen that framework further. It strengthens and clarifies the duties applying to cloud providers, brings data centres and managed service providers into scope, and enables security and resilience requirements to be updated as risks evolve.
The UK has adopted and benefited from new technology faster than many other nations. That has powered our economy and enabled growth and innovation. However, in an environment where we are so dependent on this technology, we need to ensure that our systems are resilient and that we have plans in place for when there is disruption. That will ensure that where there are problems, we can recover faster, and that is true for both Government and industry.
In parallel, we are also working to diversify Government’s supply arrangements, including backing small and medium-sized British suppliers. By using Government’s collective buying power, we can encourage a more competitive and resilient technology market—for example, through the national cloud infrastructure programme, which will improve resilience by diversifying supply options and enabling the Government to contract for cloud services at a whole-of-system level.
The first duty of any Government is to protect their citizens. That is why we have been taking significant action to improve resilience across the nation. The Government cyber action plan, published in January, sets out how we will improve cyber-security and digital resilience across Government and the public sector. I have already mentioned the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, which is currently in this House. The Bill will boost UK cyber-defences and improve the resilience of our essential and digital services. In the recent Budget, we committed £500 million to build the secure foundations for an AI-enabled economy, including measures to strengthen the public sector technology estate.
A cross-Whitehall technology risk group for the UK Government is now established. It is actively reviewing and overseeing the response to other major strategic risks. We have recently established a chief IT operations function for the UK Government, which has been key to our work on the recent incident. Its critical role will be to systematically review post-incident reports, service outages and performance, and to work with IT operational, commercial and financial leads to develop future interventions that strengthen public sector resilience.
The future technology plan will set out operational standards and practices for firmer foundations across the UK Government, alongside accelerating an intensive baselining of the public sector technology stack, technical health and dependencies. We take these actions as any responsible Government would do in order to protect our citizens, our businesses and our economy.
This outage, like the CrowdStrike incident in 2024, shows how dependent our economy is on interconnected IT systems and why resilience planning matters. We have also seen in the US how outdated technology can have an impact on services the public relies on. My Department will now be working with partners across Government and industry to fully understand the impacts of this incident and gather any further lessons learned. We will use this work to strengthen our resilience. In that spirit, I commend this statement to the House.

Government response to the recommended bovine TB control strategy for England
Ministerial foreword
For too many farmers and their families, bovine TB has brought uncertainty, stress, and financial hardship, while affecting cattle health and farm productivity.
I have enormous admiration for the farming families who persevere despite these challenges. But no one should have to accept bovine TB as an unavoidable fact of life and livelihood. For some, a single test result can undo years, and sometimes generations, of hard work.
This has to change.
Few aspects of bovine TB control have also been more contested than the role of wildlife in disease transmission and the use of badger culling to manage disease as part of the response.
This summer marked an important turning point: for the first time in over a decade, no badger culling took place, with all licences either expired or revoked by Natural England. This government has now met its manifesto commitment to end the badger cull by the end of this Parliament.
But the end of badger culling is not the end of the bovine TB story.
Reading the co-designed strategy, I was struck by something that I believe is essential to future progress: lasting solutions are most effective when they are developed with the people most affected by them. With that in mind, I believe the publication of the recommended Bovine TB Control Strategy for England in June provides the foundation for a different future.
Since its publication, my officials have begun considering the strategy’s recommendations. This government response sets out our initial conclusions and the steps we are taking to turn those recommendations into action.
This government welcomed the strategy when it was launched in June. Today, we are now formally adopting it as the government’s long-term plan for tackling bovine TB in England.
While some recommendations will of course require further development, analysis and operational engagement before they can be fully implemented, the strategy sets a clear direction of travel.
There are no quick fixes to bovine TB.
Progress will require sustained commitment, continued partnership, and a willingness to learn and adapt as new evidence emerges, rather than waiting for perfect answers before taking action. And by adopting the co-designed strategy, we are continuing the momentum created by all those who gave their time, expertise and experience to help shape this work. We owe it to them, and to the farming families and rural communities affected by bovine TB, to now turn that collective effort into tangible and timely change.
Baroness Hayman of Ullock, Minister for Biosecurity and Trade
Introduction
In June, a major milestone was reached with the publication of the recommended co-designed Bovine TB Control Strategy for England.
This recommended strategy was developed by a Steering Group of the Bovine TB Partnership through a co-design process involving more than 100 farmers, vets, scientists, industry representatives, government officials, as well as members of the public, who all contributed their views and lived experience through working groups and public dialogue workshops.
Together, these contributions shaped a shared vision for the future of TB control in England.
Since the strategy was published, we have been considering its recommendations. Here we explain the initial conclusions this government has reached and what this means for farmers, vets and rural communities.
Looking towards bovine TB freedom: why the next decade matters
For many farming families, bovine TB is not just a disease that affects cattle. It is a constant uncertainty that shapes day to day decisions on farms as well as decisions about the future. It holds back productivity and growth and can undo years of hard work overnight.
For far too many farmers and their families, this burden has become a fact of life and daily running of their businesses.
Professor Sir Charles Godfray and his panel challenged the government to increase the pace and urgency of our efforts and made several recommendations that have been reflected in the strategy.
The strategy responds by setting out five priorities to get ahead of the disease:
cutting cattle-to-cattle transmission
making better use of TB data and evidence
preventing infection through innovation and new tools
putting farmers and vets in the driving seat locally
reducing disease risk from the wider environment
Together, these actions are designed to help England achieve Officially Bovine TB Free status by 2038.
And this goal matters.
In real world terms it would mean: reducing the uncertainty created by bovine TB, giving farm businesses the confidence to invest, innovate, grow and plan for the long term, while supporting stronger rural economies.
The prize is about so much more than disease control. It is about supporting a productive farming sector that is ready for the future.
A plan the government is committed to
Against that backdrop, the government’s position is clear: the co-designed strategy published in June, and the recommendations it contains, are the government’s plan for tackling bovine TB in England. In fulfilling the recommendations we will consider the pace and sequencing of delivery alongside future funding decisions.
The recommended strategy is the government’s strategy.
At the same time, it is right that the government continues to consider each of the recommendations carefully. As with any significant package of proposals, we must look at how each recommendation would work in practice and begin to consider any statutory duties and legal obligations that are engaged, and any wider implications as we turn them into actionable policy.
Preparing for delivery
Over the summer, we started that process of reviewing each of the strategy’s recommendations, considering how these fit together as a coherent package.
This work remains ongoing and will continue to evolve through the policy development process. It should therefore be regarded as an initial assessment rather than a final evaluation. It nevertheless provides a sufficient basis for the government to form an initial view of each recommendation, identify where progress can be made quickly, where further work is required, and the steps needed to begin turning the strategy into action.
And while some recommendations need to be completed before others can begin, some are moving forward right now.
Action already being taken
Expansion of ancillary TB testing
The Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA) has begun practical implementation work to support its development. This includes a pilot exploring the use of privately funded ancillary TB blood testing to help identify infection earlier or support risk-based decision-making, providing operational learning and evidence to inform how this approach could be delivered and rolled out effectively.
Further expansion of government-funded, mandatory interferon-gamma blood testing
Economic analysis indicates that wider use of this test alongside the primary skin test in TB breakdown herds could deliver a net benefit by finding infected cattle sooner, shortening breakdown duration and reducing the consequential costs borne by farmers. While additional testing creates upfront government and farm costs, the principal modelled benefits would accrue to farmers through fewer and shorter TB breakdowns.
Continued development of cattle vaccination and new companion ‘DIVA’ skin test to Detect Infected among Vaccinated Animals
The on-farm component of the third and final phase of the cattle vaccination field trials has completed, and work to create the required digital tools to track and trace vaccinated animals is progressing well. Following a competitive procurement exercise, the department has also awarded a contract to a diagnostics manufacturer (Thermo Fisher) to produce the reagent for the DIVA skin test to Good Manufacturing Practice standards. An application for the marketing authorisation of CattleBCG has also been submitted to the Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD). A separate application is planned to be made in 2028 to the VMD for the DIVA skin test reagent once the required dossier of supporting evidence has been compiled. We continue to engage internationally with World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and key trading partners, as well as with industry stakeholders domestically.
Expansion of badger vaccination in high-priority areas
In line with 2024 commitments, and following a competitive procurement exercise, we have awarded a contract for a new Badger Vaccination Field Force to expand the local, farming community-led approach to badger vaccination developed in East Sussex to several more areas where bovine TB incidence is high and/or increasing. This year, three new vaccination areas have been established in North Shropshire, North Worcestershire and South-East Oxfordshire, with further areas planned for 2027 and 2028. The new field force adds another targeted control measure, supporting on-farm biosecurity and our core cattle measures, while protecting badgers from infection. Further information is available on the Badger vaccination field force website.
Roll out of a national wildlife TB surveillance programme
Delivering another commitment made in 2024, later this November we will introduce a new government scheme to collect found-dead badgers for TB testing. Building on similar initiatives in Wales and Northern Ireland, the scheme will improve our understanding of the role of badgers in the epidemiology of bovine TB. It will initially focus on five counties in the High-Risk Area of England. Several surveillance projects focused on TB surveillance in wild deer are also underway, including in Exmoor, Cornwall and Oxfordshire, working with local stakeholders to collect blood samples and/or submit carcases alongside existing deer management activities.
Going further where the evidence supports it
As a co-designed strategy, we recognise that the recommendations reflect the collective judgement of those farmers, vets, scientists, industry representatives, and government officials involved in the process. Reaching that point required participants to find common ground, balance different perspectives and, at times, make compromises.
The government nevertheless believes that the process has produced a strong and credible strategy for tackling bovine TB, which is why we are adopting it as our approach. But the recommendations do not need to set the limit of our ambition.
As part of our initial considerations, we have explored whether there are opportunities to move further or faster, strengthen delivery, or unlock additional benefits. Where supported by the evidence, we intend to test these opportunities through carefully designed pilots, in keeping with the strategy’s spirit of learning by doing.
Potential alignment with EU sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) rules may also shape how certain recommendations are implemented. UK and EU bovine TB controls share common international foundations, including WOAH standards, but there are some differences between our current domestic controls and the EU framework. We are working through the implications carefully and will seek proportionate, practical approaches that support effective disease control while avoiding unnecessary impacts wherever possible.
Exploring the role of compensation
One area we believe warrants immediate further consideration is compensation for the compulsory slaughter of cattle that test positive for bovine TB.
There are no easy answers.
Affected farmers already face significant financial and emotional impacts beyond those covered by compensation. These can include lost income, movement restrictions, additional operating costs, and disruption to breeding and trading. At the same time, compensation represents a significant cost to taxpayers, typically amounting to between £25 million and £30 million net each year.
We owe it to farmers and taxpayers alike to consider whether current arrangements remain appropriate, affordable and sustainable.
Defra-commissioned research, which is expected to be published shortly, found that simply reducing compensation rates alone is unlikely to deliver lasting changes in disease-risk management. Farmers’ decisions are also shaped by whether they believe measures will work, their ability to put them into practice, their experience of disease and their trust in the system.
The research suggests there may be greater potential in linking compensation more clearly to proportionate, evidence-based expectations around biosecurity, safer herd management and risk-based cattle trading. Any changes would need to be fair, practical and recognise the different circumstances and risks faced by farms.
The government would therefore welcome the co-design Steering Group using its first delivery plan, due to be published in late spring 2027, to set out how work on longer-term financial sustainability, including how compensation could better support shared responsibility and effective disease-risk reduction while maintaining confidence in the system, should be taken forward.
Transforming bovine TB governance
Among the strategy’s recommendations, one stands out as particularly significant: the creation of a new industry-government Strategy Board.
If the last few years have taught us anything, it is that progress is fastest when those responsible for delivering change work together towards the same goal. The co-design process that shaped this strategy proved exactly that. Farmers, vets, scientists, industry representatives, and government came together, often bringing different perspectives, but united by a shared determination to tackle bovine TB. Their discussions were supported by insights from public and stakeholder dialogue workshops.
In doing so, the co-design process showed that a different way of working with farmers and scientists is possible. They showed that collaboration can replace division, that shared ownership can drive better decisions, and that partnership can create the conditions to accelerate progress. That is why the government will take forward the recommendation to establish a new industry-government board, replacing the existing Bovine TB Partnership for England.
Its purpose is simple: to embody the principles of “partnership” even more.
The new board will not replace the role of ministers or government, which will continue to hold statutory responsibility for bovine TB control, including decisions on public spending, legislation and other matters reserved to ministers. But at the same time, the board can fundamentally change how the control of bovine TB is governed in England.
Accountability will be at the heart of the board’s role. It will set the delivery priorities, oversee progress, advise on how to allocate funding and resources, and help partners resolve challenges so that priority actions stay on track.
To make delivery and accountability clear, the board will publish rolling three to five-year delivery plans and regular progress reports. These documents will hold the board and delivery partners to account for the progress being made and set out the challenges that remain and their plan for resolving them. It will be supported by a programme-wide monitoring, evaluation and learning approach, which we are establishing and intend to publish shortly, with clear outcome measures and regular reporting. This lays the foundations for understanding what is working, for whom and in which circumstances, and for using evidence to inform delivery, improvement and value for money.
Establishing the new board
Over the coming months, we will put the new governance arrangements in place, launching a public appointments process for board members later this year. This will establish the board in 2027, at the earliest opportunity, bringing together a mix of organisational representatives and individuals appointed for their expertise and experience.
While building the new board is important, we know the challenge of bovine TB will not pause for our governance, and neither will delivery of the strategy.
That is why the government has asked the existing co-design Steering Group to develop the first delivery plan, to be published in late spring 2027. This will allow early priorities to be identified and agreed before the new board is established, helping maintain momentum by reviewing and approving that plan as one of its first actions.
We will set out the new board’s terms of reference and provide further updates as recruitment progresses. In the meantime, if you are interested in serving on the board and believe you can make a meaningful contribution to that ambition, we encourage you to register your interest by emailing: btbengage@defra.gov.uk. Doing so will allow us to keep you informed as recruitment progresses.
Empowering local communities
Another important theme running through the strategy is the need to put farmers and their vets more firmly in the driving seat. After all, they are the people who know their farms and their herds best.
While bovine TB is a national challenge, local farmers, vets and their communities are often best placed to shape effective solutions. The strategy calls for a different balance: a strong national framework, combined with greater scope for local leadership, innovation and collective action.
Our role is not to dictate every response from the centre. Instead, it is to create the right conditions to enable locally led action to flourish and contribute to wider national goals. We see the TB Hub as central to that ambition. Over time, we want it to become more than a source of information, to become a focal point for coordinating local engagement, helping to connect farmers, vets and local groups, share practical experience, and ensure that lessons learned in one area can benefit others.
Building this local leadership model will take time. One of the first tasks for the new board will be to develop and publish a national framework to guide and support local action. But that should not mean waiting to get started.
Across the country, there are already examples of farmers, vets and local communities coming together to tackle bovine TB. Where those groups exist, we want to support them. Where there is interest in creating new ones, we want to encourage and enable that as well. We recognise the growing interest in local action and will provide further information on how groups can get involved, access support, and connect with emerging local and national arrangements.
The ambition is simple: to connect local action with national priorities, creating a clear and uninterrupted line of sight from farm to forum, from those on the ground to those setting priorities nationally.
Conclusion
There are no quick or simple solutions to the challenges posed by bovine TB. As Professor Sir Charles Godfray and his panel observed in the opening of their 2025 evidence review, “we would be offering false hope if we pretended this will be other than a protracted campaign”.
There will inevitably be challenges along the way, and issues that a new Strategy Board will need to work through together. So, while there is more to be done, the strategy published in June 2026, and adopted by the government through this response, provides a clear long-term plan for tackling bovine TB in England.
There are now little more than 11 years left until 2038. There is no time to stand still. This government response is not the end of the process, but the beginning of the next phase: delivery. It marks the start of what we intend to now be a regular drumbeat of pace, progress and accountability. Recruitment to the new board will begin shortly, followed by its establishment at the earliest opportunity in 2027, and publication of its first delivery plan soon thereafter.
And by continuing to work together with farmers, vets, industry and rural communities, we can build on the progress already made and move closer to our shared ambition of a future free from bovine TB.

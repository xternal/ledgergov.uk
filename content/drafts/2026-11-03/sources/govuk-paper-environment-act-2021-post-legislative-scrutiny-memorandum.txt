Environment Act 2021: Post Legislative Scrutiny Memorandum
Executive summary
This memorandum provides a detailed post-legislative scrutiny of the Environment Act 2021 (the Act), focusing on its implementation, achievements, and challenges. It primarily covers England, with references to Wales, Northern Ireland, and Scotland where applicable.
Post-legislative scrutiny of the Act finds it has largely established the legal and institutional framework needed to deliver stronger environmental governance. Most core provisions have commenced with key statutory mechanisms in place. The Act has also enabled important reforms across governance, waste, air quality, water, nature and biodiversity. In several areas, implementation is still in progress, and the evidence base is not yet mature enough to determine whether the intended environmental outcomes are being achieved.
The review found good progress has been made in establishing the Act’s governance mechanisms in line with the objective to create a comprehensive legal framework to strengthen environmental oversight and provide clarity and certainty. The statutory cycle of Environmental Improvement Plans, targets, monitoring and annual reporting are all operational, and the Office for Environmental Protection has been established as an independent scrutiny body in England and Northern Ireland. The Environmental Principles Policy Statement has also introduced a cross-government framework for considering environmental principles in policy development. These reforms have strengthened transparency and accountability. However, data lags and uneven evidence across the environment remain constraints on assessing real-world environmental improvement.
The Act has also provided the legislative basis for significant policy reforms. In relation to resources and waste, its objective was to support the transition to a more circular model. The review found that the Act has enabled extended producer responsibility for packaging, deposit return scheme legislation and future digital waste tracking. However, many of these reforms are newly operational or not fully commenced, limiting the extent to which their effectiveness can be currently assessed.
The Act has strengthened the local air quality management framework and smoke control enforcement, improving accountability and collaboration to meet the Act’s objectives on public health and accountability. It has also improved transparency on storm overflows and placed drainage and wastewater management planning on a statutory footing to help secure long-term resilient water services. However, many of the intended environmental benefits across the air and water sectors will only become apparent over longer timescales as investment programmes, infrastructure improvements and regulatory interventions take effect.
Overall progress on the Act’s objectives of creating the change required to reverse long-term decline in nature and biodiversity is positive. Strengthened biodiversity duties, Local Nature Recovery Strategies, conservation covenants and related measures have created new tools to support nature recovery and embed environmental considerations in planning and decision making. Some delivery challenges remain, particularly around capacity, publication timelines, monitoring and the time needed for ecological outcomes to become measurable.
Overall, this review finds that the Act has been effective in creating the framework and powers intended by Parliament, and in many areas those powers are being used constructively. It has improved scrutiny, enabled major reforms, and established clearer mechanisms for planning, monitoring and reporting. The principal limitations are in the pace of implementation, the maturity of delivery systems and the delay before environmental outcomes can be evidenced. Future scrutiny could focus on the delivery of long-term policy changes through Environmental Improvement Plans, which is a key part of the governance framework and covers policies from across the broad policy areas covered by the Act.
1. Introduction
This memorandum has been prepared by the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) for submission to the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee. It is published in accordance with the process for post-legislative scrutiny set out in the Guide to making legislation document.
Environmental protection is a devolved matter, subject to a small number of reserved areas. Most of the provisions in the Act are applicable only to England. However, several provisions also extend to and apply in Wales with a significant number of provisions having Great Britain, UK or England, Wales and Northern Ireland extent. Where provisions apply to Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland, the devolved governments were consulted. Preliminary assessments on relevant provisions were provided by the Scottish and Northern Ireland governments, and the Welsh government will provide any necessary assessment to the Senedd.
The purpose of post-legislative scrutiny is to systematically identify problems with the implementation of Acts. It allows lessons to be learned and disseminated to benefit other legislation. Significant achievements can be identified and highlighted.
Annual progress reports (APR) for the Environmental Improvement Plan (EIP) set out environmental progress since the Act received Royal Assent. These provide more information on the impact of implementation of the Act. The most recent Annual progress report was published in July 2026.
Structure of the report
Given the breadth and complexity of the Act, this memorandum is structured around the principal parts of the Act, with each part broken down into the relevant chapters, provisions and policy themes. This approach reflects the Act’s legislative framework and enables related provisions to be considered together. For each section, the memorandum summarises the objectives of the relevant provisions, their implementation and commencement, any legal or review activity, and a preliminary assessment of how they have operated in practice since Royal Assent.
1.1. Origins of the Act
Environmental governance (Part 1)
In January 2018, the government published the 25 Year Environment Plan (25YEP). The Draft Environment (Principles and Governance) Bill 2018 was published for parliamentary pre-legislative scrutiny on 19 December 2018. This fulfilled the requirement to publish a draft Bill under section 16 of the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018.
Pre-legislative scrutiny reports by the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Select Committee and the Environmental Audit Committee in the previous parliament were published on 30 April 2019 and 24 April 2019 respectively. The government of the day responded to these reports when it introduced its Environment Bill in October 2019, which Part 1 of the Act largely took forward.
Environmental governance: Northern Ireland (Part 2)
Under the Northern Ireland Act 1998, the Northern Ireland Assembly had legislative competence for several areas of law. Part 2 of the Act allowed the Office for Environmental Protection (OEP) to exercise its functions in Northern Ireland, subject to the approval of the Northern Ireland Assembly.
Waste and resource efficiency (Part 3)
In December 2018, the government published Our waste, our resources: a strategy for England. Part 3 of the Act provided the legislative framework needed to deliver on many of the commitments in those strategies. It introduced new powers and amended existing legislation such as the Environment Act 1995 and Environmental Protection Act 1990.
Air Quality and environmental recall (Part 4)
The previous government published the Clean Air Strategy (CAS) in January 2019. The Act implemented key proposals outlined in the CAS and amended the Environment Act 1995, the Clean Air Act 1993 and the Environmental Protection Act 1990.
Water (Part 5)
Part 5 of the Act set out measures to achieve the water related objectives set out in the 25YEP. It amended the Water Industry Act 1991, Water Resources Act 1991 and the Land Drainage Act 1991 to do so.
Nature and biodiversity (Part 6)
Part 6 of the Act put in place specific nature and biodiversity legislative requirements set out in the 25YEP. It amended the Natural Environment and Rural Communities (NERC) Act 2006, Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017 (S.I. 2017/1012), and The Forestry Act 1967.
Conservation covenants (Part 7)
The Law Commission examined the case for conservation covenants and concluded that legislation should be introduced, preparing a draft Bill in 2014. In 2019, the government consulted on whether to introduce legislation for conservation covenants in England, proposing the use of the Law Commission’s draft Bill as the basis for legislating. This became part 7 of the Act.
Miscellaneous and general provisions (Part 8)
The use of chemicals in the EU is regulated by Regulation (EC) No.1907/2006 concerning the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH), establishing a European Chemicals Agency (the “REACH Regulation”). The REACH Regulation forms part of retained EU law by virtue of the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 (“the Withdrawal Act”). The REACH Enforcement Regulations 2008 (S.I. 2008/2852) provide for the contravention of various provisions of the REACH Regulation to be a criminal offence and set out which domestic bodies enforced those offences. Part 8 and Schedule 21 gave the Secretary of State the power to amend the REACH Regulation and the REACH Enforcement Regulations 2008 (S.I. 2008/2852) as they applied in the UK at the end of the transition period.
1.2. Objectives of the Act
The Act was introduced to Parliament by the Conservative government between 2019 and 2024. It was intended to provide a clearer and more robust framework for environmental governance on leaving the European Union. It aimed to manage the impact of human activity on the environment, create a more sustainable and resilient economy, and enhance well-being and quality of life. It sought to deliver outcomes by engaging and empowering citizens, local government and business.
Environmental governance
Part 1 of the Act was intended to provide a comprehensive legal framework for environmental governance tailored specifically to a UK context. The new system was designed to strengthen environmental oversight and be clear and accessible, providing certainty to businesses and citizens.
By placing a statutory requirement on government to publish and maintain an EIP, it created a statutory cycle of monitoring, planning and reporting which was intended to ensure continuous improvement to the environment. The government established a new framework for setting environmental targets including targets on PM2.5 and species abundance.
The requirement for an Environmental Principles Policy Statement (EPPS) within the Act was meant to protect the environment from damage by making environmental considerations central to the policy development process across government.
The Act created the OEP, a domestic independent body that was designed to contribute to environmental protection and the improvement of the natural environment. The OEP was created to monitor progress in improving the natural environment in accordance with the EIP and targets. It included powers to act on serious breaches of environmental law.
Provision was made for it to provide advice on proposed changes to environmental law and manage compliance with environmental law in a proportionate way.
The Act sought to increase transparency, strengthen scrutiny for Parliament of future environmental legislation and inform environmental domestic work through statements to Parliament and reviewing other countries’ environmental legislation.
Part 2 of the Act enabled the OEP to exercise its functions in Northern Ireland and have appropriate Northern Ireland representation on the OEP board. It also made provision for a separate EIP and environmental principles in Northern Ireland.
Implementation of the environmental governance provisions is considered in sections 2.1 to 3.1 of this report.
A new direction for resources and waste management
Part 3 of the Act was intended to move the economy to a more circular model, keeping resources in use for longer and maximising their value.
The provisions helped consumers make purchasing decisions that support the market for more sustainable products, such as powers to introduce clearer labelling and setting minimum eco-design requirements for products. The government wanted a shift towards durable, repairable and recyclable products.
Extended producer responsibility was intended to make producers responsible for the full net cost of managing their products at end of life, helping to prevent waste.
The Act introduced powers for modulated fees to incentivise producers to design their products building in re-use and recycling. It created charges for single-use items and established deposit return schemes.
Waste tracking measures were intended to encourage businesses to adopt legitimate waste management practices. Regulators and local authorities could then effectively combat waste crime with better access to evidence, improved powers of entry and the ability to keep fixed penalty notices at appropriate levels.
It also included powers to allow government to stop the export of waste, including plastic, to developing countries.
Through provisions in Part 3, the government sought to support recycling by making collection services from households and businesses more consistent across the country. Other provisions were intended to promote proportionate and effective enforcement against littering.
Implementation of the resources and waste management provisions is considered in sections 4.1 to 4.4 of this report.
Improving the air we breathe
Part 4 of the Act intended to deliver public health benefits by addressing air pollution. Through reductions in fine particulate matter, it aimed to deliver benefits to public health as well as wider benefits, resulting in benefits for the economy.
Provisions within Part 4 were intended to strengthen the ability for local authorities to address air quality issues through updating, simplifying and strengthening the local air quality management framework and sharing responsibility across relevant bodies.
New measures aimed to reduce emissions from domestic solid fuel burning and provide the power to compel recall of vehicles of environmental non-conformity.
Implementation of the air quality and environmental recall provisions is considered in sections 5.1 to 5.2 of this report.
Delivering sustainable water resources
The Act intended to help secure long-term resilient water and wastewater services, ensuring a cleaner, greener and more resilient country for the next generation.
Part 5 of the Act reformed elements of abstraction licensing to link them more tightly to the goal of restoring water bodies to as close to their natural state as possible. This included enabling variation or revocation of abstraction licences without the regulator being liable to pay compensation in certain conditions.
The Act introduced additional requirements on water companies planning for future water supply, wastewater and drainage networks. These measures were intended to enable more resilient solutions to drought and flooding.
It improved effective oversight by ensuring the regulator could obtain relevant information through strengthened enforceable information-gathering powers in the Water Industry Act, 1991. It amended the process for modifying the condition of companies’ licences, creating a more transparent and flexible process that gave Ofwat the powers it needed to respond to changing priorities.
Provisions in Part 5 provided powers to update the list of priority substances and their respective standards which are potentially harmful to surface water and groundwater. This was to keep pace with scientific and technical knowledge after EU exit.
It aimed to enhance flood and coastal erosion risk management by addressing a current barrier to the expansion of existing, or creation of new, internal drainage boards (IDBs). This included allowing updates to the valuation IDBs use to charge for their work, through secondary legislation.
Implementation of the water provisions is considered in sections 6.1 to 6.6 of this report.
Restoring and enhancing nature and green spaces and delivering environmental ambition at the local level
The Act was intended to create the change required to reverse the long-term decline in nature by supplementing existing legislation and policy on protected sites and species and introducing new incentives, actions and planning tools to drive further improvements for nature.
Provisions in Part 6 established spatial mapping and planning tools to inform nature recovery. These Local Nature Recovery Strategies (LNRS) were intended to support better spatial planning for nature by setting out priorities and opportunities for protecting and investing in nature within a local area. This included a map of existing nature assets and identifying key opportunities for enhancement.
Introduction of strategies for protected sites and species was intended to tackle on and offsite pressures, restore protected sites, to help the government meet its environmental targets and create environmental headroom for growth.
Mandating biodiversity net gain in the planning system was intended to ensure development enhanced biodiversity, created new green spaces for local communities and stimulated nature markets. This was expected to result in the creation and avoidance of loss of several thousand hectares of habitat for wildlife each year and to increase the public benefits of ecosystems.
Strengthening the Natural Environment and Rural Communities (NERC) duty was intended to ensure public authorities played their part in enacting meaningful environmental change. This required public authorities to carry out strategic assessment of how they can improve biodiversity.
The duty to consult on street tree felling aimed to ensure local people could express their views over the proposed management of street trees in their locality. Other provisions strengthened the enforcement powers of the Forestry Commission and the courts to reduce illegal tree felling.
Implementation of environmental governance is considered in sections 7.1 to 7.7 of this report.
Conservation covenants
Part 7 of the Act was intended to establish a legal tool that landowners can use to secure conservation benefits when land is sold or passed on, as set out in the Conservation Covenants Law Commission project. The objective was to provide for the conservation of the natural environment and heritage assets for public good.
Implementation of the conservation covenant provisions is considered in section 8.1 of this report.
Part 8 Chemical regulation (REACH)
Part 8 of the Act amended retained EU law on chemicals aiming to ensure a smooth transition to a UK chemicals regime following the UK’s exit from the EU. This was meant to keep chemicals legislation up to date and respond to emerging needs or ambitions for the effective management of chemicals. Devolved governments were given the equivalent power to amend the REACH Enforcement Regulations 2008.
Implementation of the chemical regulation provisions are considered in sections 9.1 to 9.2 of this report.
1.3. Territorial extent
Most of the Act formed part of the law of England and Wales and applied to England. Around half of the Act’s provisions extended to and applied in Wales, with a significant number of provisions having Great Britain, UK or England, Wales and Northern Ireland extent. Sections 48, 59, 61, 65, 67, 71, 91 and Schedule 2 formed part of the law of Northern Ireland and applied to Northern Ireland only. Sections 90 and 95 applied to Wales only.
Full details of the extent and application of different provisions were provided in the Environment Act 2021 explanatory notes.

2. Environmental governance (Part 1)
2.1. Improving the natural environment (Chapter 1)
Environmental targets (sections 1 to 7)
Sections 1, 2 and 3 required the government to set long-term, legally binding environmental targets. Targets were required in at least 4 priority areas:
air quality
biodiversity
water
resource efficiency
This included specific targets on PM2.5 and species abundance.
Sections 4, 5 and 6 set out the Secretary of State’s duties in relation to the target-setting process, ensuring the targets were met and reporting on this to Parliament. It placed a statutory duty on government to monitor progress and to maintain a cycle of planning, monitoring and reporting.
Section 7 required the Secretary of State to review all relevant environmental targets to consider whether they would significantly improve the natural environment in England.
Environmental improvement plans (EIP) (sections 8 to 15)
Sections 8 to 15 required the government to have and maintain an EIP to significantly improve the natural environment. They created a statutory duty to set interim targets to keep government on track for statutory targets, as well as setting out the statutory cycle of monitoring, planning, and reporting. This included reviewing or renewing the EIP based on implementation progress and changes in the natural environment.
Environmental monitoring (section 16)
Section 16 established a duty on the Secretary of State to obtain and publish data to monitor environmental improvement in England. This required the collection of information needed to:
assess outcomes delivered through the EIP through the Environmental Indicator Framework
measure progress towards long-term and interim statutory targets
inform updates to the EIP
The section required the Secretary of State to publish a statement specifying the types of data to be collected and to lay it before Parliament.
Implementation
Sections 1 to 16 of the Act commenced on 24 January 2022.
Target legislation was due to be laid before Parliament by 31 October 2022. Laying of the Statutory Instruments (SIs) was delayed due to the volume of consultation responses received, as explained in the Environmental targets consultation summary of responses and government response.
Environmental target SIs were introduced and debated through an affirmative procedure in Parliament in December 2022 (House of Lords 16 December, and House of Commons 19 December). The SIs came into force on 30 and 31 January 2023.
Other reviews
The OEP provides an assessment of the government’s progress towards improving the environment in accordance with Environment Act targets and interim targets through their APRs:
in its 2023 report, Progress in improving the natural environment in England, 2021 to 2022
in its 2024 report, Progress in improving the natural environment in England 2022 to 2023
in its 2025 report, Progress in improving the natural environment in England 2023 to 2024
in its 2026 report, Progress in improving the natural environment in England 2024 to 2025. The 2026 report covered 2024 to 2025, but followed the publication of EIP25. The OEP published a briefing note alongside the report to share an initial assessment of the EIP25
During the second revision of the EIP, the Defra Secretary of State requested and received OEP advice on the Government’s review of its Environmental Improvement Plan outside of this cycle under section 30(1)(b) of the Act.
Legal issues
There have been 2 pre-action protocol letters on the governance section of the Act, which were subsequently withdrawn.
In 2024, there was a challenge on the Secretary of State’s decision not to review the 2023 EIP, which was withdrawn when the government announced the review of the EIP.
In 2026, there was a challenge on the decision not to include a commitment on Forest Risk Commodities in EIP25, which was withdrawn following further engagement on the government’s plans for the policy area.
Preliminary assessment: Environmental targets
The government successfully set statutory Environment Act targets under the provisions of the Act. An environmental targets policy paper published in August 2020 set out the approach to setting targets under the Act. The process required:
Setting the scope.
Developing proposals using a systems approach and robust evidence gathered through expert groups, meeting the objective for independent advice to be sought.
Public consultation on proposals, factoring in responses to inform targets.
Drafting target legislation and laying before Parliament.
A public consultation on setting targets under the Act was conducted from March to June 2022, with the Environmental targets consultation: summary of responses and government response published in December 2022. Whilst responses to the consultation generally called for more stretching targets, the then government’s response was that the targets need to be ambitious but be based on evidence that they are achievable as per requirements of the Act.
Six Environment Act SIs covering air quality, water, waste, biodiversity, tree canopy and woodland cover and Marine Protected Areas targets, came into force in January 2023. Full details are provided at Annex C: Secondary Legislation.
The first review of environmental targets (the Significant Improvement Test) concluded that meeting the Environment Act statutory targets, alongside the broader suite of statutory targets, would lead to significant improvement in all 6 components of England’s natural environment assessed in the review.
Preliminary assessment: EIPs
The EIP is achieving its objectives to set out the government’s vision for the natural environment and its plan to improve it.
To date, the government has produced 3 EIPs for England:
the 25 Year Environment Plan, created prior to the Act and retrospectively designated the first EIP in the Act
the first revision, EIP23 Environmental Improvement Plan 2023, laid in January 2023, setting the first interim targets for the Act targets
the second revision, EIP25 Environmental Improvement Plan 2025, laid in December 2025, setting the second set of interim targets. Delivery plans for each interim target were first published alongside EIP25
There have been 2 section 16 statements laid in Parliament and published setting out the types of data used for monitoring the condition of the natural environment.
The most recent iteration of the plan, EIP25, was the second revision of the first EIP, the 25 Year Environment Plan. EIP25 sets out measurable commitments across every priority – from cleaner air and water to restoring nature and resilient landscapes. The EIP sets out how the government will work alongside individuals, communities and businesses to achieve its nature ambitions.
It was developed in response to feedback, including from the OEP, to provide a more robust and coherent delivery plan for the interim targets and EIP commitments. The latest EIP was largely well received, including by the OEP, which welcomed the way government had considered its advice during its development. Feedback from Natural England indicated that the EIP helps it align delivery of its work with government priorities and environmental targets.
As the EIP is a statutory plan it cannot be amended without revising or renewing the plan. The EIP cycle has benefits for long-term planning and prioritisation, however it presents challenges for adjusting the plan to urgent pressures or new policy developments because it presents a snapshot of delivery plans approximately every 5 years.
To balance this, the government published delivery plans for the Environment Act statutory targets alongside the EIP, these are live plans that can be amended between EIP reviews and revisions based on the latest evidence and delivery information.
The government also intends to use the EIP APRs to update the EIP as new policies or programmes become important for the delivery of the goals. This will improve transparency and make the plan more reactive to change. The first APR published since the publication of EIP25 included a new commitment following the publication of the Land Use Framework.
If government chooses to create other cross-cutting policy delivery plans, like the EIP, for other policy areas, Defra recommends considering how plans can remain live to emerging issues in a proportionate way.
Preliminary assessment: EIP Annual progress reports
There have been 3 APRs since the implementation of the Act. APRs effectively set out implementation progress of the EIP and whether the natural environment has improved over the preceding years. The assessment of progress is based on the Environmental Indicator Framework and other key data trends. Progress towards statutory targets is also considered. The OEP uses the APR to inform its own annual reports, to which the government then responds.
The OEP views the annual cycle of government and OEP reporting as the key mechanism for transparency and accountability on target delivery. However, in its view, there is insufficient data in some areas and a risk that some targets will not be met without a significant increase in the pace of cohesive actions taken to achieve them.
Responding to OEP feedback, the government changed its approach to APRs based on the more coherent outcomes-based framework in the latest revision of the EIP. The OEP welcomed the change to APRs and the inclusion of the government’s response to the
OEP’s recommendations to help shorten the reporting cycle.
Similar provisions in future legislation should consider what is the most effective frequency for APRs. Understanding changes in the natural environment depends on trends across years. Five years’ worth of data is required for short term trend assessment, and key data is published 2 to 3 years after it is collected. Annual reports do not allow time for any changes to be reflected in data or trends. A progress report on a 2 to 3 year basis rather than annually could allow more focus on programme delivery and see more change in the data between reporting periods.
Preliminary assessment: Reviewing and revising EIPs
Both reviews of the EIP clearly set out how statutory requirements were met, including consideration of:
what had been done to implement the plan
whether the natural environment had changed
whether the government should take further or different steps to improve the natural environment
For EIP23, the government held a one-stage EIP review process. For EIP25, the government held a 2-stage EIP review process. An initial rapid review was held to take stock of progress and inform the development of the revision. The rapid review was then built on over the full revision process culminating in a report covering the full review period. Taking a phased approach front-loaded analysis to better inform the revision. The department recommends this approach for future reviews for the revision or replacement of the EIP.
Preliminary assessment: Environmental monitoring
EIPs, EIP reviews and Environment Act targets are developed using varied data, including the Environmental Indicator Framework. The indicators relate to the 10 goals of the EIP but are not themselves targets. They are a way to objectively monitor the high-level changes happening in the natural environment. The indicators support the statutory cycle of monitoring, planning and reporting.
To date, 2 dedicated statements on Monitoring the condition of the Natural Environment (section 16) on the types of data used have been tabled and published.
The latest EIP also has a dedicated in-depth Environmental Improvement Plan monitoring plan which sets out how evidence is effectively used to monitor the EIP and targets, including theories of change, key metrics for all commitments and an assessment of the contribution commitments make to deliver the outcomes.
Defra is undertaking an evaluation of the latest EIP. The evaluation uses research and analysis to understand the EIP’s impact and processes and will identify lessons for improving the design and delivery of future EIPs.
2.2. Policy statement on environmental principles (sections 17 to 19)
Sections 17 to 19 require the Secretary of State to prepare a statutory policy statement on the environmental principles, explaining how the 5 principles must be interpreted and applied proportionately when making policy.
Implementation
A consultation on the Environmental principles draft policy statement was held between 10 March 2021 and 2 June 2021. The interim OEP provided advice at Defra’s request, published on 16 August 2021. The Environmental Audit Committee provided recommendations on the government’s draft EPPS in a report published on 28 July 2022. The Environmental Principles Policy Statement (EPPS) was published on 31 January 2023 and the duty came into force on 1 November 2023. Policies made after this date are in scope of the EPPS duty.
Legal issues
The EPPS sets out how ministers should interpret and proportionately apply the 5 environmental principles, so that they are used effectively to shape policy to protect the environment.
Proceedings were brought by Rights: Community: Action Ltd against the Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government. The issue concerned the duty under section 19(1) of the Act to have ‘due regard’ to the EPPS when making policy. As “making policy” includes developing, adopting or revising policy, the duty applies throughout the policy development process rather than only at the point of adoption.
This question was considered in litigation that progressed from the Rights Community Action Ltd versus Secretary of State for Levelling Up to the Court of Appeal, before the Supreme Court refused the claimant permission to appeal. The Court of Appeal held that a later, section 19-compliant EPPS assessment cannot remedy a breach that occurred when the policy was adopted, nor can it retrospectively validate the original decision. However, Rights Community Action Limited versus Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government (Court of Appeal) confirmed that the subsequent consideration itself was lawful and proportionate.
Other reviews
EPPS implementation has been examined through 2 formal reviews: the OEP review of implementation of the duty to have due regard to the Environmental Principles Policy Statement, published in February 2025, and Defra’s internal review completed in November 2025 (Environmental Principles Policy Statement Implementation - Early-Stage Review - ES0146). The government published its response to the OEP review of implementation of the duty to have due regard to the Environmental Principles Policy Statement on 19 May 2025.
Both reviews found that the EPPS framework is functioning broadly as intended. The EPPS duty is well implemented and used constructively across government, the support provided by Defra has been well received. Neither review suggested that Defra should direct or oversee departmental policy development. Rather, individual departments remain responsible for their own implementation and monitoring, with Defra’s role focused on enabling, facilitating, and maintaining a coherent cross government approach. They also identified opportunities to strengthen support for policymakers, which Defra continues to take forward through proportionate, targeted provisions. These include opportunities to update EPPS to drive consideration of how policies can contribute to government’s environmental targets, greater clarity in departmental guidance, and more effective cross-government guidance covering EPPS (such as the Green Book).
Recommendations were also made for greater transparency in application of the EPPS, and ongoing support for embedding of the duty in departmental cultures, including stronger leadership across government.
Preliminary assessment
This section includes an update on further progress or developments since the publication of the 2 reviews.
The EPPS duty has ensured that ministers are provided with information on the potential environmental effects of policy proposals and how the principles may be considered. The duty does not prescribe policy outcomes or determine the balance of considerations in policy decision making. The policy statement has allowed Ministers to continue to weigh EPPS considerations alongside other factors. This assessment describes the role of the duty in informing ministerial policy decision making, but it is not a requirement of EPPS to assess the direct causal impact of those government decisions on environmental outcomes pre- or post-implementation of the individual policy.
The policy statement requires policymakers to identify the potential environmental effects of their policy and use the principles to inform and influence the design of the policy. The Environmental Principles Toolkit is a comprehensive, practical internal support package to help government policymakers, analysts and lawyers understand and apply the EPPS when making policies. It provides clear, accessible guidance on how Ministers should have due regard to the EPPS when developing policy. It includes internal templates, examples, training materials and explanatory notes that support the integration of the 5 principles in policymaking.
Addressing OEP Recommendations 1, 2 and 3, new toolkit material is developed and undergoes user testing, where a need is identified. Examples include improved accessibility, and guidance on applying the principles across the 4 UK nations.
Regular onboarding sessions support new colleagues and ensure consistent understanding of the duty across government. Cross-government forums are used to build wider awareness and capability, including an EPPS specific all-government working group, with governance arrangements providing a route of escalation to senior leaders. Work is progressing to enhance senior leaders’ role in promoting the EPPS, in line with OEP Recommendations 2 and 7. This will help ensure that the duty continues to be embedded at the right strategic level. Defra is developing a horizon scanning approach across government to identify policies that have the greatest environmental implications (positive or negative) to facilitate early engagement and support.
In response to OEP recommendations 3, 4 and 5, we have refreshed the EPPS communications strategy with 3 complementary strands: to engage, enable and educate. The engage strand involves supporting individual departments on high impact policies; early intervention aims to improve EPPS application, outcomes and impact. The second strand, targeted raising awareness, is designed to enable those who most need to know about EPPS to understand it through targeted engagement. The final strand is to provide comprehensive, accessible guidance and training for all officials.
Enhanced visibility across government has been achieved through referencing EPPS in major cross-government guidance, including the Green Book, the Guide to Making Legislation, the Better Regulation templates, and the Business Case guidance. This aims to normalise consideration of the principles across policy development.
There have been 4 Defra-led implementation exercises since the duty came into force. The latest exercise (March to April 2026) gathered information on the extent of guidance and training uptake (amongst other activities) that departments are undertaking. It showed that most respondents feel confident with EPPS and regularly draw on (or adapt) Defra’s central guidance. Departments have their own governance, communication, and awareness raising in place. Legal support was noted as being available but sometimes inconsistent, and this is an area that Defra intends to explore further.
In response to OEP recommendation 8, and additional to our implementation exercises the EPPS policy team developed and tested a light touch submission sampling tool, now released across government to help departments to understand how ministers are being enabled to have due regard to the EPPS.
The EPPS duty does not require departments to publish information on how the duty was considered. Departments may choose to make such information available where appropriate, but this remains a matter for them and does not establish precedent.
Transparency is supported through existing processes and statutory frameworks, including the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and Environmental Information Regulations 2004 (EIR), alongside public authorities’ duty to cooperate with the OEP under section 27 of the Act. These processes are managed by departments in line with their established procedures and governance arrangements.
2.3. Environmental protection: statements and reports (sections 20 to 21)
Section 20 required Ministers to provide a formal written statement to Parliament before second reading of a Bill should it contain provisions that would constitute environmental law, confirming either that the Bill would not reduce existing levels of environmental protection or, if the Minister cannot give that assurance, that they nevertheless wish to proceed. The duty applies only to legislation directly related to environmental law, excluding broader planning regimes except where they involve explicit environmental processes such as Environmental Impact Assessment or Strategic Environmental Assessment.
Section 21 requires the Defra Secretary of State to lay in the UK Parliament and publish every 2 years a report on significant developments in environmental protection legislation introduced by other countries, territories, or international organisations. There is no statutory deadline for the publication of the International Environmental Protection Report which is due ‘as soon as reasonably practicable’ after each 2-year reporting period.
Implementation

Sections 22 to 27 provided the framework for the creation of a new independent OEP. monitor the implementation of environmental law - this allows them to prepare reports on this, to which Secretary of State must respond
give advice on changes to environmental law and other matters related to the natural environment when government requires it to. The OEP also has a power to provide advice about proposed changes to environmental law without being required by government
Sections 31 to 41 established the OEP’s enforcement functions, setting out powers relating to complaints, investigations, Environmental Review and Judicial Review.

Preliminary assessment
The OEP published its Strategy and Enforcement Policy in June 2022 and reviewed this in November 2024. The enforcement policy has regard to the importance of prioritising cases that it considers have or may have national implications. It sets out a staged approach, addressing the requirement for the OEP to have regard to the need to act proportionately.
The OEP and CCC have prepared a memorandum of understanding, most recently in 2025.
Defra and other public authorities have co-operated with the OEP, giving it such reasonable assistance as it requests including the provision of information. Information provision is subject to the duties on confidentiality and for the assistance given to be reasonable.
The OEP has published annual reports relating to the EIP in England, to which the Secretary of State has responded. The reporting timetable is problematic and considered confusing, as the Act sets out that Defra may publish its response to the OEP’s progress report just before the next OEP progress report is published and almost 2 years after the period it is reporting on. To improve this, this year the government committed to publishing a response in the APR, within 6 months of the report it is responding to, as it did in 2026. The OEP has welcomed this.
The OEP has published 9 monitoring reports relating to the implementation of environmental law in England and the Secretary of State has responded to 8 out of 9, with the ninth expected shortly, at the time of writing. The requirement to publish a monitoring report response within 3 months does not consider the need to lay when Parliament is sitting. That can lead to having a much shorter period to respond, which is challenging when the OEP’s report is complicated or addressed to multiple bodies. A July 2025 report on environmental inspections, for example, was 200 pages long and spanned multiple bodies within and outside Defra Group. Nevertheless, the government has responded to most section 29 reports within 3 months.
The OEP has used its powers to advise on changes to environmental law, for example, relating to the then Planning and Infrastructure Bill and the revision of the EIP.
The environmental review and judicial review - power to apply in urgent cases have not been tested as the OEP has not yet used these powers. The OEP has however intervened in a small number of significant judicial review proceedings.
The Secretary of State and The Department of Agriculture, Environment and Rural Affairs (DAERA) have between them paid to the OEP such sums as they consider are reasonably sufficient to enable the OEP to carry out its functions. The OEP’s budget from Defra has been protected for 3 years from 2026 to 2027.
The OEP has launched 6 investigations in England but resolved a much larger number of interventions without a formal investigation. Defra has co-operated with these investigations, providing timely responses to information notices. The OEP’s report on combined sewer overflows (December 2025) found that “Throughout this process, all public authorities engaged constructively, with a focus on the outcome of protecting the environment.” More recently, the OEP has closed an investigation into the emergency authorisation of neonicotinoids, with the Chief Regulatory Officer stating, “During the investigation there was significant and constructive engagement between the OEP and Defra.” (July 2026).
Confidentiality of proceedings has not been tested in detail. In 2025 the Information Commissioner issued a decision notice relating to a decision the OEP made in relation to a request for information under the EIR. The Commissioner upheld the OEP’s decision not to disclose, citing regulation 12(5)(b) of the EIR (disclosure would adversely affect the course of justice).
The OEP Board has been established and several extensions, re-appointments and new appointments made.
In exercising functions in respect of the OEP, the Secretary of State and the Northern Ireland department must have regard to the need to protect the OEP’s independence. Secretary of State has taken this duty seriously. This has been demonstrated by Dame Glenys Stacey telling the Environment Audit Committee in January 2026 that the OEP had demonstrated its independence: “I cannot think of any single issue or moment where we have been anything other than completely independent and I think that pattern is set. I think that is what is needed and what Parliament expected, so that is really good.” [HC1653 Q15].

3.1.4 Preliminary assessment: EIP, APRs, EPPS and OEP
Northern Ireland has now met the key legislative requirements under the Act in relation to Environmental Improvement Plans, although delivery was delayed due to the absence of an executive. The first EIP was published in September 2024 and the first APR was published in January 2026, establishing the required statutory framework.
In practice, implementation has progressed from framework development to initial delivery. The EIP provides a comprehensive cross government framework structured around 6 Strategic Environmental Outcomes and supported by defined actions across departments. Governance arrangements, including the EIP Project Board, have enabled coordination across delivery partners.
The first EIP APR provides an initial evidence base for assessing progress. It demonstrates that systems for monitoring and reporting are in place, including the Outcome Indicator Framework. Early evidence indicates progress in areas such as air quality, marine protection, and climate governance, alongside improved transparency and data availability. However, implementation remains at an early stage, and measurable environmental outcomes are limited. A significant proportion of indicators show no change, while evidence gaps and data limitations remain. Progress has also been affected by resourcing constraints, delayed policy delivery, and the complexity of cross-departmental actions.
Overall, the legislative framework is now operational and functioning as intended, but it is too early to assess its impact on environmental outcomes. Further scrutiny is likely, as additional reporting cycles are completed and more robust evidence emerges.
The Environmental Principles Policy Statement (EPPS) has been developed and published, establishing the statutory framework required by the Act. The associated duty on departments to have due regard to the statement came into force on 2 September 2026. Implementation remains at an early stage. In practice, the EPPS provides a clear and comprehensive articulation of environmental principles and their application in policymaking. It aligns with principles previously embedded through EU law and provides a consistent domestic framework.
At present, there is limited evidence of the EPPS influencing policy outcomes. Departments are still developing awareness, capability, and processes to apply the due regard duty effectively. Consistency of application across departments will take time to establish, and further guidance and support will be required. The introduction of a statutory due regard duty represents a strengthening of environmental governance. However, effective implementation will depend on how well departments evidence consideration of the principles in policymaking.
Overall, the framework is in place and conceptually robust, but it is too early to assess effectiveness in practice. Further scrutiny may be required as evidence emerges of how the duty is applied across departments.
The OEP has been established and is operational in Northern Ireland, providing an independent oversight function that has begun to influence both compliance with statutory duties and the development of environmental policy.
In practice, the OEP has demonstrated its ability to drive compliance. This is evidenced by its investigation into DAERA’s failure to publish the first Environmental Improvement Plan (EIP) by the statutory deadline, which was subsequently resolved following publication in September 2024. This indicates that the OEP is exercising its enforcement role in a proportionate and effective manner in response to systemic issues.
The OEP has also begun to influence policy development through its advisory and scrutiny functions. Advice provided by the OEP highlighted the need for clearer priorities, targets, and evaluation mechanisms, which is reflected in the structure of the EIP and subsequent reporting. This is also evidenced in the fact that the OEP was consulted on the creation of the Northern Ireland EPPS and all 16 of its recommendations were incorporated.
The introduction of independent monitoring and annual reporting has strengthened accountability arrangements, replacing the previous EU-level oversight framework. The requirement for DAERA to respond formally to OEP reports introduces a structured cycle of scrutiny and follow-up.
Since its establishment, the OEP has undertaken a small number of interventions in Northern Ireland, most notably its investigation into the delayed publication of the EIP. It also now has an ongoing statutory role in monitoring progress against the EIP and reporting annually within 6 months of DAERA’s APR. The first report was published on 24 June 2026, following publication of the APR in January 2026, and DAERA will be required to respond to the findings. However, implementation remains at an early stage. The OEP has yet to complete a full monitoring and reporting cycle, and there is limited evidence of its impact on long-term environmental outcomes.
Overall, the OEP is operating as intended, providing independent scrutiny and reinforcing compliance with environmental law. A more definitive assessment of its effectiveness will depend on future reporting cycles and the extent to which its scrutiny drives measurable improvements in policy implementation and environmental outcomes.

Preliminary assessment: Powers to make charging schemes
Waste exemptions charging was successfully introduced by the Environment Agency in England in 2025. Extant, free to register waste exemptions last for 3 years and remain valid until they expire so it will take time for a full picture to develop. Smoke control areas: amendments of the Clean Air Act 1993: preliminary assessment
The Act changed enforcement of SCAs from a criminal to a civil regime and introduced a financial penalty system for failure to comply with SCA requirements. Many Local Authorities have been raising awareness of SCAs with the public, and Defra is aware of Local Authorities issuing warning letters and conducting inspections in response to complaints and breaches, with some cases of fines being issued.
Progress has been made in regulating the sale and acquisition of the most polluting solid fuels used in SCAs. The Act removed the limit on fines for non-compliant fuel sales and introduced a requirement that retailers must notify customers of relevant legal restrictions so that there is greater awareness that it is an offence. The introduction of the ‘Ready to Burn’ labelling for compliant fuels under the Air Quality (Domestic Solid Fuels Standards) (England) Regulations 2020 makes it easier for consumers and retailers to know which fuels meet the relevant requirements.
The Act also removed the statutory nuisance exemption for private dwellings in SCAs. This enabled Local Authorities to more easily act against those who emit smoke from their homes, where the smoke can be considered prejudicial to human health or causing a nuisance.
As these amendments required new enforcement activity by Local Authorities, Defra has provided additional funding to each Local Authority with a confirmed SCA from 2022 to 2023 through to 2025 to 2026. Defra will continue to support Local Authorities with advice and guidance about SCAs through the Air Quality Hub and will keep the ongoing effectiveness of the SCA statutory regime under review, including barriers to enforcement.
Additionally, the Act enabled Local Authorities to extend SCA orders to inland waterways vessels. A total of 7 Local Authorities have used these powers. To facilitate people with permanent moorings within SCAs to upgrade their solid fuel appliances, the Act introduced a requirement for Local Authorities to provide reimbursement for certain adaptations made. Through the Waterways Air Quality Grant Scheme, Defra provided £1.6 million to 7 Local Authorities between 2022 and 2026 to support this.

Preliminary assessment
Drainage and wastewater planning to assess risk to sewerage network and network capacity has been placed on a statutory footing, meeting the objectives of this section. It is a major development in how the water industry identifies need, risk, and plans infrastructure delivery and upgrades to meet that need.
The preparation of the first statutory round of DWMPs is underway, with publication of the final plans by the end of August 2028 to inform the 2029 Price Review process (PR29).

Effective long-term drainage and wastewater planning is essential to ensuring the sector is prepared for the risks facing their networks currently and, in the future. DWMPs produced because of the provisions will require companies to:
properly assess the capacity of their drainage and sewerage network
identify the risks currently facing that network
project the impact of future risks (including population growth, climate change and urban development)
plan necessary new or upgraded infrastructure to meet these risks
They will help water industry identify and tackle the root causes of problems in the network. As well as working with partners to deliver best-value solutions to these risks.
The requirements of the DWMP regime were expanded by section 5 of the Water (Special Measures) Act 2025. To require all DWMPs to address the “use that is made of nature-based solutions, technologies and facilities” within the undertakers’ networks. This ability to flexibly change the requirements of DWMP ensures an effective, relevant planning regime that can pivot to tackle new priorities.
6.2. Storm overflows (sections 80 to 84)
Section 80 placed new duties on government to prepare a plan for reducing storm overflow discharges and their adverse impacts on the environment and public health, and report on the progress of that plan. It also places duties on the Environment Agency and sewerage undertakers wholly or mainly in England to publish annual reports on storm overflows.
Section 81 set a duty for sewerage undertakers wholly or mainly in England to report in near real time on when a discharge from a storm overflow starts and stops.
Section 82 set a duty for sewerage undertakers wholly or mainly in England to continuously monitor the quality of water upstream and downstream of discharges from storm overflows and sewage treatment works.
Section 83 set a duty for sewerage undertakers wholly or mainly in England to secure a progressive reduction in the adverse impacts of discharges from their storm overflows.
Section 84 required government to publish a report on the actions which would be needed to eliminate discharges from the storm overflows of sewerage undertakers wholly or mainly in England and the costs and benefits of doing so.
Implementation
Sections 80 and 84 of the Act came into force automatically 2 months after the Act passed. Section 81 commenced partially on 17 May 2024 with the remaining elements commencing on 1 January 2025. Section 82 partially commenced on 3 November 2023 with other elements, but not all, commencing on 17 May 2024. In particular, subsection (1) is in force for the purposes of making regulations, but the duty on a sewerage undertaker to continuously monitor the quality of water upstream and downstream of an asset is not yet in force.
Water companies have been asked to install monitors at 25% of sites by 2030, aiming for 100% by 2035, with the pace of the rollout to be determined by the next price review.
Section 83 has not been commenced as it is considered for the time being that current water company actions, such as those to meet the targets under the Storm Overflows Discharge Reduction Plan (SODRP), are sufficient to meet the original policy aim.
Legal issues
A challenge was brought in relation to section 80 concerning the lawfulness of the SODRP published under section 80 in August 2022. The challenge was dismissed in the Summary of Judgment: Wildfish Conservation and Marine Conservation Society and others v Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs and The Environment Agency.
The judicial review was brought by Wildfish Conservation, the Marine Conservation
Society, Richard Haward’s Oysters, and Hugo Tagholm.
Other reviews (if applicable)
While not of direct relevance to Environment Act sections 80 to 84, the OEP launched an investigation into Defra, the Environment Agency and Ofwat on the regulation of network Combined Sewer Overflows (storm overflows) in England in 2022. In December 2025, the OEP published its final report, highlighting the corrective actions that Defra, Ofwat, and the Environment Agency have taken to rectify issues that the OEP had highlighted in its investigation.
Preliminary assessment
All storm overflows now have monitors installed recording the frequency and duration of their operation. The programme to install event duration monitors on storm overflows was completed in December 2023. As of January 2025, water companies have been publishing data through regional maps on their individual websites. Water UK’s National Storm Overflow Hub consolidates live data from all 9 English water companies into a single, publicly accessible platform. The legislation has achieved the aim of monitoring the frequency and duration of discharges, including making that monitoring information publicly available.
The Environment Act places duties on the Environment Agency and sewerage undertakers to report Event Duration Monitoring (EDM) data annually. This is currently being achieved, with these reports being published annually.
This has led to the Environment Agency publishing consistent EDM data. This evidence, in its identifying of storm overflow operation, has led to development of compliance measures, and intelligence-led permitting.
The Storm Overflows Discharge Reduction Plan was first published in August 2022 and updated the Storm Overflows Discharge Reduction Plan in September 2023. The Storm Overflows Evidence Report and Storm Overflows Discharge Reduction Plan Impact assessment on key targets proposed in the SODRP were published in November 2021 and September 2022, respectively.
A progress report on the storm overflows discharge reduction plan was published in December 2025, covering the first 3 years of what will be a 28-year programme of work to 2050. Together, these have achieved the aim of publishing a report on the elimination of discharges from storm overflows, preparing a plan for reducing storm overflow discharges and their adverse impacts on the environment and public health, and reporting on the progress of that plan. Further progress reports are required to be published every 5 years, with the next iteration due in 2030.
To deliver on the SODRP, we are expecting £60 billion of investment to be delivered across England by 2050, which should result in a reduction of over 300,000 spills from storm overflows per year by 2050, and ensure that no storm overflow is permitted to spill more than 10 times per year on average.

7.1. Biodiversity gain in planning (section 98 to 101)

None.
Other reviews
The National Audit Office published a report on Implementing statutory biodiversity net gain on 17 May 2024, shortly after mandatory BNG went live on the scale needed to meet demand, ensuring compliance, and effective monitoring to assess performance.
Preliminary assessment
The feedback and evidence government has gathered suggests BNG implementation has been broadly successful for larger developments. With a greater number of developers seeking ecological advice earlier in the process and designing their site layouts to avoid impact to biodiversity and seeking to deliver more habitat onsite.
For those who are unable to meet all of their BNG onsite, an off-site market has also rapidly developed with over 10,000 hectares of habitat created or enhanced currently listed on the national register, and many more sites preparing for registration, thereby helping to increase private investment into nature recovery.
However, there was emerging evidence that some smaller developments and specific types of development were finding BNG a challenge. As a result, in May 2025, government consulted on “Improving the implementation of Biodiversity Net Gain for minor, medium and brownfield development”. It proposed changes to exemptions, the metric process, increasing flexibility to go off-site for minor development, and changes for brownfield developments on ecologically valuable sites.
At the same time, government launched a consultation on how mandatory BNG will apply for nationally significant infrastructure projects (NSIPs).
On 15 April 2026, government published responses to both consultations including a timeline for implementing the changes confirmed to the BNG system throughout 2026. This includes confirmation that BNG will apply to NSIPs from 2 November 2026.
Government has introduced a new exemption for sites of 0.2 hectares or below, a new exemption for temporary development, and repealed the current self and custom build exemption. There will also be further targeted changes to exemptions for parks and playing fields and biodiversity related development. Other changes include amendments to the biodiversity gain hierarchy for minor development, and targeted changes to the statutory biodiversity metric tool.
Also on 15 April, a further BNG consultation was launched on considering a targeted exemption for brownfield residential development. This closed on 10 June, and a government response will be published in due course.
Government provides funding to local authorities and other local planning authorities for BNG implementation. £46 million grant funding was provided over the period 2021 to 2022 through to 2025 to 2026, with a further £29 million committed for the 2026 to 2027 through to 2028 to 2029 period, which will be distributed through the Revenue Support Grant, a central part of the Local Government Finance Settlement.
Government continues to fund the Planning Advisory Service to help Local Planning Authorities prepare for and meet their BNG obligations.

This section puts spatial planning for nature on a statutory footing and consistently maps important existing habitats and opportunities to create or restore habitat through Local Nature Recovery Strategies.
7.3.1 Implementation

Regulations made under section 105(4) and statutory guidance made under section 106(5) were laid before Parliament on 23 March 2023. Other reviews:
The OEP published “A review of Local Nature Recovery Strategies and their role in contributing to nature recovery commitments in England” on 19 June 2025 on the crucial role of LNRS to inform local decision-making, set out local priorities and deliver the Environment Act targets. It found they need to be prepared and published at pace to substantially contribute. Government’s response to the OEP’s report was published on the 16 October 2025.
Preliminary assessment
Overall, whilst some responsible authorities have taken longer to publish their LNRS than the government had hoped, the system of strategies created by sections 104 to 108 is now firmly established and the use of these strategies has been embedded in legislation and policy.
On 30 June 2023, 48 “responsible authorities” were appointed by the Secretary of State for the Environment Food and Rural Affairs to lead the preparation of the LNRS for their area. These 48 areas together cover the whole of England. On this date, Defra launched a ‘data viewer’ to provide responsible authorities with a national habitat map and other relevant datasets.
There is no legal deadline by when LNRSs must be published but the government estimated that it would take responsible authorities 12 to 18 months to complete the preparation of their LNRS based on 5 pilots. The first LNRS (for the West of England Combined Authority) was published on 7 November 2024. However, most LNRSs have progressed slower than originally estimated. As of the end of September 2026, 45 of the 48 LNRSs have been published, and the final 3 are approaching publication.
Whilst preparation of LNRSs has generally been slower than expected, feedback on the preparation process and on the quality of LNRSs produced has been positive, as evidenced by the OEP’s report.
The EIPs in 2023 and 2025 demonstrated consistent strong support from government for the importance of LNRSs in delivering environmental targets set under sections 1 and 3 of the Environment Act, and other national environmental commitments. The role of LNRSs in targeting habitat creation and improvement to meet biodiversity net gain requirements has been set out in the biodiversity metric user guide. This was first published on 29 November 2023.
The Levelling Up and Regeneration Act 2023 and the Planning and Infrastructure Act 2025 have, together, created duties for neighbourhood plans, local plans, minerals and waste plans, supplementary plans and spatial development strategies to “take account” of relevant LNRSs. Defra and its arms-length bodies use published LNRSs to inform decisions on the allocation of public funding across agriculture, tree planting and natural flood management.
The government’s English Devolution policy, set out in the English Devolution White Paper and enabled by the English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 built on the roles and responsibilities set out in the Environment Act (which relate to the preparation, publication, review and republication of LNRSs) by establishing a role for responsible authorities to oversee the delivery of the strategy that they have prepared. The scope of this role has been co-developed with the responsible authorities and focuses on embedding the LNRS in planning and other local decision-making processes, working with partners to develop LNRS proposals into fundable projects and monitoring and reporting on progress made. Funding for this delivery role has been allocated by Defra until the end of the current Spending Review period.
The English Devolution White Paper also set out government’s intention to appoint Strategic Authorities as responsible authorities over time. The English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 establishes the legal framework to empower these Authorities and sets out their areas of competence. The LNRS responsible authority role forms the core of the environment and climate change competence, while Defra reviews what else might be devolved. This enables strategic authorities to consider nature recovery and environmental improvement alongside other competences like transport, health and economic development and seek opportunities for them to support each other.
The LNRS regulations require the Secretary of State to announce when LNRSs are to be reviewed and republished every 3 to 10 years. The period when the first review and republication could be announced began on 13 April 2026 and continues until 12 April 2033. The Secretary of State has not yet decided when to call the first review and republication but has committed in the 2025 EIP to determine and announce the timing for this.
7.4. Conservation (sections 109 to 111)
Section 109 has provided Natural England with the power to prepare and publish Species Conservation Strategies (SCS) for improving the conservation status of any species of fauna or flora.
Section 110 provided Natural England with a power to prepare and publish Protected Site Strategies (PSS) to improve the conservation and management of protected sites and manage the impact of plans, projects or other activities on the conservation and management of the protected site. The Act specifies what may be included and who Natural England must consult in preparing a PSS. It also places a duty on the specified public authorities to cooperate with Natural England when they are preparing a PSS, so far as it is relevant to their functions, and gives Secretary of State a power to issue guidance on the application of that duty. The Act also includes a duty to have regard to a PSS so far as it is relevant to any duty the relevant person has under the legislation specified in the Act.
Section 111 introduced amendments to the licensing provisions in the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (WCA81) and the Conservation of Habitats and Species
Regulations 2017 (“2017 Regulations”), providing greater consistency across the licensing regimes. These:
made it possible to grant licences for development related activities impacting certain species of animal and plant
facilitates the wider implementation of landscape scale licensing for development purposes
reduced the administrative burden of licensing on customers and the licensing authority (Natural England)
Implementation
Section 109 commenced on 30 September 2022.

Legal issues
Section 10(1)(c) of the WCA81, which was inserted by section 111(1)(b) of the Act, includes an error. Section 10 provides that offences under section 9 are also displaced by the granting of a licence under regulation 55 of the 2017 Regulations. However, the provision in subsection (1)(c) erroneously refers to the licence being granted by Natural
England under regulation 55. No such power vests in Natural England directly, it is instead given to the ‘relevant licensing body’, which includes Natural England in some cases, but the Secretary of State in others. The error has not yet been corrected as there has not been a suitable legislative vehicle.
Other reviews
The OEP report ‘Review of implementation of laws for terrestrial and freshwater protected sites in England’ published on 3 December 2025, highlighted that the most significant change in approach in this area (addressing the main causes of poor protected site condition) has been legislating for and piloting PSS, and the government’s commitment to scaling up this work. It also highlights PSS development work with respect to green finance and green commerce (opportunities for private investment in nature restoration).
Annex E to the 2025 Habitats Regulations Post Implementation Review provides an analysis of wildlife licences issued under the Habitats Regulations but does not evaluate section 111 specifically.
Preliminary assessment: Species Conservation Strategies
The Act does not fully define the scope of Species Conservation Strategies (SCS). To advise Defra, Natural England refined thinking on how to define and realise the full power of SCS. Three pilot projects (for water vole, hazel dormouse, and widespread reptiles) tested whether strategic licensing approaches, as part of a SCS, could maximise conservation outcomes for certain species whilst enabling economic growth.
Natural England also explored the potential to use SCS in a more ambitious and holistic way, through workshop discussions, both internal and with external stakeholders.
Through these workshops, stakeholders formed consensus on a recommended purpose definition for SCS, species selection criteria and advice on practical implementation of the SCS. Stakeholder consensus was enthusiastic about the potential for SCS to achieve nature recovery.
Focus on the Planning and Infrastructure Act 2025, and associated resourcing decisions, has impacted Natural England’s ability to progress the pilot projects and the timeline of delivery of advice to Defra on the future use of SCS. This has limited the implementation and benefit realisation of SCS to date.
Preliminary assessment: Protected sites strategies
Natural England ran a 3 year research and development project (2022 to 2025), including running 5 pilots, to test components of the PSS approach and how and when to deploy PSS effectively. A key focus of the project was to refine the approach enabled by PSS by bringing together partners to tackle off-site pressures and improve site condition.
Building on the test and learn approach taken in the project, Natural England will deliver at least 20 Protected Site Strategies across England by 2030.
Preliminary assessment: Wildlife conservation licences
The amendments introduced under section 111 have been successful in achieving their objectives. They have:
allowed Natural England to develop and launch a Species Conservation Strategy for the water vole in East Anglia. Natural England is now developing a similar strategy for widespread reptiles. These strategies are primarily designed to benefit development and infrastructure projects
allowed developers to apply for licences for activities that impact non-avian animals and plants protected under the WCA81. So far licences for 11 different species have been granted
allowed Natural England to extend licences where appropriate, reducing the burden on customers and itself. This has been a particular benefit for District Level Licensing for great crested newts. Previously, these schemes required licences to be reissued every 2 years, creating a significant administrative burden for Natural England and partner organisations delivering schemes
provided reassurance to stakeholders that licences are only granted in appropriate circumstances
ensured that licensing provisions for animals and plants in the WCA81 are compliant with the Bern Convention
Use of the new exception (introduced by sub-section 111(1)) has been largely, if not entirely, obviated by Natural England issuing a single licence covering both the WCA81 and 2017 Regulations where both apply to the same activity. This became possible due to the amendment introduced by sub-section 111(2). This provision has, therefore, proved unnecessary.

Preliminary assessment
This section is achieving its objectives to facilitate voluntary, legally-binding private agreements to conserve the natural or heritage features of land.
Since the Act was introduced, Defra has set the criteria for designation as a Responsible Body (RB), published a list of approved RBs, and set up a quarterly RB forum to discuss best practice on development and implementation of conservation covenants. Applications to become an RB are reviewed by a panel, which approves them based on the published criteria. Current RBs include Local Authorities, environmental NGOs, and independent environmental consultancies.
RBs must submit an annual return showing how many conservation covenants they have entered into and the area of land covered. RBs may also submit additional information on the types of habitats covered, any work that has been done to create, enhance and restore habitats, and any potential future covenants.
As of 31 March 2026, there were 41 Responsible Bodies and at least 138 conservation covenants in place. This level of interest in obtaining RB status and the increasing use of conservation covenants shows that they are a respected legal tool to secure conservation benefits, supporting the Act’s objective to allow land to be maintained for conservation over the long-term. However, since most covenants are relatively new, insight into their efficacy in securing those benefits when the land is sold or passed on is limited.
The majority of covenants, so far, are terrestrial. Some RBs have plans for this to expand into riverine and intertidal habitats. Most covenants are secured for Biodiversity Net Gain and other nature recovery projects, contributing to the Act’s objective to protect and enhance biodiversity. They include meadow, scrub, mixed and native woodland, hedgerows, species rich traditional hay meadows, floodplains and ponds. There are also some covenants secured for the purposes of securing land use change in connection with the sale of nutrient credits to developers (nutrient neutrality) with more in the pipeline.
The use of conservation covenants to secure land use change for both regulatory compliance and voluntary purposes is in line with government’s expectations for these instruments. The government will continue to monitor the uptake and efficacy of conservation covenants as their use expands and matures.

Preliminary assessment
This section is achieving its objectives to keep UK chemicals legislation updated.
Under paragraph 1 of Schedule 21 to the Act the Secretary of State may make regulations under this paragraph only if they consider that the provision made is consistent with Article 1 of the REACH Regulation, and they must publish an explanation to that effect. The statement must be published no later than the start of the consultation on the proposed provision. Two SIs have been made under this power to extend the deadlines by which companies must submit registration information and the statements for them were published alongside the consultations.
Under paragraph 5 of Schedule 21 to the Act the Secretary of State must consult before making provision to amend the REACH Regulation. Defra carried out public consultations on Extending the UK REACH Submission Deadlines in 2022 and Extending the UK REACH Transitional Registration Submission Deadlines in 2026 before making these SIs.
Responses to the consultations were from companies and representative trade bodies in the chemicals and related sectors, as well as from environmental and other NGOs. The government has published its response to each consultation: 2023 Regulations and 2026 Regulations. In each case the consultation response sets out the views of stakeholders alongside the government’s intention of how to proceed.
As required by paragraph 3 of Schedule 21 to the Act, the Scottish and Welsh government also consented to the proposed regulations.

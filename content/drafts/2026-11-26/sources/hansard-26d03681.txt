The Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero (Ms Polly Billington)
With permission, I will make a statement on the energy price cap. Yesterday, Ofgem announced that from 1 January to 31 March 2027, the price cap will rise to £2,166 for a typical dual-fuel household paying by direct debit.
Let me start with the context. This is a stark reminder that 40 years of Conservative privatisation has left Britain dangerously exposed to international gas markets and global events. Ofgem is clear that the price cap rise is driven primarily by soaring international wholesale gas prices as a consequence of the war in Iran.
I know what this latest price spike means for families in this country—the people who write to colleagues in this House, like the son of a vulnerable 94-year-old lady facing electricity bills of £60 to £70 a month, which she is finding hard to cover, given her small pension, or the lady who wrote,
“I am currently in bed under an electric blanket to avoid putting on heating. I can hardly feel my fingers.”
They are desperately worried as we head into the coldest month of the year, and I share their concern. That concern drives our duty to fix our broken energy system, which is simply not delivering for people. Until they can afford the energy that they need to stay safe, warm and well, the energy system is not delivering.
Today, I will set out three things that we are doing to fix this situation: the support we are providing now, the action we are taking to fix the energy market and how, finally, we will protect Britain from the fossil-fuel price shocks that are driving prices up. Let me start with the price cap. It is set independently by Ofgem and limits the amount that suppliers can charge their customers on default tariffs for each unit of gas and electricity they use, and the maximum standing charge for access to the grid.
It is important to be clear about what the £2,166 figure means. It is not a maximum bill. It represents the annualised bill for a typical household. Households will pay more or less depending on their energy use, their payment method and where they live. The increase also covers only the next three months. In tangible terms, as Martin Lewis explained this week, a household on Ofgem’s typical-use figure will pay around £57 a month more because of the increase, or roughly £170 extra over the period covered by this price cap.
Fixing this is a long-term structural project that requires urgent action, but we know that households need support here and now. I know how hard it is, and how people are struggling, so we are delivering that support. From January, our household energy payment will get help to those who need it most, with 20 million households automatically receiving an extra £100 straight into their bank accounts, where councils hold direct debit details. Those who do not pay by direct debit will be contacted by their council to arrange payment via bank transfer or council tax credit.
We are providing equivalent funding to the devolved nations, with the Scottish and Welsh Governments already confirming that they will launch similar payments. There is also £100 million for local authorities to offer discretionary support to struggling households who need help but are not eligible for the core rebate. This is on top of action that we have already taken to remove £150 of costs from bills from April 2026, and the extension of the temporary VAT cut on electricity bills until the end of June 2027. We have also extended the £150 warm home discount, and around 6 million households will be eligible for that this winter.
I want to encourage people to check their tariff, shop around for the best deal and consider switching to save money on their energy bills. Changing to a fixed tariff or a direct debit could save some households £160 on average. There is also helpful advice available for consumers on how to reduce how much energy they use. Yesterday we launched our home energy advice service, offering personalised Government-backed advice for families. It helps them check whether they qualify for a grant, and helps them access simple guides on how to make their home warmer and cheaper to run.
I recognise that these measures do not make the upcoming increase announced yesterday painless, but they provide meaningful support to households this winter, particularly for those who need it most, while we tackle the structural issues behind these sky-high bills. That means changing how our energy market works. We are confronted with the legacy of 40 years of privatisation. Decisions made in Parliament in the 1980s led us to the mess we are in today. There was a misplaced conviction that free markets and competition would deliver efficiencies and lower costs. That conviction was from in a time of abundant fossil fuels that has passed. It was an age before millions of homes could generate, store and flex their own electricity—an age when we believed that we could rely on international gas markets indefinitely and not worry about British energy security. Those assumptions no longer hold. Ukraine in 2022 proved that, and the Iran war must be the final wake-up call; we have to change. We cannot be hostages to fortune anymore, because our people, who face months of cutting back on life’s essentials this winter, end up paying the price.
Our future system needs to be built for clean, reliable, secure, home-grown, affordable energy. We need a system in which energy consumers can produce their own electricity. The grid must be able to accommodate both those things. Energy is changing, and the market needs to change with it. We need to change the way we regulate the market, so that consumers experience positive change at home. That includes bringing in smart tariffs that reward families for using cheap, clean electricity when it is abundant; better deals for prepayment customers, including the certainty of fixed tariffs; packages that bring together clean technologies—solar, batteries and heat pumps—and cheaper tariffs, giving households a genuine route out of poverty; and having a system where we make good use of modern technology, electrification and data to offer consumers choice, control and the ability to predict what they will pay.
Alongside that, we are making the energy market fairer, including by removing standing charges from gas bills, tackling energy debt, targeting help at those who need it most, and ensuring that Ofgem has the powers that it needs to protect people from scams and poor service. That is the process we are undertaking at the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, and we have a long way to go, but we are determined to fix this system so that it delivers for the British people.
Finally, yesterday’s announcement underlines once again why we must tackle the underlying cause of high and volatile energy bills: the fact that we are so reliant on international gas prices and fossil fuel markets. If I am honest, I would not start from here, more than four years after the crisis in Ukraine. The Conservatives spent £38 billion on untargeted help, but did nothing to wean us off the cause of the problem. That suggests to me that for far too long, the challenge of reforming our energy market has been put in the “too difficult” box, even when the consequences are severe for our economy and the cost of living. We will not shirk that challenge. We need to put control over the price of our energy back in the hands of the British people.
We are delivering a more secure, affordable and resilient energy system, built on the transition to clean home-grown power and a reformed electricity market. That is how we reduce our exposure to this volatility and give households greater protection from future price shocks. It is how we bring down energy bills for good. It is how families and businesses can look forward with confidence, allowing them to spend, invest and put more money back into our economy.
We want to see a future where the British people no longer pay the price for a crisis half a world away—a crisis, by the way, that the Conservatives wanted us to get embroiled in. We will keep looking at what we can do to support people in the here and now, but we also need to get to the root causes and fix them, identify the future risks and protect ourselves from them, and find the opportunities to do things better and seize them. Now is our time to do it. I commend this statement to the House.

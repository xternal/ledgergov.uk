Ministerial foreword
Across England, trees are woven into the fabric of our lives. They shape the character of our landscapes, from our towns and cities to our farms, villages and woodlands. They support wildlife, contribute towards net zero, provide sustainable timber, and improve the places where we live and work. Whether it is a woodland that supports local jobs, a tree planted in memory of a loved one, or the canopy that provides shade on a hot summer’s day, trees make a lasting contribution to our environment, our economy and our communities.
Their importance is only growing. We are facing a changing climate, with more frequent periods of extreme heat, drought, flooding and wildfires. At the same time, pests and diseases continue to threaten the health of our trees and woodlands, while nature faces unprecedented pressures. As these challenges become more pronounced, so too does the need for healthy, resilient and well-managed trees across our landscapes.
Trees can help us respond to many of these challenges. They provide habitat for wildlife and contribute to nature recovery. They capture and store carbon, helping us tackle climate change, while supporting adaptation by cooling our towns and cities and providing shade for livestock and wildlife during hotter summers. They help slow the flow of water, improve soil health and make our landscapes more resilient to both drought and flooding.
Yet the value of trees extends beyond the environmental and economic benefits they provide. Trees strengthen our connection to the places we live. They create opportunities for communities to come together through the public spaces they improve. They boost our health and wellbeing, shade our towns and cities, and help create landscapes that people are proud to call home.
The future of England’s trees will be built through partnership. Whether it is a community group planting and restoring a local woodland, volunteers working with a Community Forest, or a farmer planting trees on their land to provide shade for livestock, each action contributes to a larger national effort.
This plan will bring communities from across the country closer to nature. From the significant commitments made here, communities will have greater and fairer access to nature spanning all the way from the 3 new national forests we are establishing and the streets where they live, through to the trees we are planting in less advantaged communities. This will ensure that, wherever nature thrives, these spaces will be for everyone to steward, cherish and enjoy.
At a time when the impacts of climate change are becoming increasingly visible and many species continue to decline, there is growing recognition that healthy, resilient trees and woodlands are essential natural assets.
This government is steadfastly committed to addressing both the climate and nature crises. This plan sets out a shared vision of a healthy and resilient natural environment, brought closer to communities for them to enjoy.
Jenny Riddell-Carpenter MP
Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for Nature, Coasts and Rural Affairs

It will improve the woodlands we already have and create the healthy, resilient forests we need.
Climate change is already reshaping where and how our trees grow. The plan puts climate adaptation at the heart of woodland creation and management so that our trees and forests can cope with new pressures. It will support decisions on species, provenance and woodland structure that respond to changing conditions including drought, wildfire and storms, strengthen resilience to pests and diseases, and safeguard biodiversity and timber supply.
The actions we take under the plan will bring more woodland into sustainable management, improving habitats, supporting rural employment and supplying renewable materials for a low-carbon economy. Woodland creation will complement this work, with success measured by condition and resilience as well as hectares planted.

Collectively the actions we take will help England to grow more of the timber we use, helping to substitute carbon-intensive materials in construction and manufacturing. Well-designed mixed forests, integrated with landscape, water and nature, will diversify species, age and structure. I welcome the opportunity it gives us to bring our knowledge, experience and shared care for woodlands together.
Trees reward foresight, and the woodlands we nurture today will be enjoyed by generations to come. By improving existing woodlands, restoring ancient habitats and creating resilient, productive forests, the plan will build a stronger woodland inheritance for nature, people and the economy. I warmly support its ambition and look forward to working with everyone who will help bring it to life.

The value of trees, woodlands and forests
Our vision
an expanded, more resilient, nature-rich and productive treescape in England, where trees, woodlands and forests support thriving communities, nature recovery, climate resilience and sustainable economic growth for generations to come
The benefits of trees, woodlands and forests
Trees have multiple benefits. They provide an estimated £13.1 billion (in 2026 prices) each year in economic, social and environmental value in the United Kingdom (Plant biosecurity strategy for Great Britain (2023 to 2028)).
Our trees support public health and wellbeing and flourishing communities
People made an estimated 326 million visits to the nation’s forests last year (Forestry Commission, 2026). Trees help to reduce stress, enable more active lifestyles and support access to, and connection with, nature. Increasing access to trees, woodlands and other green spaces, particularly where it is currently limited and in less advantaged areas, will help ensure that more people can benefit from nature close to where they live, work and spend time. This forms an important part of ensuring nature is accessible and enjoyable for all, including our commitment to make sure that everyone has access to green or blue spaces within a 15-minute walk from home.
Trees are an important part of the economy
Trees enliven our high streets and city centres. They support rural businesses by supplying timber, wood products and horticultural stock. They contribute to a thriving leisure and recreation industry, from camping and glamping experiences to adventure activities such as mountain biking and paintballing. Our arboriculture and forestry sectors underpin this, helping to ensure that our trees deliver the benefits needed for our economy and communities.
Timber is essential to many of the products and supply chains we rely on
Global demand for wood products is projected to increase by roughly one-third by 2050 (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2026). Timber is used in everything from paper, packaging, furniture and a wide range of everyday products. It plays a key role in delivering housing and infrastructure that is environmentally positive, contributing to lower-carbon construction and manufacturing. At the individual building level, carbon storage is approximately 50% higher in timber-framed homes than in masonry homes (Climate Change Committee, 2019).
The environmental benefits provided by our trees are vital parts of our natural infrastructure
Trees support thriving habitats and wildlife. Oak trees, for example, can support up to 2,300 species, including birds, mammals, fungi and invertebrates (UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology, 2019). Ancient woodlands and ancient and veteran trees, in particular, provide unique and irreplaceable resources for a wealth of biodiversity.
Trees can filter pollution, improve our resilience to flooding and buffer and improve river habitats. They can enhance water quality by reducing soil erosion and runoff. Appropriately placed woodlands can help to reduce up to 80% of pollutants draining down from upslope agricultural activities. Woodlands between the field edge and a river can decrease sediment run-off by 90 to 100%, nutrient losses by 20 to 80%, and reduce pesticide loss in run-off by 60 to 100% (Forestry Commission The benefits of woodland creation: woods and water).
Trees are important for climate change mitigation and adaptation
Trees capture carbon dioxide as they grow, store carbon as timber in our built environment and substitute carbon-intensive materials. Forest Research has estimated that trees in the UK sequester 16.5 million tonnes of carbon dioxide (MtCO₂) annually (Chapter 4 – Carbon, Forestry Statistics 2026). Our efforts on tree planting, establishment and management are expected to deliver 10% of the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs’ (Defra) contribution to England’s carbon budgets in 2050.
Trees build resilience in communities and on land, helping adapt urban and rural landscapes to climate change and protecting from environmental hazards. They provide shade and cooling for people, livestock and wildlife, improve heat and drought resilience, mitigate flooding and help communities withstand more frequent periods of extreme weather.
The need for action
England is one of the least densely forested countries in Europe. We have 10% woodland cover (Forestry Statistics 2026) compared to 47% for Europe as a whole (Forestry Facts and Figures 2026). While differences in geography, land availability and competing demands on land use mean comparisons with European counterparts are not straightforward, there is significant scope to increase tree cover in England. The benefits of doing so are manifold.
But our trees, woodlands and forests face unprecedented threats. Climate change is altering the rhythm of the seasons and increasing the likelihood of drought, flooding, wildfires and storm damage. And the warming climate and an increasingly interconnected world have accelerated the arrival of new pests and diseases, with 21 new threats in the last 16 years compared with 5 in the previous 30 years (Plant biosecurity strategy for Great Britain (2023 to 2028)).
Only 56% of England’s woodlands are sustainably managed, which has remained broadly unchanged for over 10 years (Forestry Commission, 2026). This leaves their condition vulnerable to decline. Damage is occurring through excessive foraging by deer and bark-stripping by grey squirrels, limiting their use, growth and regeneration when uncontrolled.
We have lost around 50,000 hectares of productive conifer woodland in England since 2010 because of habitat restoration, woodland restructuring, development pressures and low rates of replacement conifer planting (Forest Research, 2026). This has reduced the future supply of domestically grown softwood, with further declines forecast over the next 50 years (Forest Research 2026). Without action, this could make it more challenging to maintain a resilient domestic timber supply.
Trees take a long time to establish, mature and deliver their full benefits. Concerted action is needed now to drive progress towards achieving our climate mitigation, climate adaptation, nature recovery, flood mitigation, water quality, societal and economic growth goals.
The approach
This action plan sets out a framework for government, industry and stakeholders to achieve our vision. It presents priorities and actions to ensure our treescape grows, becomes more resilient to threats and continues to deliver benefits, by:
empowering communities to access, engage with and steward trees.
maximising the contribution of trees, woodlands and forests to growing our economy.
protecting existing trees, woodlands and forests from threats.
improving their condition, diversity and ability to adapt, so they are better able to withstand and bounce back from threats.
expanding and connecting them, to increase the extent of their benefits.
ensuring decisions are made using the best available evidence and science
Government targets will help track progress towards our vision. The statutory tree target is to increase tree canopy and woodland cover to at least 16.5% of all land in England by 2050 from the 2022 baseline of 14.9% (equivalent to a net increase of 210,000 hectares). This action plan will support delivery of the interim target set in the Environmental Improvement Plan (EIP) 2025 to achieve a 0.33% increase in tree canopy and woodland cover by 2030 (equivalent to a net increase of 43,000 hectares), as well as longer-term delivery beyond 2030. Its actions also support the EIP commitment that everyone has access to green or blue spaces within a 15-minute walk from home.
Government’s investment
To achieve our vision, we are investing over £1 billion this parliament in the largest tree planting and forestry sector support programme that England has ever seen.
There has already been significant progress. Tree planting rates over the last 2 years are the highest in England since 2004, with over 13,500 hectares planted. Over 12,800 hectares have been planted through government funding (Forestry Commission, 2026). This includes over 4,300 hectares through England’s Community Forests, providing trees and woodlands at the heart of communities. We are establishing 3 new national forests, each with a scale of between approximately 200 and 600 square miles, showcasing how tree planting, establishment and management can work for nature, access, development and growth.
Through this action plan, we are both driving our delivery further and focusing on what will make a difference.
Working in partnership
This action plan has been developed in consultation across government, relevant arm’s-length bodies and with key stakeholder groups. Many actions are jointly owned by government and industry, highlighting our shared commitment to achieving our vision.
Its delivery will be a collective effort. Industry, land managers, conservationists, charities, community groups, volunteers and campaigners all have a role to play in continuing to help shape the future of England’s trees and woodlands together.
The Trees Action Plan, along with government commitment and funding, provides a firm footing to turn intention into increased action. It will help to ensure England’s treescapes remain resilient and deliver lasting benefits for generations to come.
Chapter 1: Community engagement and stewardship
Communities have a central role to play in delivering the ambitions of this action plan. From helping to plant trees, to monitoring pests and diseases, to engaging in post-planting tree care, people are guardians of these natural assets. We will ensure that communities can access the many benefits that trees provide and feel empowered to take an active role in their long-term stewardship.
Healthy people
System connections
Activity in this section supports, or is complemented by, wider government commitments, including:
action to deliver trees, quality green spaces and other natural features in towns and cities under EIP commitment 78
action to make sure that everyone has access to green or blue spaces within a 15-minute walk from home under EIP commitment 86
action to improve inclusive public access to nature for physical and mental health and enhance visitor experience under EIP commitments 87, 88 and 91
Approximately 30% of adults in England visited woodlands and forests in the last year (Natural England, 2026). Regular visits to woodlands deliver around £175 million of mental health benefits per year (in 2026 prices) in England, reflecting reduced incidence of depression and anxiety and lower associated costs to the NHS and wider economy (Forest Research, 2021).
We want more people, from all backgrounds, including less-advantaged communities, to be able to access, enjoy and benefit from our woodlands and forests. We will continue to improve the amount and quality of public access through our grants and initiatives. This includes promoting active travel routes, encouraging landowners to install accessible infrastructure such as gates and waymarking, and improving information about woodland features, routes and points of interest.
Our public forest estate is everyone’s to access, steward and enjoy. Forestry England is our country’s largest land manager, managing over 1,500 woodlands and forests that span nearly 200,000 hectares (an area twice the size of Dartmoor National Park) on behalf of the public (Forestry Commission, 2026). These include some of our most iconic woodlands, which are open and accessible for all, and provide vital benefits for nature and timber supply. We are investing in expanding the public forest estate, securing a legacy of open access rights and improving its accessibility for people at risk of poor health or who are less able to participate. We will also create more opportunities for people to enjoy the estate, from children’s play, parkrun and mountain biking to camping and overnight cabin stays.
The National Estate for Nature group brings together some of England’s largest public, private, institutional and third sector landholders. We will work with these organisations, as well as other landowners, to explore opportunities to enhance access to nature on their estates, where it is safe and feasible to do so. This will help communities to enjoy and benefit from the natural landscapes, including woodlands, around them.

1.1. create 3,700 hectares of new publicly accessible woodland by March 2030 by expanding the public forest estate and dedicate this woodland in perpetuity as open access land. This will also support nature recovery and future timber supply, as set out in action 5.7. Responsible: Forestry England
1.2. support over 20 million active visits, including 3 million by children and young people, to the public forest estate by 2030 through phase 4 of the Active Forests programme to encourage more people to be active and benefit from time spent in forests. Responsible: Forestry England, Sport England
1.3. invest £4.5 million in new recreation and access infrastructure across the public forest estate (£4 million) and Woodland Trust estate (£0.5 million) in financial year 2026 to 2027 to improve access opportunities for everyone, particularly those at higher risk of poor health outcomes, including those who are physically inactive and have limited access. Responsible: Forestry England, Woodland Trust
1.4. by March 2027, develop a financial model to assess the impacts of climate-related events, such as storms, windthrow, flooding and wildfire, on forest-based recreation, leisure and tourism businesses, to provide improved evidence to inform adaptation and resilience planning across the sector. Responsible: Forestry Commission
1.5. support the government’s COVID-19 Commemorative programme by delivering several reflective spaces across the public forest estate to offer quiet places for reflection while promoting health and wellbeing through access to nature. These will be completed by April 2028. Responsible: Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, Forestry England
1.6. strengthen the remit of the National Estate for Nature (NEN) group to increase access to woodland while respecting operational, environmental and safety constraints. Responsible: Defra, NEN group members
Healthy places
System connections
Activity in this section supports, or is complemented by, wider government commitments, including:
action to deliver trees, quality green spaces and other natural features in towns and cities under EIP commitment 78
action to make sure that everyone has access to green or blue spaces within a 15-minute walk from home under EIP commitment 86
Trees are a central component of placemaking. They help strengthen people’s connection with the places they call home and are a shared asset that communities can value, enjoy and care for.
Inequities exist, however, regarding the provision of high-quality green spaces. 38% of adults in the least deprived areas live within a 5-minute walk of a green space, compared to just 26% in the most deprived (Natural England Local greenspaces in everyday life report, 2025). We need to focus our efforts where trees can make the greatest difference for communities and the environment.
We will plant up to 500,000 trees outside woodland as part of a new Community Trees Fund. This will empower strategic authorities, local authorities, mayors, community groups and environmental organisations to plant trees where they will have the most impact, such as in areas of higher deprivation and areas with less available green space. The fund will support projects that align with local priorities and plans, such as Local Nature Recovery Strategies.
The Tree Equity for Hartlepool project, a multi-partner project led by Trees for Cities and Hartlepool Borough Council, is an example of how working with communities can be a powerful tool in establishing trees and improving access to nature in areas of low tree cover. By bringing in different voices and expertise, and carrying out meaningful community engagement, this project has helped to give communities a sense of ownership of the trees planted in their areas.
Creating new partnerships also helps to focus efforts. The National Forest Company, with our support, will mentor and support 3 new national forests to establish and prosper. All 3 forests will expand local opportunities for public access to nature, nature engagement and outdoor learning, and our third new national forest, to be planted in the Midlands or the North of England, will particularly focus on addressing health inequalities. By seeking to ensure access to nature for everyone, these forests will deliver substantial health and wellbeing outcomes, including better mental health and increased physical activity.
We are also continuing to invest in England’s Community Forests and wider woodland creation partnerships, which will support landowners in providing high-quality, accessible woodlands.

We will make enabling support available to help create strong applications and successful projects, particularly in areas of higher deprivation and lower tree cover. Projects will be funded to reflect real world costs, including increased support for more deprived areas. Responsible: Defra, Forestry Commission
1.8. promote the National Lottery Awards for All England Environment Programme across community forests, woodland creation partnerships and the new national forests to support appropriate community-led projects which help more people enjoy and connect with trees and woodlands where they live. Responsible: National Lottery Community Fund, Community Forests, Woodland Creation Partnerships, new national forests

Chapter 2: Trees in a growing economy
The forestry, arboriculture and nursery sectors provide a crucial source of green jobs. They generate employment throughout the wood supply chain, from roles that add value to wood products to skilled careers in timber construction. Trees and forestry also represent significant and growing investment opportunities for private finance, particularly in relation to carbon. Our second new national forest, sited in the Oxford to Cambridge Growth Corridor, has been developed to complement the government’s regional growth agenda and will demonstrate how woodland creation can be integrated into large-scale economic growth.
We will support our forestry sector to meet expected demand for timber products, unlock new private investment and strengthen supply chains. This will help move the sector onto a more secure, sustainable long-term financial footing.
Growing the productive forestry sector

The UK is currently the third largest importer of timber and associated wood products globally, importing over 80% of our needs (Chapter 3 – Trade, Forestry Statistics 2026). We will always need to import timber, but we need a sustained domestic supply. It is important that we expand the area of productive, timber-producing woodland, on both public and private land, and develop new and efficient ways to harness sustainable homegrown timber. Maintaining strong biosecurity and managing pests and diseases, such as Ips typographus, will also be a key part of this – more detail is provided in Chapter 3 ‘Protecting our trees, woodlands and forests’.
Forestry England is England’s largest certified sustainable timber supplier. It is at the forefront of balancing sustainable timber production with biodiversity and public access. We will look to promote all opportunities available to build on this through our other grants and initiatives, such as the new national forests, community forests and the woodland carbon purchase fund. Anyone creating woodland should consider the role of fast-growing conifer species, not only for timber but also to support our pathway to net zero.
Newly established woodlands will need to be actively managed to support their long-term health, resilience and timber quality. Bringing existing woodlands into management can also increase their productive potential and the supply of homegrown timber. Further details on this are set out in Chapter 4 ‘Improving our trees, woodlands and forests’.
Well-developed national and local markets for wood products will support a thriving domestic timber sector. Utilising timber as a building material can drive economic growth while reducing the carbon impact of our built environment. Hardwood supply chains can be diversified beyond woodfuel and biomass by developing markets for innovative wood products, such as wood-based textiles or composite materials. It is also important that we invest in modernising the sector, bringing new technologies, techniques and processing capabilities to local businesses.
Actions:
2.1. evaluate previous Woodlands into Management Forestry Innovation Funds and use the findings to inform new innovation and capital productivity funding, including support for timber-processing and wood product businesses. This will increase sector capacity and strengthen domestic timber markets by developing local supply chains. Responsible: Defra, Forestry Commission
2.2. ensure that 80% of Forestry England’s woodland creation programme is managed to support sustainable hardwood and softwood timber production by March 2030. This will support our timber and wood-processing industries and help to strengthen domestic timber supply, while maintaining the environmental benefits of woodlands for nature. Responsible: Forestry England
2.3. increase the proportion of conifer species planted through Forestry England’s woodland creation programme from 36% to approximately 50% by March 2030 to support future domestic timber supply. This will include planting a greater diversity of forestry species to increase the resilience of these woodlands to climate change and pests and diseases. We will also look for opportunities to promote conifer planting in other publicly funded grants and initiatives. Responsible: Forestry England
Building a more resilient tree supply
System connections
Activity in this section supports, or is complemented by, wider government commitments, including:
action to support innovation, productivity and capital investment in tree and seed production under EIP Commitment 54
action to enhance the adaptive capacity and resilience of the treescape by increasing its extent, connectivity and diversity, improving its condition and conserving the genetic diversity within and between species under EIP Commitment 66
action to increase the biosecurity of plant supply chains under EIP Commitment 82
objectives set out under Outcome 3: A biosecure plant supply chain of the Plant biosecurity strategy for Great Britain (2023 to 2028)
A diverse tree resource is important for the long-term growth of the trees and forestry sector. Planting a wide range of species and provenances, while maintaining genetic diversity within and between populations, allows the treescape to adapt, evolve and thrive. It provides options for appropriate planting choices to manage different risks. We need to better understand, protect and sustainably utilise this diversity, including by identifying priority species for the future. This is covered in Chapter 4 ‘Improving our trees, woodlands and forests’.
Global trade provides access to planting stock, but it brings risks of invasive pests and pathogens. While we continue to collaborate internationally on this issue, including sharing knowledge, surveillance and international standards, we can reduce these risks by increasing our domestic production. This can benefit local economies and help support more resilient, lower-carbon supply chains.
England’s tree nursery sector is small. Around 18 nurseries grow 90% of saplings used for woodland creation across the UK (Forestry Commission, 2026). They face challenges from changing weather conditions, biosecurity threats and demand and economic uncertainties. We need to work with them to address these challenges, scale up delivery and maximise innovation. This must be supported by seed supplied via a diverse network of high-quality seed stands and well-developed seed orchards. Seed collecting, processing and storage are specialist skills. The sector needs sufficient research support, access to data and appropriate training and guidance.
Actions:
2.4. support initiatives to identify appropriate species for future tree planting in all settings, identify barriers to their production, supply and use, and develop our knowledge, capability and capacity to grow them. This will include investing in species assessments and trials, standardising approaches to assessing risks and opportunities, and developing guidance on silviculture and use of alternative species to support their uptake. Responsible: Defra
2.5. use Forestry England’s new Delamere seed processing facility to demonstrate ‘gold standard’ tree seed processing to the wider sector, including by hosting key stakeholders and students and providing basic training on Forest Reproductive Material, forest genetic resources, seed processing and seed testing. Explore the feasibility of options to use this facility to process seed on behalf of others, especially for priority conservation species or local provenance material which are not commercially available. Responsible: Forestry England
2.6. by 2030, invest £8.97 million in innovation grants to support domestic production, via tree seed and vegetative propagation, of all kinds of tree planting stock, to deliver a diversity of species and provenances to increase resilience to climate change, and help the sector to manage risks from extreme weather, biosecurity threats and economic uncertainty. Responsible: Defra, Forestry Commission
2.7. by 2028, Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, working with the Forestry Commission and others, will deliver new data, guidance and training on tree seed collection, processing, storage and use on the Institute of Chartered Foresters’ ‘Silvestor’ learning platform. Responsible: Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, Forestry Commission, Institute of Chartered Foresters
2.8. work with stakeholders to gather and share supply and demand data for planting material, strengthening links between growers and end-users to minimise waste and support a broader range of tree species in production. Responsible: Defra, Forestry Commission, tree nursery and forestry sectors
Improving access to private finance and building timber, carbon and nature markets
System connections
Activity in this section supports, or is complemented by, wider government commitments, including:
action to mobilise private investment and finance to restore and protect nature under EIP Commitments 1, 52 and 53
action to drive investment in tree planting, forest management and domestic supply chains to support the use of timber as a renewable, low-carbon building material, as set out in the Timber in Construction Roadmap 2025
action to enable private finance, as set out in the Farming Roadmap 2050: Growing England’s Future, the Land Use Framework for England and Growing Places: Mobilising private finance for local nature recovery
Private investment as well as public funding is needed to meet our ambitions. Under the right market conditions, tree planting can represent a unique private investment opportunity. Government has a priority to build this opportunity.
Carbon, nature and timber markets need greater certainty and clarity. Growing Places sets out the government’s work to support high-integrity nature markets, including work with the British Standards Institution to develop UK Nature Investment Standards. Flex 703 will establish clear principles for high-integrity UK nature-based carbon investment schemes and projects. The government’s recent response to the 2025 consultation on Voluntary Carbon and Nature Markets sets out further action, including carbon market guidance for UK organisations in spring 2027.
HM Revenue & Customs has published guidance clarifying the tax treatment of ecosystem service payments, including Biodiversity Net Gain units and carbon credits from woodland, helping to reduce uncertainty for land managers, developers and investors.
Ongoing improvements to the Woodland Carbon Code will strengthen transparency, reduce transaction costs and support market growth. We are de-risking private investment in woodland creation through Defra’s investment in the Big Nature Impact Fund, a private equity vehicle launched in 2026. The fund aims to demonstrate that commercial returns can be made from creating new woodland and other habitats when ecosystem services and the benefits of woodland are reflected in the market.
We are continuing to target our efforts to enable woodland developers and managers to generate reliable long-term income from these markets. We are exploring government buying woodland carbon units via a woodland carbon purchase fund. This fund, which is still in development, could provide developers with long-term demand and price certainty, in turn driving private investment into woodlands.
Actions:
2.9. regularly review guidance for the forestry sector on the rules for combining income from different government grant schemes and carbon and nature markets to ensure land managers are aware of how the relevant opportunities apply to woodland creation and management. Responsible: Defra, Forestry Commission, Department for Energy Security and Net Zero

Chapter 3: Protecting our trees, woodlands and forests
Standing trees, woodlands and forests are at the heart of England’s landscapes. They are, however, becoming increasingly exposed to threats from pests and diseases, drought, floods, wildfire, storms, and deer and grey squirrel damage. Protecting our existing treescape, as well as considered planting of new trees, is critical to mitigating these risks.
Treescape resilience
We take an evidence-based approach to defining and applying resilience as laid out in the Forest Research Resilience Implementation Framework and the UK Forestry Standard (UKFS), which describes a forest’s resilience as:
The ability of a social or ecological system to resist or absorb disturbances while retaining the same basic structure and ecosystem provision, by having the capacity for self-organisation and the capacity to adapt to stress and change while providing the same services.
This action plan is underpinned by a commitment to treescape resilience, as a function of its extent, condition, diversity, and connectivity. Providing a common thread across the plan, this commitment is reflected in actions that help to reduce risk, support recovery and enable adaptation. This will ensure our treescapes continue to provide benefits for thriving communities, nature recovery, climate resilience and sustainable economic growth long into the future.
In this chapter, we focus on how we can improve our treescape’s ability to respond to disturbances or shocks by reducing the risk and recovering effectively. Adapting to these pressures by moving towards a more resilient state is also crucial – this is covered in Chapter 4 ‘Improving our trees, woodlands and forests’.
Figure 1: The resilience framework. This chapter focuses on how we can reduce risk and facilitate recovery.
The resilience framework combines:
Reduce risk: Initial action to reduce the threat or absorb the impact of a risk with no substantial change or loss to the treescape.
Recover: Facilitating a suitable response when a threat does occur to allow the treescape to recover wherever possible.
Adapt: Driving long term changes to strengthen our natural resource and favour the survival of trees, woodlands and forests and their supporting landscapes to adapt to threats.
Managing threats
Managing threats is a critical component of protecting England’s treescapes and securing the productivity and benefits they provide.
Trade, globalisation and climate change all provide opportunities for new pest and disease introductions that threaten our biosecurity, while increasing the frequency and severity of environmental disturbances that threaten our trees, woodlands and forests. These pressures create a series of compounding effects that can lead to potentially catastrophic impacts on our trees, woodlands and forests.
Case study 1: Ips typographus
Ips typographus (eight-toothed spruce bark beetle) is a regulated pest capable of causing significant impacts to spruce woodlands and domestic timber production, having already caused widespread devastation of spruce in mainland Europe.
The response to Ips typographus demonstrates how we have worked with the sector, and will continue to do so, to support both immediate pest management and longer-term resilience. We are continuing to use extensive surveillance, movement controls and eradication measures to prevent the establishment of the pest in Great Britain. In the South East and East of England, we are continuing to work with woodland owners and managers to reduce the vulnerability of susceptible spruce stands and support their transition to woodlands containing a mix of tree species better suited to current and future environmental pressures.
Effective biosecurity and pest and disease management is fundamental to help safeguard woodland creation and regeneration, protect timber production, maintain ecosystem services, and reduce the environmental and economic costs of outbreaks. Overall, our approach aims to strengthen the long-term resilience and productive capacity of both regional treescapes and the wider forestry sector.

Plant biosecurity is a critical part of national biosecurity, protecting our environment, economy and food security from harmful pests and diseases. The core framework for our approach to managing biological threats is set out in our Plant Biosecurity Strategy, which delivers 4 outcomes:
a world class biosecurity regime
a society which values plants

Reducing the risk
We use horizon scanning, risk assessment, surveillance controls and inspections to help prevent harmful pests and diseases from entering and becoming established. We continually screen and prioritise new and emerging risks through the UK Plant Health Risk Register. Pest Risk Analyses help us prioritise the highest risks. This work operates under the leadership of the UK Chief Plant Health Officer and through a coordinated UK-wide approach.
Checks at the border and inland, supported by engagement with businesses and land managers, reduce the risk of harmful pests and diseases entering through trade or planting and enable rapid action when threats are identified.
Established programmes and interventions prevent the introduction of harmful organisms, detect outbreaks early, eradicate them where feasible and proportionate, contain or slow the spread of established threats and manage their long-term environmental, social and economic impacts.
We maintain robust programmes for regulated quarantine pests and diseases, including targeted interventions for threats such as Ips typographus and oak processionary moth. We will regularly review management policies and operational responses considering emerging evidence and provide tailored grant support where appropriate.
Surveillance, research and analysis are central to every stage of this approach. Surveillance at the border and inland provides the evidence needed to inform prevention, early detection and effective intervention. We will continue to strengthen surveillance and diagnostic programmes, target them at the most significant risks, and adopt new diagnostic, surveillance and management technologies where these can improve efficiency and effectiveness. This will help us identify changing threats and intervene earlier, directing resources to the areas of greatest need.
We will work with the sector to improve biosecurity standards among those growing and selling trees, including by promoting Plant Healthy certification and introducing the Biosecure Procurement Requirement across tree planting grants. These measures will reduce the risks from publicly funded planting and improve woodland establishment and resilience.
Response and recovery
When threats materialise, we will use established operational response arrangements led by the UK Chief Plant Health Officer to coordinate our responses. Our preparedness is continually improved through readiness reviews, contingency plans and incident response exercises focusing on the highest priority threats. Responses will be proportionate to the nature, scale and likely consequences of an outbreak. Depending on the circumstances, interventions may include movement restrictions, tracing, targeted surveillance and inspections, removal or treatment of affected material, and measures to contain or slow further spread.
We are continuing to provide targeted grant support through schemes such as the Tree Health Pilot and Countryside Stewardship Woodland Tree Health grants, helping landowners and managers respond to pests and diseases, restore affected woodlands and improve their resilience to future threats.
Together, these interventions protect unaffected trees, reduce environmental and economic losses, and create the conditions for affected treescapes and businesses to recover.
We are also working in partnership with stakeholders across the sector to implement the grey squirrel policy statement and deer impacts policy statement for England. Reducing the effects of over-browsing and bark stripping will protect woodland regeneration and establishment, improve ecological condition and help ensure that investment in woodland creation and management delivers lasting results.

System connections

Climate change is increasing the exposure and vulnerability of trees, woodlands and forests to environmental threats, including wildfire, severe wind damage, drought and flooding, and the interactions between them. These pressures can also interact with biological threats by weakening trees and creating conditions in which pests and diseases can establish or cause greater damage. Building resilience to these combined pressures is essential if England’s treescapes are to adapt to the changes and continue delivering benefits long into the future.
We are delivering our commitments in the National Adaptation Programme 3, published in July 2023, to support the forestry sector in tackling the threat of major environmental disturbances and adapting to climate change. We will contribute to the development of future adaptation actions in the National Adaptation Programme 4, due to be published in 2028.
Reducing the risk
We will improve how we prepare for and manage major environmental disturbances by supporting resilient woodland creation, design, appropriate species and silvicultural choices and long-term management. This includes encouraging greater structural, species and genetic diversity where appropriate, alongside management approaches that improve the ability of trees and woodlands to withstand and recover from disturbance.
This year saw significant wildfire activity with more than 1,300 wildfire incidents recorded in England by the end of August 2026 (Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, 2026). Wildfire presents one of the most significant climate risks facing the country. We are developing the first England Wildfire Action Plan, covering prevention, preparedness, response and recovery, to build a stronger and more resilient national response to wildfires.
We will continue to provide accredited risk management training, including vegetation fire and incident management courses, and undertake preparedness exercises for priority environmental threats. We will update key guidance including Building Wildfire Resilience into Forest Management Planning, Managing Forests for Wind, and Managing Trees for Drought Resilience to help woodland owners and managers understand risks, take preventive action and protect the long-term condition and productivity of their woodlands.
We will work with forest and woodland owners and managers, the public and other relevant stakeholders, including the Resilient Treescapes Advisory Group, to improve awareness of the threat of wildfire, windblow and drought, and provide advice on mitigation measures to increase future resilience. This includes, for example, maintaining firebreaks (gaps in vegetation to stop or slow wildfire spread).
Response and recovery
When disturbances occur, we will work with partners to understand their impacts and support effective response and recovery. We will apply the lessons learned to future woodland planning and management. New contingency plans will support an effective response in these circumstances.
The wildfire seasons of 2025 and 2026 have been a wake-up call for the need to change our approach to counteracting the risk of wildfire, particularly given the implications of climate projections. We are developing an integrated approach to wildfire management and planning, ensuring that we cover the preparedness and recovery phases, as well as the response phase. In time, this will create a far more resilient woodland and non-woodland landscape. We will do this by:
extending the responsibilities of the Forestry Commission to incorporate signing off wildfire management plans and grant-funded wildfire resilience capital items
extending the capabilities and capacity for Forestry England to prepare for, respond to, and recover from wildfire incidents on the public forest estate
enhancing the capacity of the Forestry Commission to advise on wildfire resilience and engage with Local Resilience Forums to put the forestry sector on the front foot to respond to wildfire and other natural disturbance events

Ancient woodlands and ancient and veteran trees are irreplaceable habitats. Natural England data from the Ancient Woodland Inventory and ongoing inventory update show that England’s ancient woodlands cover more than 412,000 hectares across more than 55,000 areas (Natural England, 2026). Developed over centuries, these habitats cannot be recreated within human timescales, but face increasing pressures from fragmentation, invasive non-native species, pests and diseases, deer browsing, air pollution and climate change.
These habitats deserve higher management standards and already have greater protection. We will improve the implementation of existing protections for ancient woodland and ancient and veteran trees, as well as other important trees within designated conservation areas or under Tree Preservation Orders. Chapter 4: Improving our trees, woodlands and forests
Not only do we need to protect our treescape, but we must also improve its condition and ability to adapt to current and emerging threats.
Diverse treescapes, in terms of species, structure and genetics, are one of nature’s best tools for building resilience. Different species, genetic characteristics and structures respond differently to threats. Diversity therefore reduces the risk of widespread impacts and increases the potential for recovery and adaptation when threats occur.
Improving ecological condition is equally important. Management that restores and improves key ecological processes, such as growth and regeneration, can strengthen the long-term adaptive capacity of our treescape. Given ecological time lags, action to improve existing trees, woodlands and forests now can deliver benefits for nature sooner than relying on woodland creation alone.
We will help land managers to improve the design of new and existing woodlands, bring more woodlands into management, improve survival rates of newly planted trees, restore ancient woodlands, and make better use of our forest genetic resources. Together, this will create healthier, more diverse treescapes.

Proactive management, in line with the UK Forestry Standard (UKFS), is crucial to effectively support resilience, biodiversity, climate change mitigation and adaptation and community access. It can also provide economic benefits from biomass and improved timber quality.
The percentage of woodlands managed in line with the UKFS has remained stable at around 56% in England over the past decade (Forestry Commission, 2026). Woodland owners face barriers such as reduced economies of scale in managing smaller woodlands, fragmented and underdeveloped supply chains, and skills and knowledge gaps. We are working to address these challenges and aim to drive-up woodland management to a new target range of 65% to 70% by 2040.
Support for woodland management is available, including through Countryside Stewardship Higher Tier (CSHT), Landscape Recovery and Tree Health schemes. Recent changes to CSHT have reduced entry thresholds and introduced a minimum payment, opening access to smaller woodland owners. Grants are available for woodland management and other plans, such as species management plans which help control invasive and non-native species. The development of a sector-led woods into management delivery plan will aid coordination in our shared delivery of increasing woodland management and consider additional mechanisms for increasing woodland management.
We are developing tools and guidance to help transition to more resilient forms of management. This includes the recently published handbook for forest managers on continuous cover forestry (CCF). CCF can increase resilience to threats such as storm damage compared to approaches such as clear-felling. We are also investing in research to find ways to increase resilience to individual and combined threats, such as using mixed species planting for drought adaptation.
Deer and grey squirrel impacts are increasing and are now one of the greatest challenges that woodlands face. Excessive browsing, foraging and trampling by deer put pressure on ground flora and inhibit natural colonisation, undermining woodlands’ ability to naturally adapt. Grey squirrels strip bark, causing tree damage and mortality. These impacts weaken the economic value of woodlands and their ability to deliver environmental benefits. We are continuing to monitor delivery of our grey squirrel policy statement and deer impacts policy statement for England using National Forest Inventory impact data, including woodland ecological condition surveys and squirrel reports.

Woodlands that are managed and in good ecological condition are more resilient and provide the most benefits. Structural diversity is a key part of this. It can be created through a varied mix of different tree and shrub species, different heights and ages of vegetation, fallen and standing deadwood through to areas of open canopy that allow light in for new trees, plants and wildlife to thrive. Over the last 50 years, however, the ecological condition of our woodlands has deteriorated (UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology, 2024). Only 9% of our native woodlands (NFI woodland ecological condition in England, 2020) and 40% of woodland habitat on Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) (Natural England, 2024) are now in favourable condition.
We need to see more woodlands of all types coming into management and restore plantations on ancient woodland sites (PAWS). We have introduced additional payments for PAWS restoration in the Countryside Stewardship Higher Tier scheme. Forestry England has committed to sensitively restore all 43,000 hectares of PAWS on the public forest estate and it will continue to make improvements to its protected sites. Site-specific constraints, including from pests such as Ips typographus, will continue to need careful consideration.
We continue to incentivise woodland creation that helps expand and connect ancient and native woodlands and support their management. The inclusion of periodic woodland condition assessments within woodland management plans will help track how condition is changing and ensure the best outcomes are achieved. Landscape Recovery is taking forward multiple large-scale projects integrating woodland improvement and expansion within wider connected landscapes.

Chapter 5: Expanding and connecting our trees, woodlands and forests
By making decisions strategically, in line with the Land Use Framework for England, tree planting, establishment and management can deliver multiple objectives. This will mean managing trade-offs and providing better information and data to the decision makers and land managers so that the right trees can be planted in the right places for the right reasons. The arboriculture and forestry sectors work at a local level, and tree planting, establishment and management organisations are well placed to contribute to devolution and local plans.
A key priority is to continue to balance the expansion of woodland with safeguarding our high‑value habitats, such as peatlands, species-rich grassland and open landscapes for breeding waders, as well as our most productive agricultural land. Planting sufficient fast-growing conifer species will be critical to ensure that we are able to capture the carbon required to meet net zero.
We will continue to improve our woodland creation offers, support more tree planting outside of woodlands, simplify grants and regulations and continue to expand the public forest estate. Three new national forests will create resilient landscapes of woodland, agroforestry and trees outside woodland, supporting nature and local communities while creating opportunities for future growth. Together, these actions will help create a larger, better-connected treescape, with trees planted where they can deliver lasting benefits.

Woodland creation for climate mitigation
System connections
Activity in this section supports, or is complemented by, wider government commitments, including:
meeting our statutory carbon budgets, as set out in the Carbon Budget and Growth Delivery Plan
Tree planting, establishment and management are crucial ways of mitigating climate change. Actions throughout this plan that increase the extent or resilience of trees, woodlands and forests will contribute to this goal. Different types of planting will all have a role to play. This includes fast-growing productive conifer species that sequester carbon early in their growth cycle and slower-growing native species that provide valuable benefits for nature while making an increasingly important contribution to carbon sequestration over the longer term.
Given the time it takes for trees to establish, grow and mature, action now is essential to maximise future carbon savings. Early planting, establishment and management are crucial. While some benefits are realised in the near term, the majority will be delivered during the second half of the century, with trees continuing to sequester and store carbon well beyond 2050. These carbon benefits, alongside the wealth of other benefits trees and woodlands provide, are why the legally binding target was set in 2023 to increase tree canopy and woodland cover to 16.5% of land area by 2050. Achieving this target will deliver around 18 MtCO₂ carbon savings by 2050, and around 84 MtCO₂ by 2100.

Trees outside woodlands account for around 30% of England’s tree cover (Forest Research, 2025). We need to understand more about how we can maximise their benefits across different settings, particularly in areas of low tree cover. Keeping the urban forest healthy requires significant planning, investment and monitoring, often led by local authorities. When they have become diseased or unsafe, public safety risks must be managed carefully. Similar considerations can be important for rural trees outside woodlands, particularly when trees are close to important infrastructure, such as airports or railway lines. It is important these trees are managed in a proportionate and proactive way to protect public safety and infrastructure, while safeguarding their important benefits.

Case study 2: Centre for Forest Protection collaborative research helping to create resilient and genetically diverse woodlands
As we expand and connect woodlands, we need to understand how different woodland creation approaches affect the genetic diversity of tree populations and their resilience to climate change, pests and diseases.
A Centre for Forest Protection (CFP) funded project, ‘Evaluating genetic bottlenecks in planted and naturally colonised young woodlands’, helped address limited evidence in this area. Delivered by Forest Research and Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, it examined genetic diversity and tree health at multiple UK woodland sites. Advanced DNA analysis identified patterns in oak (Quercus robur) and silver birch (Betula pendula) and compared planted woodlands with those established through natural colonisation.
Overall, genetic diversity in both species was broadly similar across planted and naturally colonised woodlands. This is encouraging for tree planting efforts as it suggests planted woodlands can achieve comparable levels of genetic diversity to those found in naturally established woodlands. However, trees within planted woodlands were more closely related, possibly because seeds came from fewer sources. This pattern of genetic similarity across woodlands could potentially reduce the ability of these landscapes to adapt to changing environmental conditions over time. Findings also showed that naturally colonised seedlings and young trees showed more recent inbreeding, probably because fragmented habitats restrict pollen and seed movement between populations, highlighting a potential disadvantage of relying on natural colonisation in some locations. Health assessments found that planted trees generally experienced less deer browsing damage than those at naturally colonised sites, but susceptibility to disease varied.
The research project provided valuable new evidence on the genetic effects of seed sourcing, tree production and woodland establishment approaches that will help policy makers and practitioners make more informed woodland creation decisions. Further CFP research will continue to improve our understanding of how to create resilient woodlands for the future.

Implementation and monitoring
Work on the Trees Action Plan will continue beyond publication as we implement its actions and support delivery of its objectives. The action-based nature of the plan, working across multiple strands, precludes a formal monitoring and evaluation approach. We will take a pragmatic approach reflecting the nature, scale and funding of individual actions as well as the supporting evidence base. As a minimum, we will ensure that the outputs and outcomes are robustly measured from the outset, working closely with stakeholders where relevant. The action plan will report on how each action has contributed towards Defra’s Outcomes Framework.
